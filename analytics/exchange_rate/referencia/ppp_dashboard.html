What this tab is

Turns qualitative FX commentary from asset-manager monthly letters into a numeric time series: for each month, how much of the narrative attributes BRL/USD moves to each of nine fixed causal regimes — fiscal (Brasil), monetary/carry, politics (Brasil), global USD, commodities/terms of trade/external accounts, global risk sentiment, China/EM growth, trade policy, and capital flows/positioning. This is a manual-extraction pilot, not an automated text-to-claim pipeline — each manager's claims are hand-extracted from source letters following a fixed taxonomy and rule set (see analytics/exchange_rate/models/fx_attribution_model.md).
Sign convention: direction scores the claim's implied effect on BRL, never on the claim's own subject — +1 strongly BRL-appreciation-supportive, −1 strongly BRL-depreciation-driving. A claim that "the dollar is strengthening globally" is scored negative (bad for BRL), not positive. Each manager's corpus is scoped independently (coverage windows and document counts differ) — cross-manager comparisons should account for that rather than reading a lower claim count as "this manager talks about FX less."

What this tab is

The model: Δfx(t) = α + φ·Δfx(t-1) + βfiscal·Δfiscal(t) + βcarry/vol·Δcarry_vol(t) + βdxy·Δdxy(t) + βdxy_em·Δdxy_em(t) + βsteep_real·Δcurve_steep_real(t) + βsp500·Δsp500(t) + βry_diff·Δreal_yield_diff(t) + βicbr·Δicbr_usd(t) + ε(t) — the exchange rate's own month-over-month log return, regressed on eight economic channels plus its own one-month lag, estimated by Ridge (L2 penalty) instead of OLS or a Bayesian fit. λ is chosen by walk-forward temporal cross-validation (fit on an expanding window, score one step ahead, keep the λ with the best average out-of-sample error), and coefficients are re-estimated on a rolling 72-month window so they can track regime change instead of describing one average relationship across calm and crisis periods.

Fiscal — Brazil's 5-year sovereign CDS (USD), a direct market price of default/fiscal risk. Positive coefficient: rising fiscal risk pushes the exchange rate the same direction as a depreciation.

Carry / FX vol — the BR-US policy rate differential divided by BRL's own trailing 6-month realized volatility, a risk-adjusted carry-attractiveness measure. Negative coefficient here — the same "wrong sign vs. simple UIP" finding this project's other carry-based models keep landing on.

Dollar index (DXY) — a global, not Brazil-specific, dollar-strength gauge (mostly G10 currencies). Positive coefficient: broad dollar strength depreciates BRL alongside every other EM currency, independent of anything specific to Brazil.

EM dollar index (Broad-EM) — the Fed's own trade-weighted basket of ~19 emerging-market currencies (BRL, CNY, MXN, KRW, etc.), tested alongside DXY to isolate EM-specific co-movement from the broader, mostly-G10 dollar gauge. Comes in strong and positive — in fact larger and more stable than DXY itself once both are in the regression, suggesting most of what DXY was proxying for BRL is really EM-specific co-movement.

Curve steepening (10Y-2Y, real) — Brazil's inflation-linked curve's 10-year rate minus its 2-year rate, an alternate, market-based fiscal-risk proxy alongside CDS, isolating the real term premium net of inflation-expectations effects a nominal curve would mix in. Motivation: 5-year USD CDS may understate purely domestic fiscal-sustainability risk, since Brazil's large USD reserve buffer keeps external-default-priced CDS relatively muted even when domestic debt dynamics worsen. Same sign as fiscal risk itself. The nominal (non-inflation-linked) version of this same steepening was tested alongside it and dropped — it added no out-of-sample signal once the real version and the other seven channels were already in the regression (dropping it improved walk-forward OOS error by 1.4%, with no material change to any other channel's coefficient).

S&P 500 — the US equity index's own monthly log return, testing a "competing for capital" hypothesis: a stronger US equity market pulls capital toward US assets, pressuring the exchange rate up independently of anything Brazil-specific. Positive and one of the most stable coefficients in the model (never crosses zero across any 6-year rolling window). Tested alongside VIX and the US 10-year real yield in isolation — neither improved out-of-sample prediction, so read this narrowly as a level-of-the-index / capital-competition effect, not a general risk-appetite (risk-on/risk-off) signal.

Real yield differential (10Y, BR-US) — Brazil's real (inflation-linked) 10-year yield minus the US 10-year TIPS real yield, a risk-premium measure. Positive coefficient — read carefully: this means a WIDER BR-US real-rate gap moves the exchange rate the same direction as a depreciation, the opposite of a naive carry-attractiveness story (which would expect a higher real yield to draw capital in and strengthen BRL). Consistent instead with reading Brazil's own real yield as a risk premium, moving with fiscal risk and curve steepening rather than against them. Different from testing the US real yield alone (tried earlier, didn't improve prediction) — it's specifically the BR-US differential that carries signal.

Commodity index (IC-Br, USD) — the USD-denominated version of the BCB's IC-Br commodity price index (distinct from the BRL-denominated version used elsewhere in this project's inflation work, which embeds BRL's own move by construction and would be circular here). The single largest new coefficient this round: reliably negative across nearly every rolling window. Sign makes clean sense for a major commodity exporter — rising global commodity prices strengthen BRL.

AR(1) — Δfx(t-1) itself, kept in raw units (not z-scored). Small and negative: a month that moves more than usual tends to see a modest partial reversal the next month. Replaced an earlier version of this tab that instead gave every channel its own 6-month lag structure (48 parameters) — a walk-forward test showed that version overfits (out-of-sample error got worse with every added lag), while this single shared term, at the cost of one extra parameter, genuinely improves out-of-sample prediction.

Baseline, on the decomposition chart below, combines three things that aren't one of the eight economic channels: the anchor rate (where the bridge starts), α (the constant drift), and the AR(1) term (the exchange rate's own momentum). None of the three is a fundamental driver on its own, so they're shown as one bucket rather than three separate bars.

PPP itself was tested here too — as a freely-estimated channel (the BR-US relative inflation differential), not as an assumed equilibrium the exchange rate is measured against. Its coefficient came out unstable across rolling windows (whole-sample +0.34, rolling mean +0.04, swinging from −0.86 to +1.48) — dropping it cost almost nothing in-sample and genuinely improved out-of-sample prediction, so it's excluded and α absorbs its average drift instead, same as every channel this tab has dropped for the same reason.

12-Month Forecast (Stress Test)
Walks the last 72-month window's fitted model forward, feeding its own simulated AR(1) term at every step. Each channel below gets 12 editable boxes — one per forecast month — pre-filled with its last observed level; enter the level you expect that month, and the model converts your entered path into month-over-month deltas (chained box-to-box, anchored on the last real observation) and z-scores them the same way every other month in the fit is standardized. Shaded band is the ±1 std-error interval from the same model's own historical 72mo-window, 12-month-ahead walk-forward forecast error, widening step by step.
