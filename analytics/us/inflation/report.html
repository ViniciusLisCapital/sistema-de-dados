Release calendar — where the dates and times come fromRead from the agencies’ own schedules, and why each needed a different one

The strip at the top of the page is not a rule of thumb about which day of the month the data usually lands. Every date and time there is read from the agency's own published calendar, stored in domain/release_calendar/calendar_2026.yaml and refreshed by domain.release_calendar.update_us_calendar.

SeriesPrimary sourceCross-check
CPI BLS, Schedule of Releases for the Consumer Price Index (bls.gov/schedule/news_release/cpi.htm) the BLS bls.ics feed and FRED release 10 — all three agreed exactly when last refreshed
PCE BEA, release-calendar .ics subscription feed (bea.gov/news/schedule) BEA's own HTML schedule page and FRED release 54 — all three agreed exactly

The two agencies had to be handled differently, and the reason is worth stating because it decides which file to fix when a date looks wrong. The BLS ICS feed carries no reference month — its event titles are just "Consumer Price Index" — so the per-release HTML page, the only BLS source with a Reference Month column, is the primary one. The BEA feed carries the period in the event title itself ("Personal Income and Outlays, August 2026"), so there the feed is enough. Neither reference month is derived from a "release month minus one" rule: BEA's own 2026 calendar breaks it, running Jan 22, Feb 20, Mar 13, Apr 9 and Apr 30 while it caught up on a delayed schedule.

Times are stored in the source's time zone and converted per date, never frozen as one converted value. Both agencies publish at 08:30 in New York; the United States keeps daylight saving and Brazil dropped it in 2019, so that same 08:30 is 09:30 in São Paulo from March to early November and 10:30 for the rest of the year. A single hard-coded local time would be wrong for half the year, silently.

Two limits to know. The agencies publish one year at a time — as of this build neither had posted a 2027 calendar — so the next-release line runs out at the end of the year until they do. And the countdown ("in 12 days") is computed against the clock of whoever opens the file, not baked in at generation: if it reads past due, this file is stale and the number above it is behind the published data.

How the CPI's two trees are built37 rows the BLS declares, 355 assembled ones, and the arithmetic that proves the parentage

They are two views of one table, picked with the Tree pills, the same arrangement the Brazilian inflation report uses for its two trees (the IBGE expenditure structure and the central bank's analytical one). Neither derives from the other: they are independent cuts of the same index, and the heading, the note and the level pills all move with the selector. Ticked rows and open branches are remembered per tree — both trees call All items SA0, but a selection three levels down means something different in each.

Release tree — Table 1 of the news release, 37 rows over 5 levels, the structure the market reads on release day. Its hierarchy is declared by the BLS in the release page's own markup (<p class="subN"> for depth, hierarchical row ids for parentage), not inferred from indentation. Six of its nodes are special aggregates that do not exist in the expenditure tree at all.

Table 1 stops at Gasoline (all types) and Electricity. Wherever it stops, the expenditure tree continues underneath it in that same view, so the gasoline grades, roasted vs. instant coffee and new cars vs. new trucks are one click down rather than a tree away. The drill-down only ever goes below a row the release leaves as a leaf, never beside a published child — which is what keeps the tab honest: Motor fuel is still short of its children, because the release omits Other motor fuels, a sibling of Gasoline (all types) rather than a deeper level.

Expenditure tree — the full statistical structure, 355 items over 10 levels, from All items down to Bacon and related products. No BLS source publishes it as parent/child pairs: it is assembled from cu.item plus the published indentation of the annual relative-importance table, then proved by weight arithmetic — each of the 90 parents the weight table covers equals the sum of its children, max error 0.001. The loaded tree has more parents than that (130), because the deepest layer below has no weights to check.

Two nodes have to be re-parented for that proof to hold, and the weights themselves settle where they belong: Food and beverages 14.539 = Food 13.698 + Alcoholic beverages 0.840, and Information and information processing 3.181 = 1.466 + IT hardware 1.714. The check re-runs on every load and refuses to write if it fails, so a change in BLS's published indentation surfaces as an error instead of a quietly wrong tree.

Below the relative-importance table. The spreadsheet stops at 294 published lines; cu.item goes deeper, and 83 of those deeper items have real index series — gasoline by grade, coffee by preparation, new cars vs. new trucks, smartphones, college textbooks, inpatient vs. outpatient hospital. They are attached by the same parent rule used for the rest (the last preceding line one level up), validated before being trusted: on the 266 items the spreadsheet already positions it reproduces the published parent 254 times, and all 12 disagreements are items whose true parent is a special aggregate — none at the depth the graft touches. So the graft only ever adds leaves, and the additivity proof above runs on the spreadsheet layer untouched.

Five further items, 0.861 index points with both a weight and a series published, were being dropped by a one-comma difference between the two sources' labels; they are matched by position now, not by name.

Three cu.item entries are excluded on purpose — Information technology commodities, Video and audio products, Video and audio services. They arrive at display level 1, which would make them children of All items, and they appear in neither section of the spreadsheet: cross-cutting aggregates with no place in this tree. The loader lists them on every run, so a genuinely new BLS item shows up in the log instead of disappearing.

Two history windows: the release rows carry full history from 1913, the expenditure items start in 1990. Most detail items only begin in the 1990s, so full history there would be mostly empty. And nothing is written into the rows themselves — hover a row and it says whether the item is a special aggregate, a drill-down row, unweighted, discontinued, or a parent its children do not sum to.

What the numbers are, and what they are notEverything derives from the level · contribution is an approximation, and by how much

Every percentage on this page is computed from the level, in the browser. The level is the published quantity; Y/Y, M/M, 3-month annualised and contribution all derive from it without loss, and storing both invites the two to disagree. It also keeps the payload roughly one eighth the size.

Never compare levels across items. The base period varies by item — most are 1982-84=100, many start at December of some specific year. Two items at the same level are not "at the same price"; only the variations are comparable.

Contribution is an approximation, not an identity. It is weight × variation, with the weight joined at read time: for a month in year Y, the December snapshot of Y−1, falling back to the nearest earlier one. That is the BLS's own construction — 2025.xlsx has reference period December 2025 and is what prices 2026 — and it is the better of the available choices: using each year's own snapshot rather than one fixed vector cuts the headline reconciliation error about 30% (0.0183 → 0.0124 p.p. mean absolute, measured against this database). About 0.015 p.p. is irreducible, because relative importance is a December snapshot of a quantity the BLS price-updates continuously. That is not a seasonal-adjustment artefact — repeating the test on NSA series makes it slightly worse, which rules that out.

That error is a rounding detail on a year and a fifth of the value on a month. Contribution to Y/Y and contribution to M/M are the same arithmetic at two different lags, but a month's contribution is an order of magnitude smaller than a year's: ~0.012 p.p. is nothing against a 3.4% annual change and is a fifth of a 0.1% monthly one. So the monthly decomposition on the two CPI tabs reads as an ordering of who moved the index, not as an exact split. On the PCE tab it is exact — 0.0027 p.p. at worst over the last sixty months — because the BEA publishes the weight every month and there is no December snapshot being carried forward. Both figures are asserted in the test suite. Contribution to M/M prints to three decimals for the same reason: at two, most rows below level 2 would read "+0.00".

Both contribution metrics draw as a decomposition, not as lines — stacked bars for the ticked rows and a black line for the headline they decompose. A ticked row that is the headline becomes that line rather than a bar stacked on its own components. The bars reach the line only when the ticked rows partition the index, which is what the warning below is about.

Only levels 0–2 partition the index. Those three levels each sum to 100.000, so a decomposition there is complete. Below that the release shows only the largest children of each parent: 7 of its 13 parents leave weight with no row, 25.583 index points in total — 11.721 inside core services and 7.282 inside core goods. Contributions summed at level ≥ 3 therefore do not add to the parent. The expenditure tree does add up, and is the one to use when the detail matters.
The weight column is reconstructed, not loadedThe release’s own relative importance, recovered from a December snapshot

The Weight column of the contributions table is the release's own relative importance, dated one month behind the reference month — and that figure is in no file this database loads — the workbook publishes December only. It is recovered the way the BLS updates a relative importance: the December weight times the item's own NSA index ratio since that December, divided by the headline's ratio over the same span. Formally RI(t) = w(Dec) × [I(t)/I(Dec)] ÷ [I₀(t)/I₀(Dec)], with I₀ the All-items NSA index. This is why no monthly weight table is loaded: the release's own column drifts a long way from the annual one — Energy 7.432 in the July 2026 release against 6.383 in the workbook — and is reproducible from what is already here.

Checked rather than asserted, two ways. Against the printed table: all 37 relative importances of the July 2026 release come back within 0.001, the rounding of the printed figure. Structurally: the eight level-1 groups still sum to 100.000 after each is updated on its own. Both are in the test suite, so a wrong weight snapshot or a shifted index base fails the build. The levels behind them reconcile with the release too, and the All-items row is checked across the whole published column set: 323.048 / 333.952 / 333.918, +3.4% and 0.0% unadjusted, +0.5 / −0.4 / +0.1 seasonally adjusted.

The nine-column Table 1 view that used to print those figures on the face of the tab was removed in August 2026 — it duplicated a table the BLS itself publishes on release day. The arithmetic behind it did not go with it: the same levelAt / pctBetween path now fills the contributions table's Change column, and the reconciliation above is asserted against it directly, so the check survives the view that motivated it.

In the expenditure tree the column fills for of the 355 rows — blank for the ones below the relative-importance table, which have no weight to update, and for the discontinued ones, whose index stops before the reference month.

The two readings below the tableWhat the ranking and the drill-down do, and what the coverage line is for

Largest contributions ranks whichever rows the Rank pills pick — the tree's leaves, or every row at one depth — by their contribution over a 1, 3, 6 or 12-month window. Contribution over the window is the row's weight times its own change across the window, the same arithmetic as the chart's contribution metrics at a different lag, so it uses the same two weight conventions and carries the same measured error. Change is the item's own index over the same two months; a row whose index is missing at either end shows nothing rather than a partial figure, which is what keeps discontinued items out of the ranking instead of ranking them on a short window.

Summing the monthly contributions instead would have left the column empty, and the reason is worth knowing before building anything month-by-month on this database: October 2025 was never published. It is the only gap in the whole CPI table — 613 of 634 series blank for that one month, 21 with a value — a consequence of the US government shutdown. One missing index kills two monthly changes, its own and the following month's, so any window crossing it has no complete monthly path. Taking the ratio of the two endpoints steps over the hole without inventing anything, because it only ever needs the levels at the ends.

It prints its own coverage, and that line is the point. The Brazilian report's equivalent ranks the 614 subitems, which partition the IPCA exactly, so it can stay silent about what it covers. Nothing here partitions: not the leaves of either tree, and not any release-tree level below 2 (see the warning above). So the line under the table says how much weight the ranked rows carry out of 100 and what their contributions sum to against the headline over the same window. A gap there is coverage, not error — reading the ranking as a complete decomposition is the mistake the line exists to prevent.

12-month change — component drill-down is the same idea as the Brazilian report's Variação 12M — Drilldown de Componentes, with one simplification the source allows. There, a level's aggregate has to be reconstructed — weighted average of the subitems' monthly changes, chained into an index — because the IBGE publishes the data only at subitem level. Here the BLS publishes an index for every node of the tree, so the line plotted is the published index's own Y/Y: nothing is reconstructed and there is no chaining error to explain. What it adds over ticking rows in the table above is reach — every component of one level at once, without opening parent by parent. It follows the Tree and Adjustment pills, and is fixed at Y/Y, which is what its title says.

The PCE tree — a better-behaved sourceMonthly weights, four levels that partition exactly, and nineteen lines that subtract

The PCE price index is what the Fed targets, and it is not a rounding difference away from the CPI: different weights (the national accounts, not a household survey), different scope (it counts what employers and government pay on households' behalf, most visibly in health care) and a different formula (chained Fisher, not a fixed-basket Laspeyres). The two tabs answer different questions and are not meant to reconcile.

Both PCE tables come out of one public workbook — the BEA's Section 2 underlying detail release, 12 MB, no API key and no quota. Table 2.4.4U is the price index (2017=100), 2.4.5U the matching spending in millions of dollars. The two carry the same 402 lines in the same order, so price and spending join by line number; nothing is matched by name, which is where every hard case in the CPI tree came from.

The hierarchy is the published indentation, two spaces per level, and it is proved by the spending arithmetic: all 122 checkable parents equal the sum of their children, across all 810 months. The tolerance is not a chosen epsilon — the BEA rounds every line to whole millions, so k children against a rounded parent can differ by up to (k+1)/2 from rounding alone, and the test is that the residual fit inside that. It does, using at most 80% of the allowance; the largest discrepancy anywhere in the table is $3 million against a $20 trillion total. The load refuses to write if that stops holding.

Nineteen lines subtract. Four say Less: in the label; the other fifteen are what sits underneath one and inherits the sign without saying so. Their weights ship already negative, so a contribution whose sign is opposite to the price change is the accounting rather than an error — hover the row and it says which applies. A negative weight is not always one of those nineteen, though: Employee reimbursement is a line whose own spending is negative (−$1.7 bn, it nets out of used-car purchases), with an ordinary positive sign.

Levels 1–4 each partition the index exactly, at 100.00% of spending, and the tree's leaves sum to 100.0000%. That is two levels deeper than the CPI release tree reaches, and it is why contribution can be trusted further down here. Below level 4 coverage falls off because the tree is not of uniform depth — level 5 reaches 95.5% of spending, level 6 only 65.1% — so contributions summed at a fixed deep level are incomplete for that reason, not because something failed to load.

The weight is monthly, and dated at the base of the change. The BEA publishes each line's spending every month, so there is no December snapshot to carry forward and nothing to reconstruct: the weight is that month's share of total PCE. Which month is a measured choice — a Y/Y change is weighted by the share 12 months earlier, which rebuilds the published headline to 0.0114 p.p. mean absolute error against 0.0202 using the current month's share. Monthly contributions land at 0.0009 p.p., against 0.0124 p.p. for the CPI.

Three things are specific to this tab. SA only — the BEA publishes no unadjusted monthly counterpart, which is why the NSA pill is greyed rather than absent. Two lines have spending but no price index (Net expenditures abroad by U.S. residents, Net foreign travel): they are nets of two flows, the BEA marks them ZZZZZZ, and their price columns are blank. And the 34 addenda at the bottom — Control group, PCE food and energy, PCE excluding food and energy, the market-based family — are not part of the tree: they overlap each other and must never be summed together. They sit in a flat block because the BEA's own indentation there is inconsistent (Market-based PCE is printed deeper than the lines it heads), so giving them parentage would mean inventing it. Latest month here is , normally a few weeks behind the CPI's.

LimitsWhere the data stops, and what is deliberately not loaded
  • Weights start in 2020, so contribution does too. The BLS publishes annual December weights back to 1947, in two older formats (decade zips of fixed-width text, plus one historical workbook) that have no parser yet. A parsing gap, not a data gap.
  • CPI-U only. CPI-W and C-CPI-U are supported by the schema and the loader, not loaded.
  • SA coverage is thinner than NSA by construction: 291 of the 361 items have a seasonally adjusted series. The rest simply show no line when SA is selected.
  • 19 series are discontinued — 15 of the deepest ones (Photo Processing, Intercity train fare, New motorcycles) plus four the BLS dropped in 2024 (Legal services, Household operations, Domestic services, Repair of household items). Their history is kept, so a line that stops mid-chart is a fact about the series, not a gap in the load. Flagging needs a three-month gap, so an item merely one month behind on release day is not mislabelled.
  • Prices only, and the spending they are weighted by. The same workbook carries the real counterparts at the identical 402-line granularity — 2.4.3U (quantity indexes) and 2.4.6U (chained dollars) — plus a coarse 46-line trio cut by major type of product and major function. None are loaded, so nothing here decomposes real consumption. The detailed by-function tree is a different table (2.5.x) and is not in this file at all.
  • Two PCE lines stop in 2001-12 — Tenant-occupied stationary homes and Tenant landlord durables, both level-7 splits the BEA no longer publishes. Same convention as the CPI: history kept, last month on hover.
  • Both deep trees ship from 1990 in the payload, not from where the database starts (1913 for the CPI, 1959 for the PCE). Most detail series begin in the 1990s anyway, so full history would be mostly nulls. The release-tree tab carries its whole history because it is only 37 rows.
