Release tree — food / energy / core

Table 1 of the CPI news release: 37 rows over 5 levels. The structure the Fed and the market read on release day. The hierarchy is declared by the BLS in the release page's own markup (<p class="subN"> for depth, hierarchical row ids for parentage), not inferred from indentation. Six nodes are special aggregates that do not exist in the expenditure tree at all — marked agg.

The 37 published rows, plus drill-down. Table 1 itself stops at Gasoline (all types) and Electricity. Wherever it stops, the expenditure tree continues underneath — further rows, flagged detail — so the three gasoline grades, roasted vs. instant coffee and new cars vs. new trucks are one click down instead of one tab over. The published rows and their badges are untouched: Motor fuel stays partial because the release omits Other motor fuels, which is a sibling of Gasoline (all types), not a deeper level.

Only levels 0–2 partition the index. Those three levels each sum to 100.000, so a decomposition there is complete. Below that the release shows only the largest children of each parent: 7 of the 13 parents leave weight with no row, 25.583 index points in total — 11.721 inside core services and 7.282 inside core goods. Rows with a partial badge do not equal the sum of their visible children, and the badge states how much is missing.

Expenditure tree — the full statistical structure

The CPI's complete expenditure hierarchy: items over levels, from All items down to Bacon and related products. No BLS source publishes this as parent/child pairs — it is assembled from cu.item plus the published indentation of the annual relative-importance table, then proved by weight arithmetic: all 90 parents equal the sum of their children (max error 0.001). Two nodes had to be re-parented for that to hold; see the Appendix.

Why the index level is stored and the variations are not

Every percentage on this page is computed from the level, in the browser. The level is the published quantity; Y/Y, M/M, 3-month annualised and contribution are all derivable from it without loss, and storing both invites the two to disagree. It also keeps the payload roughly one eighth the size.

Never compare levels across items. The base period varies by item — most are 1982-84=100, but many start at December of some specific year. Two items at the same level are not "at the same price"; only the variations are comparable.

Contribution is an approximation, not an identity

Contribution is weight × variation, with the weight joined in at read time: for a month in year Y, the December snapshot of year Y−1, falling back to the nearest earlier one. That is the BLS's own construction — the file named 2025.xlsx has reference period December 2025 and is what prices 2026 — and it is the better of the available choices: using each year's own snapshot instead of a single fixed vector cuts the headline reconciliation error about 30% (0.0183 → 0.0124 p.p. mean absolute, measured against this database).

Two reasons it still will not close exactly, both worth knowing before treating a decomposition as an identity:

  • ~0.015 p.p. is irreducible. Relative importance is a December snapshot of a quantity the BLS price-updates continuously, so no single vector reproduces every month. This is not a seasonal-adjustment artefact — repeating the test on NSA series makes it slightly worse, which rules that out.
  • The release tree is not exhaustive below level 2. Contributions summed at level ≥ 3 do not add to the parent, because the release omits the smaller children. The expenditure tree does add up (all 90 parents verified), and is the one to use when the detail matters.

Where the deepest items come from, and why they have no weight

The relative-importance table stops at 294 published lines. cu.item goes deeper, and those deeper items have real published index series — 83 of them. They are attached to the tree by the same parent rule used for the rest (the last preceding line one level up), applied to cu.item's own publication order.

That rule was validated before being trusted: on the 266 items the spreadsheet already positions, it reproduces the published parent in 254, and all 12 disagreements are items whose true parent is a special aggregate — none of them at the depth the graft touches. So the graft only ever adds leaves; it never moves an item the spreadsheet has placed, and the weight-additivity proof runs on the spreadsheet alone, untouched.

Five more items were being lost to a plain label mismatch: the spreadsheet writes "Men's underwear, nightwear, swimwear, and accessories" and cu.item writes the same thing without the second comma, so the name join failed and the item vanished — with both a weight and a series published. Each of the five was confirmed by position, not by resemblance: each has exactly one cu.item candidate sitting between its coded neighbours in publication order, and for two of them the API's own series_title carries the spreadsheet's spelling. Together they are 0.861 index points that used to be invisible here.

The two re-parented nodes

In the expenditure tree, the published indentation puts Alcoholic beverages and Information technology, hardware and services at a level where the weights do not add up. The weights themselves settle it: Food and beverages 14.539 = Food 13.698 + Alcoholic beverages 0.840, and Information and information processing 3.181 = 1.466 + IT hardware 1.714. With those two moved, all 90 parents close to within 0.001. The check re-runs on every load and refuses to write if it fails, so a change in BLS's published indentation surfaces as an error rather than as a quietly wrong tree.

Known gaps

  • Weights start in 2020 in this database, so contribution does too. The BLS does publish annual December weights back to 1947, in two older formats (decade zips of fixed-width text, plus one historical workbook) that have no parser yet. This is a parsing gap, not a data gap.
  • A monthly weight exists for the 37 release rows — the release's own weight column is one month behind the reference month, and differs materially from the December snapshot (Energy was 7.432 in the July 2026 release against 6.383 in the December 2025 workbook). Not loaded; recoverable from the release archive, which needs the release-date calendar.
  • CPI-U only. CPI-W and C-CPI-U are supported by the schema and the loader but not loaded.
  • SA coverage is thinner than NSA by construction: 291 items have a seasonally adjusted series against 361 with NSA. Items missing SA simply show no line when SA is selected.
  • 19 items have no observation for more than three months — 15 of the deepest ones (Photo Processing, Intercity train fare, New motorcycles) plus four the BLS dropped in 2024 (Legal services, Household operations, Domestic services, Repair of household items). They are kept, with history, and badged last 2024-09 so a line that stops mid-chart reads as a fact about the series rather than a gap in the load. The badge needs a three-month gap, so an item merely one month behind on release day is not mislabelled.
  • Three cu.item entries are deliberately excluded from the expenditure tree: Information technology commodities, Video and audio products and Video and audio services. They arrive at display level 1, which would make them children of All items, and they appear in neither section of the relative-importance table — cross-cutting aggregates with no place in this tree. The loader lists them on every run, so a genuinely new BLS item shows up in the log instead of disappearing.
