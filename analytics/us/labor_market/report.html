Appendix — source, method and what to watch out for

Everything below is either quoted from the release's own Technical Note or measured against this database. Where a number is measured, it says so.
The controls on the two payroll tableswhat each one changes, and what it leaves alone

The controls above the two payroll tables are independent, and they are applied in the order they are listed. Two of them change what the number is, one changes what it is measured against, one changes only how noisy it looks, and the last one changes nothing about the number at all. Which controls appear depends on the data: a choice the numbers do not offer is not shown as an empty control, it is simply absent.

ControlWhat it changes
AdjustmentWhether the normal seasonal rhythm of hiring is taken out. The unadjusted view is the one where an industry's parts add up to the industry: the BLS adjusts each series on its own, so in the adjusted view the parts add only approximately.
DenominatorWhether you see the count of jobs or that count as a share of the whole payroll. Every industry is divided by the same total, so shares can be read against each other and stacked.
ComparisonThe reference point. Value is the level itself; Change is how many jobs were added since last month or since the same month a year ago; % is that same movement as a percentage. Change and % are not the same reading — a month can be "+127" thousand jobs and "+0.09" percent, and only the axis label tells you which one is on screen.
SmoothingAverages the line over three, six or twelve months to take out month-to-month noise. It never changes the unit or the scale: the three-month average of the monthly change is still a monthly change in jobs, which is the figure most commentary quotes.
ChartLines or stacked bars. Stacking is only offered where the rows genuinely add up to their total.

On the hours and earnings table a sixth control appears for average hourly and average weekly earnings: Basis, which switches between earnings in current dollars and the same earnings in constant 1982-84 dollars, as the BLS publishes them. It is the first thing applied, before any comparison, and it is not shown for the measures where the survey has no constant-dollar counterpart.

Some options are greyed out depending on the others, and when that happens the reason is printed under the controls rather than hidden in a tooltip. On hours and earnings, what greys out changes with the measure you pick: an average per worker has no total to be a share of, and industries cannot be stacked into one, while the aggregate hours and payrolls measures can be both. Two controls are missing on both tables on purpose: there is nothing to pick for window, because these are headcounts and weekly rates — adding twelve months of a weekly payroll would not give a year of payroll — and the payroll employment table has a single measure, so it shows no measure control either.

The controls on the four household tablesthe same five controls, and why they grey out more often here

The household tables carry the same five controls as the payroll ones, in the same order, and they mean the same things. What is different is that a row of one of these tables can be a count of people, a rate, or a length of time — the labour-force table holds four counts in thousands and three rates in percent, and the duration cut holds four counts and two averages in weeks. A chart has one vertical axis, so what you tick decides which controls still make sense, and the ones that stop making sense are greyed with the reason printed underneath.

What you have tickedWhat that leaves available
Only counts of peopleEverything. Percent changes read as percent changes, and the rows can be stacked where they are parts of a total.
Only ratesChange is offered and comes out in percentage points; the % readings are greyed. A percent change of a percentage misleads: the unemployment rate went from 4.3 to 4.1 over the year to August 2026, which is −0.2 points and would read as "−4.7%".
Rates and counts togetherThe % readings are greyed for the same reason, and Change stays — a difference is honest in both units at once, so the axis says the units are mixed and the table shows each row's own.
An average duration in weeksPercent changes are offered: weeks are not a percentage, so "average duration rose 7.4% over the year" is a real reading. But a duration does not stack with a count of people, so it always draws as a line.

Shares need a total that exists. In the labour-force table the total is the civilian population, and that is the one line this survey never adjusts for seasonality — so % of total is only available in the unadjusted view, and the control says so instead of drawing an empty chart. Read that view and you get the published rates back: the labour force as a share of the population is the participation rate, and employment as a share of the population is the employment-population ratio, both to within a tenth of a point. In the composition table the total is the level of unemployment, which lives in the first table rather than in the one you are reading — which is why the axis names it. The rates tables and the U-1 to U-6 table have no total at all: each row already divides by its own base.

Two of the three cuts of unemployment do stack. In the unadjusted data the four reasons and the four duration buckets each close on the unemployment level to within a thousand people; in the adjusted data they land within about 3%, because each series is adjusted on its own. The part-time cut does not stack: the two indented lines are two of the four reasons the survey publishes for involuntary part-time work, so they cover most of that line and not all of it.

"Productivity = output minus hours" is off by up to 15.9 pointsand the release itself writes the identity that way

The release text reads "productivity increased 1.4 percent, as output increased 1.7 percent and hours worked increased 0.3 percent" — and 1.7 − 0.3 is exactly 1.4. The subtraction works at ordinary rates and breaks at large ones, because the real relation is a ratio, not a difference: one plus productivity growth equals one plus output growth divided by one plus hours growth.

Measured across the 317 quarters of nonfarm business and the 157 of durable manufacturing:

FormMean errorWorst error
Subtraction0.14 p.p.15.9 p.p.
Ratio0.04 p.p.0.18 p.p.

The worst case is durable manufacturing in 2020 Q3: output rose at a 91.3% annual rate and hours at 37.1%. The subtraction says productivity rose 54.2%; the published figure is 39.5%. Nothing about 54.2% looks wrong. The 0.04 p.p. that remain in the correct form are the rounding of three series published to one decimal, and they do not shrink further. The same applies to unit labor costs, which are hourly compensation divided by productivity, not minus it.

This is why the page shows the published series for each reading rather than deriving one from another, and why the load refuses to store the table unless both identities close in ratio form — and separately in the index, where they close to 0.002.

Nothing on the productivity tab adds upthe sectors overlap, and what is published is an index

Two independent reasons, and either one alone would be enough.

The six sectors contain one another instead of partitioning. Business is nonfarm business plus farms. Manufacturing and nonfinancial corporations are both cuts from inside nonfarm business. There is no total, and no set of these rows sums to one.

And the one pair that does partition still does not add, because what is published is an index. Durable plus nondurable manufacturing is manufacturing in levels, but two indexes based at 100 in 2017 come to about 200 — measured, the ratio averages 2.02 on productivity. So stacked bars and a "% of total" reading are switched off across the whole tab, with the reason on the disabled control, and a ticked parent never turns into a line over its own parts, because it has none.

Manufacturing's two components are still shown indented under it. That indent marks containment, which is real, and not a sum, which is not.

One "Output" column, two output conceptsand the incomparability is in the catalogue, not just the footnote

The release warns in prose that the concepts, sources and methods behind manufacturing output differ from those behind business and nonfarm business output, and that the two are not directly comparable. That warning does not have to be taken on trust — it is visible in which series exist:

No sector publishes both. This page follows the release's own Table A1 in showing one Output row across all six — the alternative, two separate rows, would leave every chart half empty — and prints the warning in the chart subtitle whenever both concepts are actually plotted together, so it appears where it is relevant rather than only here.

Four more measures are published for nonfinancial corporations only: profits, unit profits, unit nonlabor costs and unit combined input costs. That is the release's Table 6, and it exists because those corporations are the only sector where the accounts separate profits from the rest of nonlabor payments. Their pills stay on screen, disabled, rather than disappearing.

Three readings, two precisions, and one number not to rebuildthe file carries three decimals; the printed release carries one

Every quantity here comes as three separate published series: the index, the change from the previous quarter compounded to an annual rate, and the change from the same quarter a year earlier. Those are the three panels of every table in the release, and the reading pill picks between them rather than computing anything.

The precisions differ, and the direction is the useful part: the index is published to three decimals and the two growth rates to one. Three decimals is exactly what the BLS says it computes with; the one-decimal index in the printed release is described there as being for convenience. Measured consequence:

Rebuilding the growth rate from…Mean errorWorst error
the index as published in the data file (3 dp)0.025 p.p. 0.0500 p.p., never above
the index as printed in the release (1 dp)0.27 p.p. 1.35 p.p.

So the first row's residual is entirely the rounding of the published rate — half of its last digit — and the rebuild is, if anything, finer. The reason this page shows the published series anyway is that the published number is the citable one: someone comparing against a headline wants 1.4, not 1.3999, and the year-over-year reading here is the BLS's rather than ours. The transferable warning is about the second row: do not rebuild a series from figures printed in a release table.

One consequence of the three-series design worth knowing: in the annual average the two growth readings carry the same value, because "previous quarter" and "same quarter a year earlier" both collapse to the previous year. The annual average is also published only for complete years, so the current year never has one.

What the business-cycle bars measure, and what they do notendpoints, not an average of quarters — and two spans that differ

Each bar is the growth rate between the endpoints of its window, annualized: the index at the start against the index at the end. It is not the average of the quarterly rates, which is a different and generally larger number whenever growth is uneven — for nonfarm business productivity since 2019 Q4 the two differ by about a fifth of a point. The release computes it the first way, and so does this page.

Two caveats the chart cannot hide:

The long-term bar starts where each sector's own series starts, which is what the release quotes — a fixed date would give manufacturing a long run its data does not cover. So the two sectors' long-term bars are not measured over the same span, and the chart's axis labels say which span each one used.

The cycle dates are the NBER peaks the BLS uses. A window that ends at a peak and one that ends in the middle of an expansion are not the same kind of object, and the current cycle is the second kind: it has not ended.

Ten of these rates are quoted in the release text, and the build refuses to publish the report unless it reproduces all ten.

These numbers get revised, and the release says by how muchthe first estimate has moved by −1.1 to +1.4 points

Each quarter is published twice, about four weeks apart, and both are on this page's release calendar. The revision is not cosmetic: in 2026 Q2, manufacturing productivity was revised from a 1.9% increase to 2.4%, and nonfarm unit labor costs from 1.3% to 1.2%.

The BLS publishes the size of this uncertainty rather than leaving it to be guessed: about 80% of the time, the third estimate of nonfarm business quarterly productivity growth has differed from the first by between −1.1 and +1.4 percentage points, measured over reference quarters from 2001 Q1 to 2026 Q1. On a series whose quarterly readings are typically 1 to 3%, that interval is wide enough to change the sign.

Two practical consequences. The first is that a single quarter is weak evidence, which is why the release frames productivity against the business cycle and why the second chart here does the same. The second is that there is no routine window to load: revisions reach back years, and the annual revision reaches back further, so every refresh rewrites the whole history rather than the tip.

The labor share is published as an index rather than as a percentage, so the level the release quotes — 52.8% of nonfarm business output in 2026 Q2, the lowest since the series began in 1947 — is not in the series itself. The index is, and it is at its own series low in the same quarter.

The payroll tree could not be inferred the usual waythe four top branches sum to 257% of the total

The BLS publishes an industry list with an indentation level and a sort order, and no parent column — the same shape as the JOLTS and CPI catalogues, where "the parent is the preceding row one level up" is enough. Here that rule builds a wrong tree, and not subtly. Total private, Goods-producing, Service-providing and Private service-providing are all at the same level and they overlap: added together they come to 257% of total nonfarm. Worse, the naive rule makes Mining and logging a child of Private service-providing, because that is the row immediately above it.

So the top of the tree on this page is declared, not derived:

Service-providing is deliberately not in the tree. It equals private services plus government, so it crosses the Total private boundary — it cannot be a level of the same hierarchy without double-counting. It is loaded in the database; what it loses is a position, not the data. The same applies to the ten residential/non-residential contractor lines in construction: the BLS publishes specialty trade contractors both by NAICS subsector and split into residential and non-residential, and both cuts close on the same parent — which makes them two axes, not two levels. Mixing them stacked to 163% of the parent.

The BLS seasonally adjusts every series independently, and says so in a footnote to its own summary table. Measured across the 284 parents of this tree:

So additivity is a construction guarantee only in the raw data. In the adjusted view the BLS imposes it at the top — levels 0 to 2 close exactly — and leaves it free in the detail. That is why the load validates the tree on the raw series and stores the adjusted deviation instead of rejecting it: validating on the adjusted data would fail a correct tree, which is what happened on the first run. A stacked view of the adjusted data is right to a tenth of a percent at the supersector level and drifts with depth.

Incomplete coverage is not an error either. The CES publishes only selected detail: 260 of the 284 parents have children that close in every month, and the 24 that do not are all at levels 4 to 6. In a complete month the 555 leaves come to 97.8% of total nonfarm.

Only four of the thirteen payroll measures add across industriesan average per worker is not a quantity
Adds across industriesDoes not
Employment, aggregate weekly hours, aggregate weekly payrolls, aggregate overtime Every average per worker (weekly hours, hourly and weekly earnings, overtime hours) and every index (2007 = 100)

Adding the average hourly earnings of two industries produces the average earnings of nothing: it is a weighted mean, and the weight — employment — is not in the series. Stacking two indexes on a 2007 base is the same mistake wearing a different unit. For the measures that do not add, stacked bars and the "% of total" type are switched off, with the reason on the disabled control. The same quantity in thousands of hours or dollars is in the measure list and does add — the index exists because it is the form the release publishes.

Nothing in this survey accumulates over twelve months. Employment is a stock; hours and earnings are weekly rates; the aggregates are one week's worth. Twelve months of a weekly payroll is not a year of payroll, so the 12-month total reading is disabled across the whole survey.

The household survey: October 2025, and three concepts that look alikefound by checking every series against the printed release

October 2025 does not exist in this survey. The federal shutdown cancelled collection; the payroll survey and JOLTS continued. A missing month empties two monthly changes — October's and November's — which is different from one month without data, and it is why those points are blank rather than bridged.

Every one of the 43 concepts on this page is checked against the number printed in the release before it is stored. That check found three series whose names read right and whose concepts are wrong:

Two more things worth knowing: the population line is never seasonally adjusted — the BLS footnotes it, and it shows a dash in the adjusted view; and the rows here are only approximately additive even before the detail, for the same independent-adjustment reason as the payroll tree (employed plus unemployed came to 169,093 against a published labour force of 169,094 in the latest month).

Only the first has an external answer key, and it is upside down. The BLS publishes the ratio itself — as unemployed persons per job opening, the reciprocal of the figure that is normally quoted. In July 2009 the published series reads 6.50 and vacancies per unemployed person was 0.153; those are the same number inverted. This page shows vacancies per unemployed person and checks it against the reciprocal of the published series, which agrees to within the one decimal the BLS publishes. The check matters because today the ratio is near 1 — about 1.05 against a published 0.95 — where an inversion would look entirely normal.

Three cautions on the other three. Net hiring is not the payroll change: different sample, universe and imputation, so read it as the turnover behind a small net number rather than as a second estimate of it. The Beveridge curve's two legs share a unit and not a base: the openings rate divides by employment plus openings, the unemployment rate by the labour force. And the establishment/household divergence is by construction — one counts filled jobs at businesses (two jobs count twice, the self-employed not at all), the other counts people (the self-employed count, two jobs count once). They will never reconcile, and the household series is far noisier: 60,000 households against 119,000 businesses.

One measure is a stock, five are flowswhy adding three months of openings gives the wrong quarter
Job openings is the number of positions open on the last business day of the month — a one-day snapshot. Hires, total separations, quits, layoffs and discharges, and other separations are counts of everything that passed through the payroll during the entire month. The release states this in a footnote to Table 7.

The consequence is arithmetic, and it is easy to get wrong without any error appearing: summing three months of openings to make a quarter returns roughly three times the right number, and still looks like a plausible chart of job openings. This report therefore offers moving averages for every measure and a 12-month total only for the five flows — the pill greys out on Job openings, because accumulating a stock produces no quantity at all.

The distinction is carried in the database too, as mt_jolts.natureza, so a future consumer does not have to know it by heart.

Rates: two different denominatorsmeasured, not paraphrased

The openings rate divides by employment plus openings — all jobs, filled and unfilled. The other five divide by employment alone. That is what the Technical Note says, and it is checkable from the published numbers alone: implied employment comes out of hires level / (hires rate / 100), and with it

Two things follow. First, the axis title on this page names the ratio rather than the unit — openings / (employment + openings), % instead of %. Second, the difference between two rates of the same month is not a quantity: openings rate minus hires rate subtracts two ratios that do not share a base.

Share of total: the denominator is each table’s own totalmining and logging is 0.30% of openings

The % of total type divides a row by the total at the top of the same table, in the same measure and the same seasonal adjustment. Mining and logging had 22 thousand job openings in July 2026 against 7,271 thousand for total nonfarm — 0.30%. Its share of hires that month was 0.47% and of quits 0.52%: a small industry can matter more for turnover than for the level of vacancies.

The denominator is that table’s own total, not one national figure for the whole page. The three tables do not share a total (see the next section), so a size class is expressed as a share of Total private (6,461 thousand openings in July 2026), a region as a share of Total US and an industry as a share of Total nonfarm. Dividing the six size classes by total nonfarm instead would make them sum to 88.86%, with the missing 11.14% being government — a sector that cut does not cover at all.

Because sibling rows all divide by the same number, shares add up the way levels do: in July 2026 the two branches of total nonfarm are 88.86% and 11.14%, the four regions 16.72 / 36.34 / 21.52 / 25.42, and the six size classes sum to 100.00%. That is why stacked bars are available here and not for rates — a rate divides by each category’s own employment, so sibling rates share no base and stacking them would invent a total. The row for the total itself reads 100.00 by construction.

The one caveat is arithmetic, not method: the BLS publishes each level rounded to the thousand, and that rounding is invisible against a 7-million total but not against a small one. Across the whole history the siblings’ shares sum to 100 within 0.15 p.p. for every measure except other separations, whose total falls as low as 168 thousand and where the worst month is 1.07 p.p. (size classes, not seasonally adjusted, November 2024). The levels behind it are within the rounding tolerance the load enforces.

Moving averages and year-over-year apply to the share, not to the levels behind it: a 3-month average is the average share over three months, and year-over-year is a difference in percentage points. A 12-month total is not offered — adding twelve shares produces no share.

The three cuts, and why they have different roots6,461 is not 7,271

JOLTS does not publish a cube. Each axis exists only at the total of the others, so the three tables on this page are three separate trees rather than three views of one:

The size-class root is the one that catches people out: it is the private sector only, so its total is 810 thousand openings smaller than the industry table's in July 2026. A tree that hung the six size classes under Total nonfarm would fail to close by exactly that, which reads as a plausible rounding gap and is not one.

Levels are additive; rates are not. Children sum to their parent in levels across the whole history, with a maximum residual of 3 thousand on the industry tree and 2 thousand on the other two. That tolerance is not chosen: the BLS publishes each level rounded to the thousand, so k children against a rounded parent can differ by up to 0.5 × (k+1) by rounding alone. The load refuses to write if any parent exceeds it — 40,656 checks per run.

State estimates are goneall 51 series stop in December 2025

The BLS catalogue still lists 51 states plus the District of Columbia for JOLTS, and every one of those series ends at 2025-M12. They are deliberately not loaded and not shown: in a report about the current month they would be dead history sitting next to live series, which is worse than an absence. The four Census regions are current (through the same month as the national series) and are the geographic cut this page offers instead.

Revisions: two months every month, five years every Januarywhy there is no “routine window”

The last two months move with questionnaires that arrive after publication — the July 2026 release revised June's openings down by 177 thousand. And with the January release, five years of data are re-benchmarked to CES employment and re-seasonally-adjusted at once.

So the load rewrites the whole history every run rather than topping up the tip. Writing only recent months would leave two vintages abutting inside the table, and that failure is invisible in the level: a permanent step at the seam becomes a twelve-month trough in the year-over-year reading, shaped exactly like an economic event. That is a defect this project has actually shipped once, on the Brazilian CAGED series.

Months the BLS marks preliminary are flagged with a p in the table.

Data gapsone month, and not in the JOLTS series themselves

Openings, hires and separations have no gap at all over 2000-12 to the present. The only blank in the JOLTS files is the unemployed persons per job opening ratio in October 2025, footnoted "Data unavailable due to the 2025 lapse in appropriations" — that ratio's denominator comes from the household survey, which stopped; the JOLTS collection did not. It is the same missing month as in this project's US CPI table.

That ratio is loaded in mt_jolts and deliberately not charted here — see the last section.

Readings offered, and why thesedisplay transforms, computed in the browser

The payload carries the published monthly value and nothing else; the five readings are derived on the fly. Shipping them pre-computed would be five times the numbers for information the monthly value already implies, and would let stored and displayed figures drift apart.

ReadingWhat it doesWhen it is unavailable
MonthlyThe published value.—
Month-over-monthThis month minus last month. A difference, so thousands for a level and percentage points for a rate or a share — never a percentage.—
3-month averageTrailing mean; needs all three months.—
12-month averageTrailing mean; needs all twelve.—
12-month totalTrailing sum; needs all twelve.Job openings (a stock does not accumulate), every rate and every share.
Year-over-year% change for levels, p.p. difference for rates and shares.—

A moving average is well defined for a stock, a flow, a rate and a share alike, which is why it is offered everywhere; a trailing sum is not, which is why it is offered only where it means something. Stacked bars follow the same logic: available for levels and for shares of the total under the first four readings (a mean of sums is the sum of means, a sum of sums is a sum, and sibling shares divide by the same total), unavailable for rates and for year-over-year, because neither adds across siblings.

Month-over-month is the change the release itself headlines — job openings +89 thousand in July 2026, hires −278, quits −157. It is deliberately a difference and not a percentage: a level in thousands and a percentage change of it are easy to confuse in a chart legend, and “+89” means 89 thousand more openings, not 89% more. Because differences of additive quantities are themselves additive, the M/M of the parts sums to the M/M of the total, so stacked bars work here as they do on the level itself.

The type control is a third axis alongside these: Level is the published count in thousands, Rate the published ratio against employment, and % of total a share of the table’s own total, derived here rather than published. The two published types are stored as the BLS releases them; the share and the five readings are computed in the browser from the monthly level.

What is not in this roundno derived metrics, on purpose

This page shows what the BLS publishes and computes nothing beyond the display readings above. No vacancies-per-unemployed ratio, no net hiring (hires − separations), no Beveridge curve — those cross into the household survey or invent a construction, and the plan is to bring the rest of the US employment data in first and build derived measures on the whole set rather than on JOLTS alone.

Loaded and waiting: the BLS's own unemployed persons per job opening series (mt_jolts, medida='UO', published seasonally adjusted only, national and by state). Not loaded: the two survey response-rate series, which measure collection quality rather than the labour market.

Source: BLS, Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey, via the bulk file jt.data.1.AllItems on download.bls.gov — one request, whole history, no API quota. The API is used on every load as an independent check that the file is on the current vintage: ten headline series over two years, and any disagreement raises rather than writing.

Tables: macro_us.mt_jolts (values) and macro_us.mt_jolts_dim (the three trees, with the parent derived from the BLS's display_level and sort_sequence, which publish no parent column). ETL: domain/db/us/labor_market/, run by jobs/update_us.py.

Validation on load: all 168 cells of the release's own Table A and all 42 of Table 7 reproduce exactly from this database for July 2026, plus the additivity checks described above.

Survey design: a stratified random sample of about 21,000 nonfarm establishments, benchmarked monthly to CES employment and seasonally adjusted with X-13ARIMA-SEATS. Standard errors are published separately by the BLS and are not in this report — a month-on-month move in a small industry is frequently inside them.
