# Fiscal Multiplier

**Type:** Transmission parameter (fiscal policy → output)
**Tags:** #fiscal-multiplier #government-spending #taxes #fiscal-policy

---

## Definition

The change in output produced by a change in government spending (or taxes), both in currency units. It is not one number: it depends on the type of spending, its persistence, how it is financed, the reaction of monetary policy and the state of the economy, so a multiplier is only meaningful together with the experiment it describes (Ramey: "a nebulous concept").

## Mechanism

- **Through hours.** With capital fixed in the short run, output rises only if hours rise; the multiplier is tied to the labor response.
- **Neoclassical channel.** Spending is a negative wealth effect: people work more and consume less. Persistent spending raises hours more than temporary spending; distortionary taxes concurrent with spending depress it, distortionary taxes expected later raise it through intertemporal substitution. With lump-sum taxes, financing is irrelevant (Ricardian equivalence).
- **Keynesian channel.** With rates fixed, 1/(1 – mpc); imports and rising interest rates lower it, accelerator effects raise it. NK models with optimizing households give values at or below unity unless many consumers are rule-of-thumb and employment is demand-determined.
- **Monetary accommodation.** At the zero lower bound, deficit-financed spending raises expected inflation and lowers the real rate, which can raise the multiplier in NK models.

## When it applies / when it fails

- **Financing and persistence decide the sign and size** in theory: temporary spending financed by concurrent distortionary taxes can have a negative multiplier; deficit-financed or permanent spending larger ones (Ramey's review of Baxter and King).
- **Purchases are not transfers.** Transfers, treated as negative lump-sum taxes, should have small effects in homogeneous-agent models with perfect capital markets.
- **Slack and the policy rate.** The case for larger multipliers in recessions and at the ZLB is theoretical and contested empirically: regime-switching estimates find higher recession multipliers, but U.S. 1939–1949, with near-zero rates, shows no amplification (Ramey).
- **Identification.** Anticipated spending changes bias VARs that use only current spending; military buildups carry war-specific effects; small changes in assumed tax elasticities move SVAR estimates.
- **Local is not aggregate.** Cross-state multipliers hold national effects (common taxes, monetary policy) constant, so they can be large where the aggregate multiplier is zero.

## BRL / application to the current analysis

Neither linked source says anything about Brazil: Ramey surveys U.S. aggregate and cross-state evidence, and Blanchard & Perotti estimate a U.S. structural VAR. What transfers is the reasoning — the multiplier depends on the type of spending, its persistence, its financing and the monetary response — not their U.S. estimates; the open-economy leakages (imports, the exchange rate, a risk premium on debt) that would matter for Brazil are not the subject of either source.
