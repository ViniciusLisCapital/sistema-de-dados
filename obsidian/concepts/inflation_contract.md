# Inflation Contract (Optimal Central-Bank Contract)

**Type:** Institutional remedy / incentive scheme
**Tags:** #inflation-contract #principal-agent #time-inconsistency #inflation-bias #central-bank-design

---

## Definition

An incentive scheme set by the government (the principal) for the central bank (the agent) in which the bank's transfer — the governor's income, the bank's budget, or more broadly legislated performance objectives — depends on realized inflation or money growth. Walsh (1995) derives the optimal one: in the standard one-period Barro–Gordon model, a transfer that falls linearly with realized money growth or inflation "eliminates the inflationary bias while still ensuring optimal stabilization policy".

## Mechanism

- **Why linear**: the inflation bias of discretion is a constant, independent of the state of nature, so the contract only needs to raise the bank's marginal cost of inflation by the same amount in every state. The bank then keeps its optimal discretionary response to shocks and only its average inflation changes (Walsh).
- **Why it beats the conservative banker**: delegating to a banker with a heavier weight on squared inflation also raises the marginal cost of inflation, but by an amount that depends on the level of inflation, which distorts the response to supply shocks — the trade-off Rogoff (1985) found. Walsh shows that trade-off "arises only when the contract offered to the central banker is arbitrarily restricted".
- **Private information is irrelevant**: since the contract depends only on observable inflation or money growth, the government gains nothing from observing the bank's private signal (Walsh, against Canzoneri 1985).
- **When the bank cares only about its transfer**: the optimal performance contract must involve output; an inflation-only contract fails unless there are no supply shocks, money has no real effect, or output stabilization has no weight. With unobserved ability and costly effort, the optimal contract becomes a contingent inflation target with a reporting requirement (Walsh, Section III).

## When it applies / when it fails

- Implementability is doubtful: a government can make side-payments that undo legislated incentives, the social loss may be large relative to any feasible transfer, and the government's output target may be private (Walsh). A dismissal rule can substitute in some cases (Walsh 1993b, as cited by Walsh).
- With private information on the banker's type, the informational requirements of the optimal rule "may be severe" (Walsh).
- Derived in a one-period model with homogeneous preferences.

## BRL / application to the current analysis

The linked sources say nothing about Brazil. The institutional example Walsh uses is New Zealand: its Reserve Bank Act of 1989 makes inflation control the sole objective and requires the Governor to agree a target rate of inflation with the government ("currently 0-2 percent"), failure to meet it being grounds for dismissal.
