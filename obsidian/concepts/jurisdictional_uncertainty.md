# Jurisdictional Uncertainty

**Type:** Institutional risk / explanation of high interest rates and missing long-term credit
**Tags:** #jurisdictional-uncertainty #institutions #long-term-credit #high-interest-rates #contract-enforcement #brazil

---

## Definition

A diffuse uncertainty about the settlement of financial contracts under a country's jurisdiction, which manifests itself "predominantly as an anti-saver and anti-creditor bias" across the executive, the legislature and the judiciary (Arida, Bacha & Lara-Resende). It combines the risk of acts of the Prince changing the value of contracts before or at their execution, and the risk of an unfavorable interpretation of the contract by the courts. Its defining feature is that it cannot be quantified beyond a short horizon, so it is not priced as a spread on long-term contracts: those contracts simply cease to exist under that jurisdiction.

## Mechanism

- **Truncated term structure.** Up to some maximum maturity T, domestic and international dollar rates are linked by near-perfect arbitrage and the uncertainty is embedded in the short-term spread over US Treasuries required to hold onshore government debt. Beyond T the domestic market disappears. Long-term instruments become illiquid, since only the original creditor knows the debtor well enough to price them, and the market cannot reveal long-term inflation expectations.
- **Identification by elimination.** Long-term credit to the country's borrowers exists offshore in foreign currency but not onshore in either currency, and the same creditors act in both markets; so the divide is the jurisdiction, not the currency or the creditor's location. Real-denominated contracts signed offshore are "contaminated" because the currency's legal foundation is domestic.
- **Distinct from related risks.** Not merely currency or inflation risk (price-indexed long instruments would otherwise exist), not frontier/transfer risk or credit risk (both priced across maturities), not the general cost of doing business (foreign firms invest directly but will not lend long locally).
- **Effects.** Higher short rates required by savers absent convertibility, lower and shorter saving, financial wealth moved offshore, preference for direct real investment; small firms constrained to retained earnings, large firms exposed to currency mismatch; weak enforcement of collateral.
- **Policy amplification.** Interventions that treat the missing long-term saving as a market failure — convertibility restrictions, artificial lengthening of public debt through banks, compulsory saving funds, forced saving through inflation and later turnover taxes — aggravate the uncertainty and raise the interest rate required under inflation targeting.

## When it applies / when it fails

- Arida, Bacha & Lara-Resende make its effect on short rates depend on currency convertibility and on the existence of a large domestic-currency public debt market: weak jurisdiction with convertibility tends to produce dollarization with moderate rates, while weak jurisdiction without convertibility, with a large captive local-currency debt market, produces high short rates and no long credit.
- Removing the distortions lowers the rate only if the program is perceived as a coherent, sustainable improvement of the jurisdiction; perceived as a window to escape, convertibility could raise the expected exchange rate instead (Arida, Bacha & Lara-Resende).
- The hypothesis is contested: Segura-Ubiergo rejects it as an explanation of Brazil's high rates because many emerging markets with weaker institutions have lower rates and the cited evidence does not support it.

## BRL / application to the current analysis

Both sources are about Brazil. Arida, Bacha & Lara-Resende argue it explains why Brazil had both unusually high short-term rates and no long-term domestic credit market after 1999; Segura-Ubiergo (2012) finds that the cross-country evidence does not support it. Neither source speaks to the period after 2012.
