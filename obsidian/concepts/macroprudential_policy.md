# Macroprudential Policy

**Type:** Policy instrument (financial stability)
**Tags:** #macroprudential #credit-growth #leverage #financial-stability

---

## Definition

Measures aimed at the financial system as a whole that limit credit growth and leverage — cyclically, tightening in the upturn (and possibly loosening in the downturn), or structurally, through permanent limits on intermediaries' leverage. Rey's tools: the Basel III countercyclical capital buffer, loan-to-value and debt-to-income ratios, monitoring of lending standards, leverage caps on FX derivative positions and levies on non-core FX liabilities of banks, and a tougher leverage ratio.

## Mechanism

Since "the most dangerous outcome of inappropriately loose global financial conditions is excessive credit growth" (Rey), acting directly on domestic credit and leverage mutes the channel through which the global financial cycle reaches an economy. It can also weaken the link between domestic monetary policy and capital inflows without capital controls: by preventing excessive credit growth in booms, the central bank may reduce banks' incentive to borrow externally when it tightens. A tougher leverage ratio curtails the system's ability to enter the feedback loops between credit, asset prices and measured risk.

## When it applies / when it fails

- Timing is the central practical issue: Rey suggests not waiting for near-certainty of a bubble, and proposes automatic rules triggered by a credit-to-GDP threshold (Borio et al.) or frequent stress tests of banks and shadow banks.
- Complex macroprudential policies "are not necessarily robust" to errors of judgment by supervisors and managers; a simple, tougher leverage ratio may lower the cost of those errors (Rey).
- Country-specific institutional details matter; domestic distortions such as subsidies to real estate and debt should be removed first (Rey).
