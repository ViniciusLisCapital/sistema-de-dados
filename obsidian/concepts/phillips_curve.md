# Phillips Curve (Expectations-Augmented, Reduced Form)

**Type:** Empirical inflation equation / slack–inflation relation
**Tags:** #phillips-curve #inflation #unemployment-gap #nairu #nonlinearity #inflation-expectations #wages

---

## Definition

The relation between economic slack and inflation, in the reduced form used by central banks: current price (or wage) inflation as a function of the unemployment gap (unemployment minus the natural rate), long-run inflation expectations, lagged inflation and supply factors such as relative import prices. Its **slope** is the response of inflation to the gap; its **persistence** is the weight on lagged inflation. Hooper, Mishkin & Sufi call this the "consensus" or "plain vanilla" version espoused by recent Fed Chairs, distinct from the New Keynesian curve in real marginal cost ([[nk_phillips_curve]]).

## Mechanism

- **Short-run and long-run slope.** With the coefficients on expectations and lagged inflation summing to one (price homogeneity), a change in the gap moves inflation today by the short-run slope and keeps moving it through the lags; the longer-term slope is the short-run slope divided by the weight on expectations. With no weight on expectations the curve is **accelerationist**: a negative gap raises inflation until the gap is closed. With a high weight on anchored expectations the same short-run slope has a small cumulative effect.
- **Wage version.** Wage growth on slack, expectations, lagged price inflation and trend productivity: a real-wage Phillips curve. It can stay steep while the price curve flattens, if the link from wages to prices weakens.
- **Nonlinearity.** The slope can steepen as the labor market tightens (the original Phillips curve was estimated nonlinearly; Tobin argued for a kink). Forms used: thresholds or splines with different slopes for negative and positive gaps, polynomials, convex functions of the unemployment-to-natural-rate ratio, and one-sided quadratics that bite only in tight markets.
- **Regime dependence.** In an anchored regime shocks are transitory and the curve is flat; if a large and persistent negative gap raises actual inflation, expectations can become adaptive, persistence rises and the curve steepens (Nalewaik's Markov-switching model, as reviewed by Hooper, Mishkin & Sufi).

## When it applies / when it fails

- **A flat estimated curve need not mean a flat true curve.** Three reasons, all products of successful policy (Hooper, Mishkin & Sufi): too few tight-labor-market episodes to detect a nonlinear slope; policy endogeneity — a central bank that leans against overheating removes the observed correlation, biasing the slope toward zero; and anchored expectations, which lower persistence and the long-run slope for as long as the anchor holds.
- **Regional data are a partial fix.** State and metropolitan-area panels with year fixed effects remove national variation, including monetary policy, and contain many tight-market observations; they show steeper, nonlinear curves. The cost is theory: regional coefficients are not national ones, and the link between them is not well established.
- **The gap is only as good as the natural rate.** The curve uses an estimated natural rate; a misperceived one (the 1960s case) makes the labor market look looser than it is.
- **The sign of a flattening depends on policy staying put.** If the central bank responds passively, or expectations unanchor, the flattened curve is the wrong guide; the estimated slope describes the policy regime as much as the economy.
- **Global factors.** Hooper, Mishkin & Sufi find global output gaps and trade intensity add little to the US curve; relative import prices are the external term that matters.

## BRL / application to the current analysis

One linked source estimates a Brazilian curve. [[economic_activity/synthesis/taxa_desemprego_equilibrio_brasil (Borges, 2022)]] uses a wage curve — unit labor cost growth on unemployment, with the minimum wage net of productivity, the terms of trade and rainfall as controls, 1996–2017 — not a price curve, and uses it to recover the natural rate rather than to measure a slope. The curve over-predicts unit labor costs in 2018–2021 given observed unemployment, which the author reads as a shift in the curve's intercept (a lower NAWRU after the 2017 labor reform), not as a flattening. It is the caveat above — the gap is only as good as the natural rate — seen from the other side: a natural rate that moves shows up as a curve that stops fitting, and the reading as a shift rather than a change in slope is the author's. The post reports no coefficients, and whether the wage-based equilibrium also holds for consumer prices is left open. The other two sources are about the United States.
