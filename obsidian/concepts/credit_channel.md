# Credit Channel of Monetary Transmission

**Type:** Transmission channel
**Tags:** #credit-channel #bank-lending #monetary-transmission #financial-frictions

---

## Definition

The view that monetary policy, and shocks originating in the financial system, affect aggregate demand through the supply and cost of credit, and not only through the interest rate on a single "bond" that the money market sets. It rests on bank loans (customer-market credit) being imperfect substitutes for auction-market credit, because of informational problems, differences in liquidity or the transactions costs of raising funds in the open market — so bank assets, not only bank liabilities, matter for transmission (Bernanke & Blinder). Credit rationing is not necessary for it.

## Mechanism

- **Three assets instead of two.** Bernanke & Blinder add a loan market to IS/LM: loan demand depends on the loan rate, the bond rate and income; loan supply is a share of banks' deposits net of required reserves, depending on the two rates. Folding the loan market into the IS curve gives the **CC curve** ("commodities and credit"), which slopes down like IS but is shifted by bank reserves and by credit-supply and credit-demand shocks.
- **Monetary policy works on both curves.** A rise in reserves shifts both LM and CC outward, so policy is more expansionary than in IS/LM; with a horizontal LM (liquidity trap) monetary policy still works through CC.
- **Credit-supply shocks are independent demand shocks.** A fall in credit supply lowers credit, income and the bond rate while raising the loan rate (Bernanke & Blinder's reading of the Great Depression and of the 1980 credit controls).

## When it applies / when it fails

- It disappears when loans and bonds are perfect substitutes for borrowers or for lenders, or when spending does not respond to the loan rate — the special assumptions implicit in the money-only view (Bernanke & Blinder).
- Its policy implications depend on which shocks dominate: credit is a good indicator of future GNP except when credit-demand shocks are important, and money is except when money-demand shocks are.

## How it appears across sources

| Source | Role |
|---|---|
| [[monetary_policy/synthesis/credit_money_aggregate_demand (Bernanke & Blinder, 1988)]] | The CC–LM model: loans and bonds as imperfect substitutes, credit-supply shocks as independent sources of fluctuations, and money versus credit as indicators and targets |
| [[monetary_policy/synthesis/credit_channel_transmission (Bernanke & Gertler, 1995)]] | The credit channel as an amplifier of interest-rate effects through the external finance premium, split into a balance sheet channel ("fairly well established") and a bank lending channel (weakened by deregulation); credit aggregates are not a valid test |
| [[monetary_policy/synthesis/financial_accelerator (Bernanke, Gertler & Gilchrist, 1999)]] | The balance sheet channel in a calibrated DNK model: net worth lowers the external finance premium, so a monetary shock has larger and more persistent real effects; the bank lending channel is left out |
| [[monetary_policy/synthesis/unconventional_policy_model (Gertler & Karadi, 2011)]] | Intermediary capital as the constraint on credit: losses on bank assets raise spreads and cut investment, and a monetary tightening is amplified through intermediary balance sheets |
| [[monetary_policy/synthesis/monetary_policy_shocks_survey (Christiano, Eichenbaum & Evans, 1999)]] | Survey of VAR evidence: after a contractionary shock bank deposits fall, banks sell securities before loans fall; consumer and real estate loans fall but commercial and industrial loans do not; small firms bear a disproportionate share |
| [[monetary_policy/synthesis/liquidity_trap_japan (Krugman, 1998)]] | Argues against a bank-centred reading of Japan: in a trap base expansion cannot raise broad money and may shrink bank credit even with healthy banks; insolvent banks with guaranteed deposits overlend; the credit crunch came only with new capital adequacy standards |
| [[monetary_policy/synthesis/implementing_it_brazil (Bogdanski, Tombini & Werlang, 2000)]] | BCB staff judge the credit channel negligible in Brazil up to 1999 (low corporate leverage, tight Real-plan policies) and expect it to grow as bank spreads fall |
