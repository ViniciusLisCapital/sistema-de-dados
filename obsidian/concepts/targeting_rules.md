# Targeting Rules (vs. Instrument Rules)

**Type:** Policy rule concept
**Tags:** #targeting-rules #instrument-rules #policy-rule #inflation-targeting

---

## Definition

A targeting rule (Svensson's "target rule") states the condition the target variables must satisfy — for example, that the inflation forecast equal the target, or that the output gap be proportional to inflation — and leaves the instrument setting to be derived from the model. An instrument rule specifies the reaction function of the instrument directly in terms of current information (the McCallum rule for the monetary base, the Taylor rule for the federal funds rate).

## Mechanism

- **From target rule to reaction function.** Setting the instrument to satisfy a target rule yields an endogenous instrument rule. In Svensson's model the target rules (2.9) and (6.8) depend only on the Phillips curve and the loss function, while the implied instrument rules (2.13) and (6.11) also depend on the aggregate demand equation.
- **Optimal policy written as a targeting rule.** In Galí's chapter 5, the first-order condition under discretion, an output gap proportional to inflation with a negative sign (4), is a "leaning against the wind" targeting rule; under commitment the optimal plan is a "levels" rule (13) relating the output gap to the deviation of the price level from an implicit target.

## When it applies / when it fails

- Svensson's case for committing to a target rule: it "focuses on the essential", revises the reaction function automatically when structural relationships change, and is "inherently more stable" and easier to identify, motivate and verify; an instrument rule needs more confidence in the model or frequent revisions that may be hard to motivate.
- The practical limit, in Galí: a targeting rule like (4) needs real-time knowledge of efficient output, and the interest rate rule that implements the discretionary outcome needs the model's parameters and real-time observation of the cost-push shock and the efficient rate.
- A targeting rule alone does not guarantee a unique equilibrium: Galí adds an inflation response satisfying the Taylor principle to the interest rate rule that implements it.
