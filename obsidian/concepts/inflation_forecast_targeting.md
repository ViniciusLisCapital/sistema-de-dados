# Inflation Forecast Targeting

**Type:** Policy strategy / implementation of inflation targeting
**Tags:** #inflation-targeting #inflation-forecast-targeting #intermediate-target #transparency

---

## Definition

The implementation of inflation targeting in which the central bank's own inflation forecast, at the horizon of the control lag, is treated as an explicit intermediate target: the instrument is set so that the forecast equals the inflation target (Svensson). Because the instrument affects inflation only with a lag, current inflation is predetermined and shocks within the control lag cannot be offset; the best the bank can do is put its forecast on target.

## Mechanism

- **Why the forecast.** In a model where the policy rate moves output with a one-year lag and inflation with a two-year lag, minimizing expected squared deviations of inflation from target reduces, by certainty equivalence, to setting the two-year forecast equal to the target (Svensson's (2.9)). The implied reaction function uses all information that predicts inflation; it has the form of a Taylor rule but is derived, not imposed.
- **An "ideal" intermediate target.** The forecast is by definition the current variable most correlated with future inflation, more controllable than inflation, observable now, and transparent: forecast above target → tighten, below → ease. Predicting policy becomes predicting inflation.
- **Output stabilization as speed.** With a positive weight on output, the forecast is brought back to target gradually: its deviation is a fraction c of the predetermined one-year forecast's deviation, with c rising in the output weight (Svensson's (6.8)–(6.9)). This is flexible inflation targeting stated as a target rule.
- **Monitoring.** The public can check the bank by comparing its own or outside ("Consensus") forecasts with the target, and the bank's repo plan with the forward rate curve; publishing the bank's model, assumptions and forecast (Inflation Reports) is the strongest discipline.

## When it applies / when it fails

- The target must be the bank's own structural forecast: targeting outside forecasters' consensus can produce instability, multiple equilibria or no equilibrium (Woodford 1994, as cited by Svensson).
- It needs a view of how the instrument affects the forecast; model uncertainty makes both implementation and monitoring harder, though Svensson argues the framework gives strong incentives to improve the model.
- Accountability for forecasts rather than outcomes requires forecasts that can be verified; until then Svensson prefers sanctions based on realized inflation.
- Money growth or exchange rate targeting coincides with it only when money growth or depreciation is a sufficient statistic for future inflation; otherwise they are inefficient.
