# Commodity Shocks (Demand vs. Price)

**Type:** External shock / transmission channel
**Tags:** #commodities #china #terms-of-trade #country-risk #emerging-markets #commodity-currency

---

## Definition

Shocks from the world commodity sector to a commodity-exporting economy, split by origin. A **commodity demand shock** is a rise in demand for resources from a large importer — in the linked source, Chinese resource demand, proxied by Chinese steel production — that raises commodity prices *and* the volumes the exporter sells. A **commodity price shock** is a move in the exporter's commodity prices not explained by that demand; the linked source reads it as a supply shock, because commodity (steel) production falls and foreign output eventually falls after it. The distinction matters because the two shocks raise the same price index but have different persistence and different effects on the exporter's economy.

## Mechanism

For an emerging-market exporter (Fry-McKibbin & Souza, Brazil):

1. **Export channel.** Higher demand raises commodity prices and the real value of resource exports; resource exports can rise even while the currency appreciates.
2. **Country-risk channel.** Better terms of trade and export earnings lower the spread investors demand on the country's foreign borrowing, so the interest rate the economy faces falls as the boom proceeds. Lower borrowing costs attract capital and amplify the expansion of the non-resource economy.
3. **Exchange-rate channel.** Export earnings and the lower interest rate appreciate the real exchange rate (a commodity currency); the appreciation offsets the inflationary effect of the boom.
4. **Sectoral channel.** Whether the non-resource sector expands with the resource sector or shrinks (Dutch disease) depends on the policy-rate response, on whether real resource exports hold up through the appreciation, and on factor reallocation.

## When it applies / when it fails

- **EME vs. developed exporter.** The country-risk channel is what makes the boom expansionary in an EME: the borrowing rate falls. In a developed exporter the central bank tends to raise rates against the inflationary effect of the boom, which is one of the reasons Australia shows symptoms of Dutch disease in Dungey et al. and Brazil does not (as reported by Fry-McKibbin & Souza).
- **Demand-driven booms last longer.** In the linked source the Chinese demand shock keeps Brazilian activity above baseline for about six years; the price shock orthogonal to it fades much faster.
- **Omitting demand overstates prices and rates.** When Chinese demand is left out, commodity prices absorb its effect, and interest-rate shocks absorb part of the commodity effect — earlier studies' large role for the interest rate in EME cycles shrinks once both are in the model.
- **The exchange-rate regime conditions the transmission.** A floating currency is a shock absorber; estimating over a pre-float sample overstates the effect of Chinese demand on domestic variables (Fry-McKibbin & Souza, Brazil from 1996Q1 vs. 1999Q1).
- **Identification is the weak point.** The split rests on ordering demand first and on one proxy for it; demand not captured by the proxy (food rather than metals) would be counted as a price shock.

## BRL / application to the current analysis

The linked source is about Brazil, 1999–2017: Chinese demand for soybeans, iron ore and oil is the main external driver of the cycle, through exports, a lower external borrowing rate and an appreciating real, with inflation offset by the appreciation. It says nothing about the period after 2017.
