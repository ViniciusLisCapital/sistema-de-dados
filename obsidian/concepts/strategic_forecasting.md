# Strategic Forecasting

**Type:** Incentive distortion in reported expectations
**Tags:** #inflation-expectations #professional-forecasters #beauty-contest #strategic-substitutability #focus-survey

---

## Definition

Professional forecasters report forecasts that serve goals other than accuracy alone: staying close to the consensus (strategic complementarity), standing apart from it (strategic substitutability), signalling ability, or attracting publicity and clients. The reported forecast then differs from the forecaster's best estimate given her information, and survey averages built from such forecasts carry the distortion.

## Mechanism

- **Beauty-contest payoff** (Areosa, after Morris and Shin). A forecaster's loss weighs the distance of her forecast from inflation and its distance from the mean forecast. Because the public signal is the better predictor of what others will forecast, a wish to stay close to peers raises the weight on public information above its precision-based (Bayesian) weight; a wish to differentiate lowers it, so private information is overweighted.
- **Publicity and reputation** (models reviewed by Coibion, Gorodnichenko & Kamdar). If the best forecaster of a period gains publicity, forecasters trade accuracy for the chance of standing out; truthful reporting may be an unlikely equilibrium in cheap-talk settings; in repeated forecasting, forecasters may imitate the patterns of more able forecasters.
- **Contest incentives.** Brazil's Top 5 ranking rewards accuracy on reference dates; it raises attention and updating around those dates (studies surveyed by Gaglianone) and may also reward differentiation (Areosa's conjecture).

## When it applies / when it fails

- It is a property of professional forecasters, whose forecasts are a product; consumers have little reason to misreport, which is one argument for using household surveys as a proxy for firms' expectations (Coibion, Gorodnichenko & Kamdar).
- The direction is empirical: in the Focus panel, 2004–2014, Areosa estimates substitutability (overweighting of private information) at almost every horizon; much of the earlier literature assumed complementarity.
- The estimate rests on a structural model; a low weight on the consensus could also reflect newer private information when the consensus is dated, an alternative Areosa argues against but does not test.
- Strategic incentives are one of several sources of bias and disagreement that do not require irrationality, alongside asymmetric loss functions and information rigidities (Gaglianone, footnote 7).

## BRL / application to the current analysis

The Brazilian evidence is on the Focus survey: forecasters overweight their private information relative to the consensus, more so when the consensus is the Top 5 group's, and the Top 5 contest shapes when they update. The sources do not say how this affects the consensus median that the BCB publishes.
