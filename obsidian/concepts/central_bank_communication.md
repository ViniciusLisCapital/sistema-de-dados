# Central Bank Communication (Transparency)

**Type:** Policy instrument / management of expectations
**Tags:** #central-bank-communication #transparency #expectations #predictability

---

## Definition

"The provision of information by the central bank to the public regarding such matters as the objectives of monetary policy, the monetary policy strategy, the economic outlook, and the outlook for future policy decisions" (Blinder et al.). Communication about goals and strategy (for example an inflation target) is "long-run"; communication about the outlook and policy inclinations is "short-run".

## Mechanism

- **Expectations, not the overnight rate.** Few decisions hinge on the overnight rate; long rates reflect expected future short rates, so a central bank's influence depends on shaping the expected path. Communication works by "creating news" (moving expectations and asset prices) and by "reducing noise" (making actions predictable), raising the signal-to-noise ratio. At the zero lower bound communication about future rates "becomes the essence of monetary policy" (Blinder et al.).
- **Why it is not redundant.** With a stationary economy, a known fixed rule and rational expectations, the public could infer everything from behavior. Nonstationarity, learning, non-rational expectations or asymmetric information — "the norm, not the exception" — make communication matter; when the public learns the reaction function, communication can avert unstable or indeterminate outcomes.
- **Communication is not commitment.** Announcing a decision, a target or a conditional forecast does not bind future actions, though the conditionality of a forecast may be hard to convey (Blinder et al.; the Riksbank's "It is a forecast, not a promise").

## When it applies / when it fails

- More talk is not always better: Duisenberg's October 2000 remark on euro intervention and Bernanke's April 2006 off-the-record remark moved markets unintentionally.
- Committees can produce a "cacophony" of voices; more dispersed Fed communication is associated with less predictable decisions, though not for the Czech National Bank. The remedy is "clarity, not silence".
- The theoretical cases for opacity — Cukierman and Meltzer's surprise argument and Morris and Shin's coordination argument — rest on assumptions (only unanticipated money matters; central bank signals much noisier than private ones) that Blinder et al. judge unrealistic, especially for information about the bank's own intentions.
- Evidence is mostly for advanced economies and financial markets; effects on the real economy and on the general public are largely unstudied.
