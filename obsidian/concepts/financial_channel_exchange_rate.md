# Financial Channel of Exchange Rates

**Type:** Transmission channel
**Tags:** #financial-channel #exchange-rates #dollar #currency-mismatch #cross-border-bank-lending #emerging-markets

---

## Definition

The effect of exchange rate movements on activity through balance sheets rather than through net exports: a change in the exchange rate revalues borrowers' assets and liabilities and changes lenders' capacity to take risk, which moves credit and, through credit, investment. When borrowers owe in a funding currency (in practice the dollar) and hold assets in local currency, the channel runs **opposite to the trade channel**: a stronger dollar, which the trade channel would read as a stimulus for the rest of the world, tightens credit and lowers activity there. In the words of Avdjiev, Bruno, Koch and Shin: "it is when the domestic currency appreciates that balance sheets strengthen and economic activity picks up."

## Mechanism

Two routes, separated by which exchange rate drives them:

- **Credit demand (bilateral rate).** A dollar appreciation against the local currency raises the local value of a mismatched borrower's dollar debt relative to its assets, lowering its net worth and its demand for credit. Even dollar-earning borrowers are hit, through higher debt service. Hedging adds to the regularity from the other side: exporters and asset managers with dollar assets and local-currency obligations borrow in dollars when they expect the dollar to fall.
- **Credit supply (broad dollar index).** The "risk-taking channel of currency appreciation": a broad dollar appreciation weakens all mismatched dollar borrowers at once, raising tail risk in global banks' diversified dollar loan books; under a value-at-risk or economic capital limit, the banks' lending capacity falls and they cut cross-border dollar credit. A weaker dollar relaxes the limit and expands credit.

The chain is dollar → dollar cross-border bank credit → investment, and it has a price dimension (EME sovereign spreads fall when the local currency appreciates) as well as the quantity one.

## When it applies / when it fails

From the sources so far:

- **Needs currency mismatch.** Borrowers with dollar liabilities and local-currency assets or cash flows. Without mismatch the dollar does not move net worth and the channel has nothing to work on.
- **Needs a risk-sensitive lending constraint** at the global lender for the supply route; the broad dollar matters because it moves the whole loan book at once.
- **Strongest for firms dependent on external finance**, and in non-tradable sectors, where no competitiveness gain offsets it; tradable-sector firms can invest more after a dollar appreciation (the trade channel).
- **Varies with the stock of dollar debt.** In Avdjiev et al. the firm-level effect is driven by the period after 2011, when EME corporate dollar borrowing rose, and by emerging Asia rather than emerging Europe.
- **Can dominate the trade channel** in overall activity: in the same source GDP growth falls after a dollar appreciation against the local currency. The bilateral rate alone can still show the textbook positive effect on investment, so which index is used decides which channel the data reveal.
