# Natural Rate of Unemployment

**Type:** Equilibrium benchmark / labor-market state variable
**Tags:** #natural-rate #phillips-curve #expectations #accelerationism #unemployment

---

## Definition

The level of unemployment "consistent with equilibrium in the structure of real wage rates", at which real wages rise at a normal secular rate; "the level that would be ground out by the Walrasian system of general equilibrium equations, provided there is imbedded in them the actual structural characteristics of the labor and commodity markets, including market imperfections, stochastic variability in demands and supplies, the cost of gathering information about job vacancies and labor availabilities, the costs of mobility, and so on" (Friedman). Below it there is excess demand for labor and upward pressure on real wages; above it, excess supply. It need not correspond to equality between unemployed and vacancies.

## Mechanism

- **Only unanticipated inflation moves unemployment.** Faster money growth first raises output and employment because product prices respond faster than factor prices: real wages fall ex post for employers while anticipated real wages rise for employees. As employees revise their anticipations, real wages return toward their initial level and unemployment returns to the natural rate (Friedman).
- **Accelerationism.** Holding unemployment below the natural rate requires ever faster inflation; holding it above requires accelerating deflation. "There is always a temporary trade-off between inflation and unemployment; there is no permanent trade-off" — the trade-off comes from a rising, not a high, rate of inflation.
- **The Phillips curve restated.** A nominal-wage Phillips curve is stable only while anticipated inflation is stable; its level rises with the average rate of price change. In terms of anticipated real wages "it all falls into place".

## When it applies / when it fails

- It is not immutable: many of its determinants are "man-made and policy-made" (minimum wages, union strength, information about vacancies), and it changes over time (Friedman).
- It cannot be known: "we have as yet devised no method to estimate accurately and readily the natural rate of either interest or unemployment", and targeting it directly would not give a determinate policy (Friedman).
- Timing is a judgment, not an estimate: Friedman puts the initial effects of a higher unanticipated inflation at "two to five years" and full adjustment at "a couple of decades", faster for very large inflation changes.

## BRL / application to the current analysis

Friedman uses Brazil as his illustration: with prices anticipated to rise "more than 75 per cent a year", wages had to rise at that rate to keep real wages unchanged, and when Brazil brought the price rise down to "about 45 per cent a year" there was "a sharp initial rise in unemployment" because wages kept rising faster than the new rate of price rise. He also cites Brazil and Chile as cases where high nominal interest rates went with rapid money growth. The linked sources say nothing about Brazil's natural rate of unemployment today.
