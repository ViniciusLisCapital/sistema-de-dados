# Fiscal Rules

**Type:** Fiscal policy framework (institutional constraint)
**Tags:** #fiscal-rules #expenditure-rule #fiscal-framework #fiscal-discipline

---

## Definition

A permanent constraint on fiscal policy, set in law or regulation, on an aggregate such as spending, the primary balance or debt, meant to "restringir a discricionariedade da política fiscal e promover a disciplina fiscal" (Eyraud et al., 2018, as cited by Conof-Conle, 2023). The rule is a commitment device: by tying the government's hands it signals to markets that debt sustainability is a priority, which is supposed to lower risk premia and support growth.

## Mechanism

A complete framework combines three pieces:

1. **An operational rule** on what the government controls directly — typically the growth of primary spending.
2. **An anchor** — a primary-balance target, or a debt path, that ties the operational rule to sustainability.
3. **Enforcement** — automatic correction when the target is missed (tighter spending growth, bans on new mandatory spending or tax benefits, spending freezes during the year) and escape clauses for exceptional shocks.

Brazil's LCP 200/2023 is an example of all three: real spending growth set at a fraction of real revenue growth (70% if the previous year's target was met, 50% if not) inside a fixed band, primary targets with a tolerance interval, and triggers that switch on constitutional spending bans after a miss. Because spending grows more slowly than revenue whenever revenue grows above the floor, the primary balance improves each year; because the band has a floor and a ceiling, spending neither has to fall in a recession nor rise one-for-one with revenue in a boom, which is what makes the rule countercyclical by design.

## When it applies / when it fails

- **Simplicity, flexibility and enforceability** are the three properties second-generation rules need; a rule that lacks one tends to be abandoned or circumvented (Conof-Conle, 2023, citing Eyraud et al., 2018).
- **A spending rule cannot reach legislated mandatory spending.** Caps and in-year freezes only compress discretionary spending; mandatory spending grows by its own legislation and by demographics, and a missed payment becomes a liability. Without reforms the adjustment falls on investment and the discretionary core.
- **Political sustainability matters as much as the arithmetic.** Brazil's previous ceiling (EC 95/2016), indexed to inflation only, ignored vegetative growth of mandatory spending and was amended repeatedly; a rule that ignores this invites exceptions.
- **Exclusions erode the rule.** Every expenditure taken out of the limit, or out of the target computation, weakens control and opens a gap between the government's and the central bank's measures of the balance.
- **A spending rule alone may stabilize debt too slowly.** It improves the primary balance gradually; when the starting deficit is large, debt and interest keep rising meanwhile, so the targets have to do the rest — and early on, with spending already parameterized, the targets depend on revenue.
- **The anchor can be weak** if the target is easy to revise (Brazilian LDOs are amended several times a year) or if the law does not say at what level debt should stabilize.

The only linked source is the Chamber of Deputies' technical note of November 2023, which describes the LCP 200/2023 framework and flags its weak points as of that date: early dependence on revenue to meet the targets, unaddressed court-ordered payments (precatórios) returning inside the limit in 2027, and frequent LDO amendments.
