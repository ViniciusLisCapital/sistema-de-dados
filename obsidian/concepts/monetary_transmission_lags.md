# Monetary Transmission Lags

**Type:** Empirical regularity (transmission mechanism)
**Tags:** #transmission-lags #monetary-transmission #var #policy-horizon

---

## Definition

The time it takes for a change in monetary policy to have its effect on the economy — the "long and variable" lags of the traditional view. Havranek & Rusnak measure it as the horizon at which the price level's fall after a monetary contraction is largest in VAR impulse responses (the transmission lag), and alternatively as the time until prices fall by 0.1 percent after a 1-percentage-point hike.

## Mechanism

- **Why lags matter for policy.** Policy acts on today's conditions while its effects arrive months later, which is why inflation-targeting central banks set policy horizons rather than react to current inflation (Havranek & Rusnak: most use a horizon between twelve and twenty-four months).
- **Why they vary across countries.** Havranek & Rusnak find financial development the only robust country-level determinant: where financial institutions can hedge against monetary surprises they need not react at once, so transmission is slower; where capitalization is low or instruments unsophisticated they react immediately. More open economies and more independent central banks show faster transmission on one measure, pointing to the exchange-rate and expectations channels.
- **Why they vary across studies.** Monthly data report shorter lags than quarterly data; strictly decreasing impulse responses (which may signal a non-stationary VAR, and whose lag depends on the reporting window) report much longer ones.

## When it applies / when it fails

- Estimates are sensitive to method: the lag measured from a strictly decreasing response is the last horizon reported, not a genuine trough.
- The relation to financial development implies that transmission in emerging economies may slow as their financial systems develop (Havranek & Rusnak).

## How it appears across sources

| Source | Role |
|---|---|
| [[monetary_policy/synthesis/transmission_lags_meta_analysis (Havranek & Rusnak, 2013)]] | Meta-analysis of 198 VAR impulse responses from sixty-seven studies: prices bottom out about 29.2 months after a contraction once misspecification is filtered out, with lags of roughly twenty-five to fifty months in developed and ten to twenty in post-transition economies; financial development slows transmission |
| [[monetary_policy/synthesis/monetary_policy_shocks_survey (Christiano, Eichenbaum & Evans, 1999)]] | Benchmark recursive VAR responses (US, 1965:3–1995:2): output declines after a delay of 2 quarters with its trough roughly a year to a year and a half out; the GDP deflator is flat for roughly a year and a half; the price puzzle appears when commodity prices are left out |
| [[monetary_policy/synthesis/new_measure_monetary_shocks (Romer & Romer, 2004)]] | With shocks purged of the Fed's forecasts, industrial production starts falling after five months and bottoms at -4.3 percent in months 22 through 27; the price level is virtually unchanged for 22 months and 5.9 percent lower after 48; conventional funds-rate measures make both responses slower |
| [[monetary_policy/synthesis/implementing_it_brazil (Bogdanski, Tombini & Werlang, 2000)]] | BCB staff view for Brazil, stated not estimated: the rate reaches durables and investment in 3 to 6 months and inflation through the gap in 6 to 9, while the exchange-rate channel acts contemporaneously |

## BRL / application to the current analysis

The linked sources say nothing about Brazil: Havranek & Rusnak report country averages only for developed and post-transition economies with at least five impulse responses.
