# Monetary Targeting (Money Growth Rule)

**Type:** Policy rule / guide for policy
**Tags:** #money-growth-rule #monetarism #monetary-aggregates #rules-vs-discretion

---

## Definition

Conducting monetary policy by a monetary total — a quantity the central bank controls closely — rather than by interest rates, unemployment or the price level directly. In its strict form, a publicly stated steady rate of growth in a specified monetary aggregate (Friedman's prescription).

## Mechanism

- **Guide by what you control.** The authority controls nominal quantities — directly, its own liabilities — and cannot peg real ones. Taking interest rates or unemployment as the criterion is "like a space vehicle that has taken a fix on the wrong star" (Friedman).
- **Why a monetary total and not the price level.** The price level is the most important guide in its own right, but its link to policy actions is more indirect and the lag is longer and variable, so aiming at it directly risks "false stops and starts"; the monetary total is "the best currently available immediate guide", and "it matters much less which particular total is chosen than that one be chosen" (Friedman).
- **Why a steady rate.** Authorities act on today's conditions while effects come months later, so they overreact ("Too late and too much"). A stated, steady growth rate removes the swings; Friedman estimates "something like a 3 to 5 per cent per year rate of growth in currency plus all commercial bank deposits" for rough price stability.

## When it applies / when it fails

- It relies on a stable money demand: Friedman notes that empirically money demand is "highly stable" if the effects of monetary policy itself are excluded, which is why looking at supply alone is enough to judge tightness.
- Friedman's preference for a monetary total over the price level is explicitly conditional on "the present stage of our understanding".

## How it appears across sources

| Source | Role |
|---|---|
| [[monetary_policy/synthesis/the_role_monetary_policy (Friedman, 1968)]] | The case for a monetary total as the immediate guide and for a publicly stated steady rate of money growth |
| [[monetary_policy/synthesis/optimal_commitment_intermediate_target (Rogoff, 1985)]] | Money-supply targeting as an incentive scheme against the inflation bias: the optimal weight on the target is positive and finite, and infinite (a k percent rule) only without aggregate demand shocks; with interest-elastic money demand an optimistic target raises time-consistent inflation |
| [[monetary_policy/synthesis/classical_monetary_model_ch2 (Gali, 2015)]] | In a flexible-price economy an exogenous money supply path always determines the price level uniquely, but the price level overreacts to persistent money growth shocks and there is no liquidity effect |
| [[monetary_policy/synthesis/inflation_forecast_targeting (Svensson, 1997)]] | Money growth targeting as money growth forecast targeting: a conditional target replicates the optimal inflation-targeting policy less transparently; an unconditional target equal to the inflation target gives more inflation variability; optimal only if money growth is a sufficient statistic for future inflation (Section 5) |
| [[monetary_policy/synthesis/credit_money_aggregate_demand (Bernanke & Blinder, 1988)]] | Money versus credit as targets: money targeting destabilizes GNP under money-demand shocks ("the familiar Achilles heel of monetarism"), credit targeting under credit-demand shocks; in the 1980s money-demand shocks dominated |

## BRL / application to the current analysis

The linked sources say nothing about monetary targeting in Brazil. Friedman mentions Brazil only as a case of high nominal interest rates associated with rapid money growth.
