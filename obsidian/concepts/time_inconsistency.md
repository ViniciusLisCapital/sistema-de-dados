# Time Inconsistency (Rules vs. Discretion)

**Type:** Credibility problem / policy-design principle
**Tags:** #time-inconsistency #rules-vs-discretion #commitment #credibility #inflation-bias

---

## Definition

A plan is time-inconsistent when, after private agents have acted on it, the policymaker would no longer choose to carry it out. Kydland & Prescott (1977): a *consistent* policy is one in which each period's decision is best given past decisions and given that future decisions are selected the same way; because agents' current decisions depend on expected future policy, the consistent policy is generally suboptimal, and the optimal plan is inconsistent. Discretion means reoptimizing every period (the consistent policy); a rule means committing to a plan, which can do better. Clarida, Galí & Gertler call the policy under discretion "time consistent" and the committed one a rule.

## Mechanism

- **Why discretion loses**: under discretion the policymaker takes expectations as given and ignores the effect of its future choices on agents' earlier decisions; "there is no mechanism to induce future policymakers to take into consideration the effect of their policy, via the expectations mechanism, upon current decisions of agents" (Kydland & Prescott). The suboptimality "is not due to myopia".
- **Inflationary bias**: with unemployment depending on the gap between actual and expected inflation and rational expectations, the discretionary equilibrium has inflation high enough that the policymaker is no longer tempted to surprise, with no gain in unemployment; committing to price stability reaches a better point (Kydland & Prescott, Figure 1). In Clarida, Galí & Gertler's New Keynesian version, an output target above potential produces inflation "persistently above target, and no gain in output" (Result 5).
- **Gains from commitment without an output bias**: when price setting depends on expected future conditions, a credible commitment to be tough in the future improves today's output-inflation tradeoff; the restricted optimum looks like discretion with a higher weight on inflation, and the unconstrained optimum makes the output gap respond in differences, keeping policy tight as long as inflation is above target (Clarida, Galí & Gertler, Results 7–8).
- **Remedies discussed**: rules evaluated by economic theory, simple enough that deviations are obvious, possibly legislated with a 2-year delay (Kydland & Prescott); a conservative central banker who weighs inflation more than society (Rogoff 1985, as presented by Clarida, Galí & Gertler — it can replicate the restricted commitment optimum but not the unconstrained one); reputation (forward guidance literature).

## When it applies / when it fails

- It requires only that agents have "some knowledge of how policymakers' decisions will change as a result of changing economic conditions" — partial predictability of policy is enough (Kydland & Prescott).
- How relevant the inflationary bias is in practice is disputed: Clarida, Galí & Gertler report the Friedman–Kuttner objection (inflation came down without the institutional changes the theory calls for) and Blinder's and McCallum's view that central banks do not try to push output above potential; they argue the commitment gains survive without that bias.
- No major central bank makes a binding commitment, so discretion is the realistic benchmark (Clarida, Galí & Gertler).
