# Forward Guidance

**Type:** Policy instrument / central bank communication
**Tags:** #forward-guidance #delphic #odyssean #communication #zlb #expectations

---

## Definition

Public statements by a central bank about the likely future path of its policy rate. Campbell, Evans, Fisher & Justiniano (2012) split it in two:

- **Delphic** — a public forecast of macroeconomic performance and of likely or intended policy, based on the central bank's possibly superior information. It does not commit the central bank.
- **Odyssean** — a public commitment to a future course of action ("just as Odysseus committed himself to staying on his ship by having himself bound to the mast").

## Mechanism

Odyssean guidance works by changing expectations of tomorrow's policy so as to improve outcomes today: at the zero lower bound, a credible promise to keep the rate at zero longer than conditions would warrant raises expected future activity and inflation and so current demand (the Krugman / Eggertsson–Woodford / Werning argument the paper cites). Its weakness is time inconsistency: when the date arrives, the central bank and the public prefer to ignore the promise, so credibility rests on reputation. Delphic guidance improves outcomes only by reducing private uncertainty — and it can backfire: Woodford (comment) argues that announcing a later liftoff date read as a weaker outlook under an unchanged reaction function should be contractionary, not expansionary.

Measured effects (Campbell et al.): the GSS "path factor" of FOMC statement surprises moves expected rates at longer horizons, Treasury yields and long-term corporate yields; forward guidance identified as anticipated deviations from an estimated interest rate rule is large (about 80 percent of the FOMC's deviations from the rule anticipated, 1996–2007) and moves bond yields more than contemporaneous policy surprises.

## When it applies / when it fails

- The two kinds are hard to separate empirically. Private forecasts respond to tightening surprises with the "wrong" sign (lower unemployment, higher inflation expected), i.e. surprises carry Delphic content; the rule-based identification is only as good as the assumed reaction function, and the Odyssean part is a residual (Woodford, Swanson in the discussion).
- Calendar-date guidance is mostly read as Delphic; Meyer's survey: 98 of 100 market participants read the FOMC's guidance as a forecast. Campbell et al. suggest bright-line thresholds (Evans's 7/3 rule) to make the commitment state-contingent and limit inflation risk; Woodford objects that a purely forward-looking threshold lacks the history-dependence (make-up for past misses) of the optimal commitment.

| [[monetary_policy/synthesis/zero_bound_alternatives (Bernanke, Reinhart & Sack, 2004)]] | Conditional commitments (BOJ zero-interest-rate policy, FOMC "considerable period") as the first nonstandard lever; surprise path statements move year-ahead expectations and five-year yields in the US, while BOJ statements show no such link |
