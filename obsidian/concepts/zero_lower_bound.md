# Zero Lower Bound (Liquidity Trap)

**Type:** Policy constraint
**Tags:** #zero-lower-bound #zlb #liquidity-trap #deflation #commitment #unconventional-policy

---

## Definition

Short-term nominal interest rates cannot be driven below zero (more generally, below the rate paid on base money), because money offers a zero nominal return. When the rate the central bank would need is negative, the bound binds and the usual instrument is unavailable — Keynes's liquidity trap, "when interest rates have fallen to a level below which they cannot be driven by further monetary expansion" (Eggertsson & Woodford 2003).

## Mechanism

- **When it binds**: in the New Keynesian model, keeping inflation on a target π* requires the nominal rate to equal the natural rate plus π*, so the bound binds when the natural rate of interest falls below –π*. With a zero target, any negative natural rate makes it bind. Expected deflation then keeps the real rate positive even with the nominal rate at zero, which deepens the contraction (Eggertsson & Woodford: a –2 percent natural rate expected to last ten quarters on average gives a 14 percent output gap and 10 percent annual deflation under a zero-inflation target).
- **Why base expansion alone does not relax it**: beyond the satiation level, extra money provides no liquidity services, and with a representative household changes in the composition of assets held by the public do not change state-contingent consumption. Eggertsson & Woodford's irrelevance proposition: open-market operations in any asset leave the set of equilibrium paths unchanged unless they change expected future interest rate policy or the path of total government liabilities.
- **What still works — expectations**: spending depends on the whole expected path of short real rates, so a credible promise to keep the rate at zero after the natural rate recovers, and to let the price level end higher, stimulates demand while the bound binds. The optimal commitment is history-dependent and can be implemented by a gap-adjusted price-level target raised by each shortfall. Forward guidance becomes the main remaining tool (Campbell et al. 2012).

## When it applies / when it fails

- A higher inflation target in normal times guards against the bound (Phelps, Summers, Fischer; Krugman's 4 percent for Japan) but costs distortions at all times; Eggertsson & Woodford find that a constant price-level target or the optimal rule does far better than any strict inflation target (losses 0.0725 and 0.036 against 100 for π* = 0, 24.1 for π* = 1 and 32 for π* = 2).
- Purely forward-looking policy — discretion included — cannot use the expectations channel: discretion at the bound has a deflationary bias. Credibility devices proposed: following the rule before the bound binds, supplying base money in proportion to the target price level, purchases of foreign or real assets, debt-financed tax cuts if the central bank cares about the debt burden.
- A self-fulfilling permanent deflation can also be an equilibrium under a Ricardian fiscal rule; it is excluded by commitments that keep nominal government liabilities from contracting.
- Discussants' objections (Friedman, Gertler, Sims in Eggertsson & Woodford): credibility of the transition is the real problem; with backward-looking inflation a pure price-level target is not optimal; a transitory fiscal expansion can raise the natural rate itself; credit-market frictions make the bound bind more easily.
