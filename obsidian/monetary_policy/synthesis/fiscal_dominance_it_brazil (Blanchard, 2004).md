## Context and motivation

In 2002 the rising probability that Lula would be elected led to "an acute macroeconomic crisis in Brazil": the rate on Brazilian dollar debt rose sharply (a higher perceived probability of default), the Real depreciated sharply and inflation rose. After the October 2002 election, the commitment to a high primary surplus target and the announced pension reform reversed all three — "In many ways, 2003 looked like 2002 in reverse." Despite inflation targeting and rising inflation from mid-2002, the BCB did not raise the real interest rate until the beginning of 2003. The paper asks whether it should have, and answers that it should not: in that environment a higher real rate would probably have been perverse. It places itself in the fiscal dominance literature (Sargent and Wallace's "unpleasant arithmetic"; Woodford's fiscal theory of the price level; Loyo's application to Brazil) and contributes "a specific incarnation", its empirical relevance and its implications for inflation targeting.

## Core argument / thesis

The standard proposition is that a central-bank-engineered rise in the real interest rate makes domestic government debt more attractive and leads to a real appreciation. "If, however, the increase in the real interest rate also increases the probability of default on the debt, the effect may be instead to make domestic government debt less attractive, and to lead to a real depreciation. That outcome is more likely the higher the initial level of debt, the higher the proportion of foreign-currency-denominated debt, and the higher the price of risk." Under that outcome inflation targeting "can clearly have perverse effects": a rate rise in response to inflation causes a real depreciation, which raises inflation further — and "fiscal policy, not monetary policy, is the right instrument to decrease inflation." The estimates say Brazil was in this situation in 2002.

## Key mechanisms / model

A one-period model with three assets: a default-free bond whose real rate r is controlled by the central bank ("the model equivalent of the Selic"), and government bonds in Reals and in dollars, both subject to full default with probability p.

- **Equilibrium returns (1)–(2)**: both risky bonds carry the same risk premium over the riskless rate, governed by market risk aversion θ times p (p proxies for the variance of the return). Default risk raises stated rates twice: to keep the same expected return (the 1 − p term), and, with risk aversion, to compensate for the risk (the θp term).
- **Capital flows and the "capital flow" relation (4)/(6)**: foreign investors choose between Brazilian and U.S. dollar bonds with risk aversion θ∗. A rise in p causes outflows only if foreign investors are more risk averse than the market; this is captured by θ = λθ∗ with λ ≤ 1 (3). With net exports increasing in the real exchange rate ϵ and the future exchange rate tied to the current one (ϵ′ = ϵη, η between zero and one), capital flows plus net exports equal zero. The locus in (p, ϵ) slopes up — more default risk, more depreciation — more steeply the higher θ∗ (Figure 1). For given p, a higher r appreciates (the standard channel).
- **Debt dynamics and the "default risk" relation (5)/(7)**: next-period debt rises with r, with the exchange rate through the dollar share µ of the debt, and with p itself (through 1/(1 − p) and λθ∗p), minus the primary surplus X. p is a cumulative distribution ψ of next-period debt relative to a stochastic threshold. Because p depends on itself non-linearly, there are typically three equilibria; the paper assumes the low one exists and is relevant ("If debt is high enough, there may be no equilibrium except p = 1"). A higher r raises p, more strongly the higher the initial debt; with no dollar debt (µ = 0) p does not depend on the exchange rate.
- **Result (Figure 4a–c)**: "high debt, high risk aversion on the part of foreign investors, or a high proportion of dollar debt can each lead to a depreciation in response to an increase in the interest rate."
- **Measuring p**: the spread on Brazilian dollar debt mixes default probability and risk aversion. Regressing the log spread (C-bond over Treasury) on the U.S. Baa spread — a proxy for θ∗ — and taking the exponential of the constant plus the residual gives a series for p (Table 1). Because risk aversion also raises p, the coefficient is estimated on months with a low Baa spread.

## Main results / findings

- **Table 1 (log spread on Baa spread, monthly)**: OLS over 1995:2 2004:1 gives 0.37 (t 9.5); AR1 0.31 (t 3.6); AR1 excluding months with the Baa spread above 3.0%, 0.16 (t 1.7); above 2.5%, 0.15 (t 0.9). The p series uses 0.16. From early 1999 to early 2002 the spread rose slightly while estimated p fell slightly — the rise is attributed to risk aversion.
- **Capital flow relation (Table 2, 1999:1 to 2004:1)**: log exchange rate on the interest differential and the risk premium pθ∗, nominal and real specifications, OLS / AR1 / IV AR1 (instruments: current and lagged federal funds rate and Baa spread). The risk premium is highly significant in every line (e.g. 12.43, t 13.1, nominal AR1); the interest differential is insignificant throughout and wrong-signed in several lines. Allowing for the expected exchange rate (equation 10, Figure 7) leaves the risk premium consistently positive and the differential "consistently wrong-signed". "Once one controls for the risk premium, it is hard to detect a consistent effect of the differential on the exchange rate" — "as is often the case in the estimation of interest parity conditions".
- **Default risk relation (Table 3, 1999:1 to 2004:1)**: p on current net debt/GDP, on the BCB-survey forecast of debt one year ahead, and on realized debt six months ahead (instrumented with the current and four lagged Baa spreads). IV coefficients are "largely similar across the three measures of debt, and significant" (0.23, t 3.4; 0.23, t 3.8; 0.21, t 3.1); only the IV AR1 with six-months-ahead debt is negative and insignificant. The relation broke down partially in the second half of 2003: debt stabilized but p kept falling, attributed to reforms and a falling dollar share of the debt (net of swaps, from 37% in December 2002 to 21% in January 2004).
- **Putting it together**: using the strongest correctly signed capital-flow estimate (line 2 of Table 2: −0.21 on the differential, 12.43 on θ∗p) and line 6 of Table 3 (0.23), with benchmark values D = 0.53, µ = 0.50, θ∗ = 0.56 (averages for 1999:1 to 2004:1) and λ = 0.50: a 100 basis point rise in the Selic appreciates the exchange rate by 21 basis points directly, depreciates it by 279 basis points through default risk, so the net effect is **a depreciation of 258 basis points**.
- **Sensitivity (Table 4)**: at a debt ratio of 13% the direct and indirect effects roughly cancel; at 63% a one point rise in the rate depreciates by 8.57%; above 70% there is no equilibrium ("This may explain the high volatility in the EMBI and the nervousness of foreign investors in 2002"). With no dollar debt the exchange rate barely moves; at µ = 0.7 the depreciation is 12.11%, and above 0.8 there is no equilibrium. At low risk aversion the exchange rate appreciates by very little; at θ∗ of 0.8 the depreciation is 21.2%, and slightly above 0.8 the equilibrium disappears.
- **Policy in 2002–2003 (Figure 9)**: until September 2002 the BCB let the Selic fall slowly; it raised it in October, November and December, but by less than forecast inflation rose, so the real rate kept falling slightly; in the first half of 2003 the real rate rose from 10% to 18%, then fell back to close to 10%. Possible reasons: the fiscal-dynamics effect explored here, reluctance during the campaign (though the first increase came before the election), and the BCB's own stated reason (it initially expected inflation to turn around faster). By the time the real rate rose in 2003, fiscal austerity "probably dominated any potentially perverse effects".

## Limitations and caveats

- **Author's limits**: one-period model — a dynamic version would make p depend on the whole future path of debt; nominal rigidities are not modeled, so central bank control of the real rate and the pass-through from the real exchange rate to inflation are assumed, not derived; the demand channel of the interest rate is left out. Blanchard speculates it is weak in Brazil (safe real rate consistently above 10%, borrowing rates averaging 30 to 40%) but says this "can only be settled by extending the model". The empirical approximations require elastic capital flows (so the residual is close to log p) and suffer simultaneity, handled by subsamples and by the Baa spread as instrument; he does not estimate the shape of ψ, specifying a linear relation instead.
- **Damaged equations**: the bodies of the real-rate definitions, of (1) and (2), of the spread definition and of (11) are missing — only fragments such as "1 + π" survive; (5), (7), the capital-flow expression after (3) and the next-period debt equation are garbled. Mechanisms are given in words above.
- **Damaged tables and figures**: Tables 1–3 print fewer statistics per row than the DW/ρ/R² headers announce, so those columns cannot be attributed; only coefficients and t-statistics are cited here. Table 4 is flattened into a single column; only the entries the text itself states are cited, the rest (and the "boldfaced" benchmarks) cannot be read. Figures 1–8 are placeholders or axis labels; Figure 9 survives only as series names.
- **Inconsistency in the source**: the text says the coefficient falls "from 0.32 to 0.16", while Table 1 prints 0.31 for the AR1 line. Flagged as written.
- The analysis is of a mechanism, not a characterization of optimal policy, and covers 1999:1 to 2004:1 (the default-risk figure uses 1990:1 to 2004:1).
