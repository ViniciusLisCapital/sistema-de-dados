# Inside the Black Box: The Credit Channel of Monetary Policy Transmission — Bernanke & Gertler 1995

## Context and motivation

Empirical work had confirmed that monetary policy actions are followed by movements in real output "that may last for two years or more", but it treated the transmission mechanism as a "black box". The textbook story — policy moves short rates, which change the cost of capital and so spending on durables, housing, inventories and fixed investment — has gaps: estimated spending equations rarely find a quantitatively important neoclassical cost-of-capital effect (non-neoclassical "accelerator" variables such as lagged output, sales or cash flow dominate), and policy, which acts mainly on short rates, has large effects on purchases of long-lived assets that should respond to real long rates. The paper sets out the authors' view of the credit channel as the content of the black box.

## Core argument / thesis

The credit channel is "an enhancement mechanism, not a truly independent or parallel channel": a set of factors that amplify and propagate conventional interest-rate effects. Its central variable is the **external finance premium** — the difference in cost between funds raised externally (equity or debt) and funds generated internally — which reflects the deadweight costs of the principal-agent problem between lenders and borrowers (evaluation, monitoring and collection costs; the "lemons" premium; the costs of moral hazard and of the covenants and collateral meant to contain it). A policy change that moves open-market rates moves the premium in the same direction, magnifying the effect on the cost of borrowing broadly defined. Two linkages: the **balance sheet channel**, which the authors consider "fairly well established", and the **bank lending channel**, "more controversial" and probably weakened by deregulation. They do not rank the two; they argue for "a credit channel of some type".

## Key mechanisms / model

- **VAR evidence (section "Responses to Policy Shocks")**: monthly VARs, January 1965–December 1993, twelve lags; the federal funds rate (ordered last) is the policy indicator and its innovations are policy shocks. Figure 1 has real GDP, the GDP deflator, commodity prices and the funds rate (monthly GDP and deflator interpolated); Figure 2 splits GDP into final demand and inventory investment; Figure 3 adds spending components one at a time, all normalized by trend GDP (a six-year moving average).
- **Balance sheet (net worth) channel**: the premium falls with the borrower's net worth (liquid assets plus marketable collateral). Tightening weakens balance sheets directly — higher interest expense on short-term and floating-rate debt, and lower asset prices that shrink collateral (the Japanese land and equity crash cited as an example) — and indirectly, as falling customer demand opens a "financing gap" against fixed or quasi-fixed costs, eroding net worth over time. Procyclical balance sheets that amplify and propagate cycles are the "financial accelerator".
- **Bank lending channel**: banks specialize in overcoming informational frictions, so a cut in bank loan supply raises the premium for bank-dependent borrowers. In Bernanke & Blinder (1988) open market sales drain reserves and deposits and limit loans; that needs banks unable to replace lost deposits, true in the US before 1980 (Regulation Q ceilings and disintermediation, onerous reserve requirements, illiquid markets for bank liabilities). Since then, it is enough that the demand for banks' managed liabilities (large CDs) is not perfectly elastic, so a shrinking deposit base raises banks' relative cost of funds.
- **Bank balance sheets**: rising rates that lower the value of securities can impair bank capital and lending capacity (Mexican banks in the peso crisis, the Swedish devaluation).

## Main results / findings

**Four facts on the response to a tightening (Figures 1–3).**
1. Interest-rate effects are transitory but real GDP and the price level fall in a sustained way: GDP starts falling about four months after the shock and bottoms out about two years after it; prices are inert for about a year; the funds rate falls back after three to four months, is a quarter or less of the initial shock after nine to 12 months and essentially back to trend at two years (the text also says "virtually back to trend at eight to nine months").
2. Final demand falls first; production follows with a lag, so inventories build up for several months before falling, and inventory disinvestment accounts for a large part of the fall in GDP.
3. Residential investment falls earliest and most sharply, with consumer durables and nondurables close behind.
4. Business fixed investment falls with a longer lag — the bulk between six and 24 months after the shock — almost all of it equipment; business structures barely respond.

**Puzzles for the cost-of-capital view.** Magnitude (large real effects from small rate moves), timing (inventories and investment fall after the rate spike is over), and composition (long-lived housing responds most, long-lived business structures hardly at all).

**Balance sheet evidence.** Figure 4: the coverage ratio (interest payments over interest plus profits, the inverse of the usual definition) tracks the funds rate closely. Figure 5 (quarterly VAR, 1965:I to 1994:II, four lags, following Galati 1994): "something over 40 percent of the short-run decline in corporate profits is the result of higher interest payments"; income falls faster than costs; the cash squeeze peaks six to nine months after the tightening, when output, inventories and investment start to fall, and interest expenses stay high as short-term debt rises. Gertler & Gilchrist (1993, 1994): large firms borrow short-term and build inventories after a tightening, small firms (assets under $250 million in 1991 dollars, about 30 percent of manufacturing sales) cannot borrow and cut inventories and production; the gap persists after controlling for sales growth, appears mainly in recessions and tight-money periods, and survives industry dummies and financial (rather than size) splits.

**Bank lending evidence (Figure 6).** In tight-money periods the prime–bill spread widens, NFIB small-business credit terms tighten, and CD rates rise by more than bill rates (a spike in 1981, after reserve requirements on CDs had ended), consistent with imperfectly elastic demand for managed liabilities. But the same patterns fit the balance sheet channel; the two cannot be separated conclusively.

**Credit aggregates are the wrong test.** Credit is "not a primitive driving force" but an endogenous factor; credit conditions are "best measured ... by the external finance premium and not the aggregate quantity of credit". Credit demand has a countercyclical component (financing inventory buildups, smoothing income shortfalls), so aggregate credit can rise while the premium worsens; the theory predicts only that agents borrow and spend less than with perfect credit markets. Even no marginal predictive power of credit aggregates is "not necessarily inconsistent" with an important credit channel.

**Households.** The bank lending channel hit housing through disintermediation under Regulation Q; the balance sheet channel works through down payments, closing costs, points and income-to-payment ratios. Boldin's (1994) "mortgage burden" (mortgage payments over the median new buyer's income) influences housing demand after controls and correlates positively with the funds rate (Figure 7), helping explain housing's strong response despite weak policy control of real long rates.

## Limitations and caveats

- **Figures 1–7 survive only as titles and stray labels** (Figure 5 is a line of scattered characters with two axis values); the response paths are known only from the text. The authors note they do not show standard error bands.
- OCR damage in the prose: the first statement of the central term reads "external finan@ jn-ernium"; footnote markers are garbled or fused into the text (e.g. "Figure 3)."nventories", "'%ote"); the Jorgensonian cost-of-capital formula in footnote 1 is split over lines; Figure 4's legend reads "Furids kite". The meaning is recoverable from context in each case.
- Two timing statements for the funds rate's return to trend coexist in the text (eight to nine months; essentially back at two years), reported as written.
- Authors' caveats: more work is needed to establish the quantitative significance of the credit channel and the relative weight of its two mechanisms; the bank lending channel is hard to measure and has probably weakened; household balance sheets deserve the attention given to firms'; the effect of adjustable-rate mortgages is unexplored.
