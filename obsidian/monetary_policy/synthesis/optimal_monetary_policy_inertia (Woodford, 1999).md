## Context and motivation

Central banks — the Fed most of all — move their policy rate in a series of small steps in the same direction rather than at once. Estimated reaction functions capture this as partial adjustment of the funds rate toward a Taylor-type target (1.1)–(1.2): Sack (1998b) estimates an inertia coefficient θ of .63 for the Greenspan Fed, "large and highly significant"; Rudebusch (1995) shows that over 1974-1979 and a later sample a target change is much more likely to be followed by another in the same direction (Goodhart 1996 finds the same for other central banks). The common reading is that inertia shows central banks are too slow, and that a "smoothing" objective is a rationalization with no place in the social objective. Under the dynamic-programming logic of engineering control, optimal policy should depend only on the current state, so any persistence in rates should come only from persistent shocks. Woodford argues this misreads the problem when private behavior is forward-looking.

## Core argument / thesis

With forward-looking private agents, outcomes depend on expected future policy, so the best policy is a **commitment** whose current actions depend on past conditions — on the commitments made earlier — and the dynamic-programming principle fails. Formalizing Goodfriend's (1991) insight: output and prices respond to long rates, which reflect expected future short rates, so **small but persistent** changes in the short rate move long rates and demand as much as large transitory ones, for a given degree of short-rate variability. The optimal plan therefore displays **intrinsic inertia** in interest rates even when shocks are serially uncorrelated. It can be implemented either by delegating policy to a discretionary central bank whose loss function includes an interest-rate **smoothing** term (absent from the true social loss), or by committing to an instrument rule in which the funds rate depends on its own lag — possibly with a coefficient above one ("super-inertial"). Evidence of inertia is not "prima facie evidence of incompetence".

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Model (Section 2)**: the standard forward-looking pair — an intertemporal IS equation (2.1), which integrated forward makes demand depend on expected long real rates (2.3), and a New Keynesian aggregate supply equation (2.2) derived from Calvo pricing (2.4)–(2.5), with empirical support from Roberts, Sbordone and Galí–Gertler. In output-gap form (2.6)–(2.7), the only disturbance is the **natural rate of interest**, and the policy rate matters only through the "interest-rate gap" between it and the natural rate. No lagged endogenous variable enters the structure, so any inertia comes from policy.
- **Loss function (2.8)–(2.9)**: squared inflation, a weighted squared output gap (around a possibly positive target) and a weighted squared deviation of the **level** of the interest rate — not of its change. The level term is a quadratic approximation to the zero-bound constraint: following Rotemberg and Woodford, the mean funds rate must be at least k = 2.26 standard deviations above zero, equivalently the average squared rate at most K = 1.44 times the squared average (2.11), whose Lagrange multipliers give the weight and target of the interest-rate term. The loss is a second-order approximation to household utility (Woodford 1999b, not in the file).
- **Optimal commitment (3.1)**: first-order conditions (3.2)–(3.4) with lagged Lagrange multipliers and initial conditions (3.5). The plan is time-inconsistent whenever there is an output target above the natural rate (the Barro–Gordon case) **or** any variation in the natural rate — so commitment is valuable even with no average inflation bias.
- **Discretion (3.2)**: the Markov-perfect equilibrium (3.6)–(3.8) makes rates a function of the current natural rate only (3.16); the policy rate moves in the direction of the natural rate but by less, and inherits only the shocks' own persistence — the "conventional view" holds only here. Its steady-state inflation (3.9) exceeds the optimum: an inflation bias.
- **Optimal non-inertial plan (3.3)**: commitment restricted to responses to the current state (3.19)–(3.25); it coincides with discretion when the natural rate is serially uncorrelated.
- **Inertia under the optimal plan (3.4)**: the optimal steady-state inflation rate (3.26) is independent of the output target, so with a zero interest-rate target it is zero despite an inefficient natural rate and a long-run Phillips curve tradeoff. Deviations follow (3.27)–(3.30): the policy rate depends on the lagged multipliers, a source of inertia independent of the shock process, and obeys (3.32) — a second-order autoregression even for white-noise shocks, with roots equal to the stable eigenvalues of the system.
- **Limiting case κ → 0 (3.5)**: the optimal rate follows exactly the partial-adjustment form (1.2), with inertia coefficient equal to the stable root of (3.34) and a target that is a moving average of current and expected natural rates (3.35)–(3.37), elasticity between 1 − β and one.
- **Delegation (Section 4)**: a discretionary central bank minimizing (4.1), which adds a squared **change** in the rate and lets the weights and targets differ from society's, yields partial adjustment with 0 < rr < 1 whenever the smoothing weight is positive (4.11). In the limiting case the optimal plan is exactly implemented with a positive smoothing weight (4.14) and a **negative** weight on the interest-rate level (4.15). Average inflation bias can be removed separately, e.g. by giving the banker a zero output-gap target (as in Blinder's proposal).
- **Feedback rules (Section 5)**: a rule making the rate a function of exogenous states only — including the optimal law of motion (3.32) — leaves the equilibrium indeterminate (5.1)–(5.2), as in Sargent and Wallace. Within the family (5.3) with lagged rate, inflation, output gap and natural rate, determinacy holds if all coefficients are non-negative and the inflation coefficient plus the lag coefficient exceed one (5.5) — the Taylor principle stated for an inertial rule. Requiring no observation of the shocks selects the rule (5.8)–(5.9), responding only to inflation and the lagged rate, with a lag coefficient larger than the optimal inertia of the rate itself, because higher past rates mean that a given inflation reading signals more underlying pressure. Section 5.3 derives a rule of the form (5.10)/(5.14), with second-order lags in the rate and first-order in inflation, that implements the optimal plan exactly and is determinate for the calibrated parameters.

## Main results / findings

- **Calibration (Table 1)**: β .99, σ .157, κ .024, ρ .35, sd of the natural rate 3.72, λx .048, λr .236, matched to Rotemberg and Woodford (interest-rate variability 1.921 against their 1.928; shadow value of variability .236 against their .224).
- **Inertia of the optimal plan**: the eigenvalues of N are about .65 and .46, so rates take several quarters to return to normal even after a purely transitory shock. A one-quarter, one-point rise in the natural rate (Figure 1, ρ = 0) raises the optimal rate by about 24 basis points on impact, 11 the next quarter and 5 two quarters later. The anticipated later tightening means inflation barely rises on impact and then falls below normal (Figure 2). Approximating by partial adjustment, the target elasticity is .44, .52 and .75 for ρ = 0, .35 and .9, with inertia θ = .46 in each case — about half the gap closed within the quarter.
- **Gains from commitment (Table 2)**: with ρ = 0, inflation variability is nearly 75 percent larger and interest-rate variance more than twice as large under discretion, and E[W] 60 percent higher (1.244 against .774). With ρ = .35, inflation variance is nearly four times larger and E[W] two-and-a-third times (2.547 against 1.097); the optimal non-inertial plan (2.279) lowers inflation variability but raises rate variability to 6.720. With ρ = .9 discretion falls into a "discretion trap" — the rate rises more than the natural rate and the loss is more than 230 times the optimum (526.3 against 2.228) — while the optimal plan raises real rates through low expected inflation (by 70 basis points in the first year), not high nominal rates.
- **Optimal delegation (Table 3, Figures 7–12)**: charging a discretionary bank with the true social loss gives E[W] = 2.547; the best weights without smoothing (λ̂r = .120) reproduce the optimal non-inertial plan (2.279); with non-negative weights, a smoothing weight of .282 gives 1.337; unconstrained, λ̂r = -.439 and a smoothing weight of .807 reach 1.097 — "more than 99.9 percent" of the gain from optimal commitment. With non-negative weights in form (4.16) it is best to put no weight on inflation and a smoothing weight of 5.51 (E[W] 1.281). For λ̂r below about -0.02 the stationary equilibrium is no longer the minimum-state-variable one; for very negative λ̂r there are multiple discretionary equilibria.
- **Super-inertial rules**: within the family (5.8), the loss-minimizing coefficients for the calibration are an inflation response of 46.1 and a lag coefficient of 13.0; for any ρ the optimal rule has a lag coefficient above one. The exact optimal rule (5.14) has A(L) = 1−12.9L+8.3L² = (1−12.2L)(1−.68L) and B(L) = 42.6(1−.65L), close to a rule with coefficients 42.6 and 12.2. In Rotemberg and Woodford's (1998) richer model the largest root is 1.33. Such a rule does not make rates explode in equilibrium: the expectation of it makes inflation undershoot after a shock, so rates return to normal, and the rule can be truncated outside the equilibrium range.
- **Reading of the evidence**: estimated Fed inertia (Sack's) is unlikely to exceed what an optimal commitment justifies, and "it is possible that ... current U.S. policy is not even inertial enough".

## Limitations and caveats

- **Author's caveats**: the model is "too simple to provide the basis for a realistic quantitative policy analysis"; the optimal numerical coefficients are "quite sensitive" to small changes in specification and to κ and σ, because inflation responds little to natural-rate shocks in a near-optimal equilibrium and is a weak indicator that must be amplified. With noisy real-time inflation data and other indicators (the output gap), the optimal lag coefficient would likely be considerably smaller, though still above the inertia of the rate itself. Credibility and learning about the rule are outside the paper; in backward-looking models super-inertial rules can give infinite variances (Rudebusch and Svensson 1998), which Woodford attributes to applying the rule without bounds.
- Section 5.3 introduces the exact rule with "the optimal rule (5.8) takes the form", although the polynomials given are those of the rule (5.10)/(5.14); the coefficients 42.6 and 12.2 then approximate (5.8). The text is reported as printed.
- Footnote markers are fused to some numbers in the text (Sack's standard error, the end year of Rudebusch's second sample), so those values are not reported here.
- **Figures 1–16 survive only as titles and labels**; the impulse-response magnitudes cited above are the text's own. The headings of the first two sections lost their numbers in the extraction. The welfare derivation (Woodford 1999b) and Rotemberg and Woodford's model are referred to, not in the file, and there is **no reference list** in the file.
- Linear-quadratic approximation: results are valid for small shocks around a steady state; the ρ = .9 discretion results involve volatility large enough that the author doubts the approximation's accuracy (footnote 58), while asserting the qualitative conclusion.
