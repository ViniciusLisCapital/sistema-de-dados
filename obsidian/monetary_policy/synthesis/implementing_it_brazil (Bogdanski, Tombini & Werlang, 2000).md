## Context and motivation

Written by Central Bank of Brazil staff a year after the fact, the paper explains why and how Brazil adopted inflation targeting in mid-1999, less than six months after the Real was forced to float on January 15, 1999. The crawling peg had been the nominal anchor of the 1994 stabilization; its collapse, after the Russian moratorium and a confidence crisis fed by a postponed fiscal adjustment, left monetary policy with no guidance, the exchange rate overshooting and inflation rising. The new Board (in office from March 4) had to calm markets and propose a new anchor at the same time, with no formal instrument independence, no research department and few staff who knew what an IT framework was. The paper records the institutional design, the operational framework and, mainly, the family of small structural models used to inform Copom decisions, and closes with lessons from the first phase.

## Core argument / thesis

Under a floating exchange rate the economy needs a new nominal anchor, and inflation targeting — with the target itself as the anchor, supported by fiscal austerity and a firm public-sector wage policy — is the framework best suited to it. Because policy acts with lags, what an inflation-targeting central bank actually does is "inflation forecast targeting": decisions are based on conditional forecasts of inflation under alternative interest-rate paths. That requires a small, transparent model of the transmission mechanism, used to discipline judgment rather than replace it, and full disclosure of the forecasts and their assumptions so that credibility is earned and the targets are met "with lesser costs".

## Key mechanisms / model

**Institutional design (Decree No. 3088, June 21, 1999).** Targets on a widely known price index, set by the National Monetary Council on the Finance Minister's proposal, two years in advance; the Central Bank is responsible for meeting them; success is judged on calendar-year accumulated inflation within a tolerance interval; a breach requires an open letter from the Governor to the Finance Minister (causes, measures, time to return); a quarterly Inflation Report. The IPCA was chosen, with a declining path of targets for 1999–2001 and a band of ±2% each year. Three design choices are argued, not just reported:
- **Declining targets, no gradualism.** The 1999 inflation was a once-and-for-all relative-price realignment after the devaluation, not an inflationary process (Brazil had price stability in 1998), so inflation should return quickly to its prior level and a gradualist disinflation was not recommended.
- **Headline, not core.** Purging items would be technically better, but after past price-index manipulations a headline index was "essential for credibility reasons", at least at the start.
- **No escape clauses.** Headline targeting plus no escape clauses justify the relatively wide band and make the targets "much tighter than they may initially appear".

**Transmission as the staff saw it (section 2.2).** Two channels carry policy to prices: an aggregate-demand channel (the interest rate affects durables and investment in 3 to 6 months, the output gap takes about 3 more months to affect inflation, 6 to 9 months in all) and a direct exchange-rate channel (the nominal interest rate moves the nominal exchange rate contemporaneously, which moves inflation contemporaneously through imported inflation). The credit channel had been negligible, given low corporate leverage and the tight policies of the Real plan, though the authors expect it to matter as bank spreads fall.

**The small structural model (section 3).** Four blocks, solved together with a chosen expectations mechanism, pass-through specification and policy rule:
- **IS curve** — output gap on its own lags and the real interest rate; a "fiscal" variant adds the primary public-sector borrowing requirement as a second, Treasury-controlled, instrument. The real exchange rate was not significant, attributed to the managed-rate sample and the small weight of net exports. The output gap comes from a linear trend or an HP filter (similar results).
- **Long-run calibration of the IS** — the estimation sample (1992:I to 1999:III) is dominated by the peg era, when rates were set to defend the parity and the equilibrium real rate was "necessarily high". Since the new regime should have a different equilibrium real rate, the IS is calibrated so that in steady state (constant debt/GDP, zero primary surplus, zero gap) the equilibrium real rate equals potential growth, imposed as a restriction on the ratio of the constant to the fiscal coefficient.
- **Phillips curve** — inflation on its own lags and/or expected inflation, the output gap and the change in import prices (foreign PPI plus the exchange rate), with the coefficients constrained so the curve is vertical in the long run. Backward-looking (adaptive expectations, fits past data, vulnerable to the Lucas critique after the regime change), forward-looking (assumes expectations converge to target as credibility grows, but needs measures of expectations), and their average.
- **Pass-through** — four alternatives: a constant coefficient, a quadratic in the exchange-rate change, a coefficient depending on the exchange-rate level, and a quadratic in the level (from a partial-equilibrium model where devaluation shifts the supply curve of tradables producers). The non-linear forms aim at the effects of a temporary overshooting.
- **Exchange rate by UIP with a risk premium** — taking first differences and assuming the change in expectations is white noise, the change in the exchange rate follows the changes in the domestic rate, the foreign rate and the risk premium. The foreign rate is projected from futures markets; the risk premium (sovereign spread over Treasuries) is the volatile, hard-to-anticipate exogenous variable. It is either set by Copom judgment or made endogenous, responding to the fiscal stance (the primary PSBR) and to other factors (international liquidity and rates, foreign markets, commodity prices, current account prospects, rating).
- **Policy rules** — an exogenous path (including the constant-rate path used in the Inflation Report fan chart, from which the direction of the needed policy change is read), Taylor-type rules with optional smoothing, forward-looking rules on expected inflation, and optimal deterministic or stochastic rules from a quadratic loss in inflation deviations, the output gap and rate changes.

**What carries the transmission in the model.** The interest rate reaches inflation fast through the exchange rate (contemporaneous) and slowly through the output gap (lagged). The fiscal stance enters twice: in demand and, through the risk premium, in the exchange rate. Relaxing the UIP block or the pass-through specification would change how much of the policy effect arrives at once.

## Main results / findings

The paper is a design document with no estimation tables; what it reports are modeling choices and their reasons.
- **The forward-looking weight was too high to believe.** Estimated Phillips curves gave a weighted average of past and future inflation "with at least 60% on the forward-looking component". Staff and Copom rejected this: it implied more credibility than expected so early, did not match the market surveys, and produced dynamics with "almost no inertia". The averaged specification was adopted precisely to force inflation persistence while keeping a forward-looking component expected to grow in importance.
- **Non-linear pass-through did not beat the linear one.** With few quarterly observations the non-linear variants gave results "very close to the linear variant", consistent with international evidence that pass-through is inversely related to the real appreciation just before the devaluation.
- **The 1999 episode as the first test of the framework.** On March 4 the Copom raised the Selic from 39% to 45% p.a. with a downward bias, explaining that observed inflation came from the depreciation and that the rate had to be high enough to offset exchange-based pressures; the bias was used twice to cut as the overshooting reversed and inflation fell. The 1999 target was met, though the authors say it is too early to judge the regime.
- **Transparency in practice**: monthly Copom meetings, immediate announcement, minutes released after two weeks and then one week (versus three months before), a quarterly Inflation Report; the Bank chose the Bank of England's "full disclosure strategy".

What would contradict the framework's premises: evidence of an ongoing inflationary process rather than a one-off realignment (the case for declining targets), a credit channel that becomes important (the authors already anticipate it), or a risk premium that dominates the interest-rate effect on the exchange rate.

## Limitations and caveats

- **The authors' own limits**: the estimation sample is dominated by the managed-exchange-rate regime, so parameters (including the equilibrium real rate and the insignificant real exchange rate) may not hold after the regime change — hence calibration and judgment; the backward-looking curve is exposed to the Lucas critique; the risk premium is largely unforecastable; the stochastic optimization is too slow to run during a Copom meeting; optimized rule coefficients "can frequently be very unintuitive"; model forecasts must be combined with market surveys, asset-price information and judgment. The authors stress that the staff must carefully identify the form, intensity and timing of shocks, and how much agents already anticipated them.
- **What the text shows**: no coefficients, standard errors or simulation results are reported, so the model can be described but not checked. The claims about transmission lags (3 to 6 and 6 to 9 months) and the negligible credit channel are stated, not estimated in the paper.
- **Damaged passages**: every displayed equation — (I) and (II) (IS), (III)–(V) (backward, forward and combined Phillips curves), the four pass-through specifications, (VI)–(VIII) (UIP, exchange-rate dynamics, risk premium), the long-run calibration restriction, (IX) (Taylor rule with smoothing) and (X)–(XI) (loss functions) — is reduced to a vertical list of symbols and cannot be read; the descriptions above come from the surrounding text. The text introducing the Phillips curve is cut ("Three variants are" … "output gap one, are constrained to sum to unity"), so which coefficients are constrained is not fully legible. Figure 1 (the model diagram) survives only as box labels.
