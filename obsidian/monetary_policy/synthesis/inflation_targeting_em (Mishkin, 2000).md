## Context and motivation

After Latin American and East Asian countries with pegged exchange rates fell into deep financial crises in the 1990s, emerging market economies (transition countries included) searched for alternative nominal anchors. Inflation targeting, used successfully by industrialized countries, was adopted by Chile, Brazil, the Czech Republic, Poland and South Africa. The paper — a short proceedings piece — outlines what inflation targeting involves for these countries and weighs its advantages and disadvantages there.

## Core argument / thesis

"Although inflation targeting is not a panacea and may not be appropriate for many emerging market countries, it can be a highly useful monetary policy strategy in a number of them." It is much more than announcing a numerical target: it requires institutional commitment, transparency and accountability, and in emerging markets it depends on prerequisites — no fiscal dominance, a sound financial system, and an exchange rate policy that is not ignored but is clearly subordinated to the inflation objective.

## Key mechanisms / model

No formal model; the argument is institutional and draws on country experience.

- **Five elements (Section I)**: public announcement of medium-term numerical inflation targets; institutional commitment to price stability as the primary goal; an information-inclusive strategy (many variables, not only money or the exchange rate); transparency through communication; accountability for the inflation objective. Many emerging markets routinely announced annual inflation targets in government plans without the other four elements, which is not inflation targeting.
- **Advantages (Section II)**: unlike a peg, policy can respond to domestic shocks; unlike monetary targeting, it does not need a stable money–inflation relation; it is easily understood. A numerical target raises accountability and can reduce the risk of the time-inconsistency trap by focusing political debate on what the central bank can do in the long run (control inflation) rather than what it cannot (raise growth, lower unemployment, improve competitiveness). This needs legislated independence — a board insulated from politicians (government members excluded, long terms, protection from arbitrary dismissal) and full control of the instruments — and a mandate putting price stability first. Inflation-Report-type documents (originated by the Bank of England) and frequent communication are central.
- **Disadvantages (Section III)**: of seven criticisms, four (too rigid, too much discretion, more output instability, lower growth) are not serious for a properly designed regime — "constrained discretion". Three matter in emerging markets:
  - *Weak accountability from poor controllability*: inflation is hard to control and observed with long lags; when disinflating from high levels, forecast errors are large and targets missed, so the regime is better phased in after some successful disinflation (Masson, Savastano and Sharma 1997). Government-controlled prices in the index call for fiscal–monetary coordination or their exclusion from the target index (as in the Czech Republic).
  - *Fiscal dominance*: large deficits eventually force monetization or a large devaluation and high inflation, so the absence of outright fiscal dominance is "a key prerequisite"; a sound financial system is another. Both are equally needed by a currency board or full dollarization, and involving the government in setting the target can help constrain fiscal policy.
  - *Partial dollarization*: inflation targeting requires exchange rate flexibility, but with dollarized balance sheets and dollar long-term debt, large abrupt depreciations can wreck balance sheets and trigger a financial crisis. The exchange rate cannot be ignored, but its role must be subordinated to inflation, and the regime may not be viable without stringent prudential regulation and supervision.

## Main results / findings

- **Chile**: the earliest emerging-market targeter, announcing in 1990 (with inflation above 20%) an objective each September for the twelve months to December of the next year. Prerequisites were in place: central bank legislation of 1989 (effective 1990) giving independence and price stability as a primary objective; fiscal surpluses every year from 1991 to 1997; bank regulation and supervision comparable to industrialized countries after the early-1980s banking crisis. The objective was phased in from "official inflation projections" to hard targets; targets fell gradually from over 20% for 1991 to 3.5% by the end of the decade; ranges were replaced by point targets from the September 1994 announcement for 1995. Inflation fell to around 3% while growth averaged more than 8% per year from 1991 to 1997.
- **Chile's 1998 mistake**: facing a large negative terms-of-trade shock, the central bank raised rates and narrowed the exchange rate band instead of easing and letting the peso depreciate; the target was undershot and growth fell to 3.4% in 1998 and a -2.9% rate in the first half of 1999 — the first recession in more than 15 years — and policy was reversed in 1999. Lesson: undershooting a target is "just as costly as overshooting", and support for an independent central bank erodes if it is seen to focus solely on lowering inflation.
- **Chile is not yet full-fledged**: no Inflation Report, no published forecasts, weak accountability.
- **Brazil**: adopted inflation targeting after its early-1999 currency crisis and, within four months of the announcement by the new central bank president, had a regime with all the "bells and whistles", including an Inflation Report with published forecasts. Inflation stayed below 10% despite a substantial depreciation, but it is "by no means clear whether Brazil can solve its deep-rooted fiscal problems".
- **Exchange rate**: emerging markets are right not to adopt "benign neglect", but have gone "too far for too long" in limiting flexibility through bands and frequent intervention, which risks making the exchange rate the de facto nominal anchor over the inflation target. Suggested remedy: a transparent policy of smoothing short-run fluctuations while letting the rate reach its market level over longer horizons.

## Limitations and caveats

- A short proceedings paper: the claims about the four "not serious" criticisms are referred to Mishkin (1999) and Bernanke et al. (1999), and the Latin American detail to Mishkin and Savastano (1999), none of which are in the file. There is no reference list in the file.
- No econometric evidence: the Chilean and Brazilian assessments are narrative, and the Brazilian one is dated to the regime's first months (paper of March 2000).
- Footnote 3 notes that Chile's controls on short-term capital flows are often cited as a factor in its success but judges rigorous prudential supervision "probably more important"; the capital-controls debate is not developed.
- The text is clean; the NBER copyright line on the cover page is cut ("may be quoted without explicit permission..." with its first half missing).
