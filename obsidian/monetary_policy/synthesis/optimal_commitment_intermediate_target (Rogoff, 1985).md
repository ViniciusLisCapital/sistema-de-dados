## Context and motivation

Even a perfectly benevolent central bank may have an inflation rate that is "systematically too high" (Phelps 1967, Kydland and Prescott 1977, Barro and Gordon 1983): when a distortion such as income taxation keeps market employment below the social optimum, wage setters rationally fear the central bank will exploit short-term nominal rigidities to raise employment, and only high wage inflation deters it. The paper studies institutional responses to this time-consistency problem — appointing a "conservative" to head the central bank, or giving it concrete incentives to hit an intermediate monetary target — in a framework different from conventional analyses where the bank is rigidly bound to a feedback rule.

## Core argument / thesis

"Society can sometimes make itself better off by appointing a central banker who does not share the social objective function, but instead places 'too large' a weight on inflation-rate stabilization relative to employment stabilization." This lowers the time-consistent inflation rate but distorts the response to supply shocks, raising the variance of employment. By an envelope-theorem argument the ideal agent places "a large, but finite, weight on inflation". The same logic covers intermediate targets (inflation, nominal GNP, money supply): flexible commitment beats both full discretion and rigid targeting, and "it is almost never optimal to constrain the monetary authorities to hit their target exactly." Flexible targeting is a "third-best" solution: first best removes the labor-market distortion; second best is a constitutional state-contingent money supply rule, which is hard to design and to change when outmoded.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Model (Section II)**: a stochastic rational-expectations IS-LM model. Aggregate supply from Cobb-Douglas firms with a productivity shock z; nominal wages set one period ahead and not fully indexed (Gray 1976), so wage setters set the wage at the expected price level; employment and output deviate with the price surprise and the productivity shock (equations (1)–(7)). "Output and employment stabilization are not equivalent to price prediction error minimization in the presence of a productivity shock." Aggregate demand falls with the real interest rate, with a demand shock u (8); money demand falls with the nominal rate and rises with output, with a portfolio shock v (9) (demand side as in Canzoneri, Henderson and Rogoff 1983). The shocks are independent and serially uncorrelated.
- **Social loss (10)**: quadratic in employment's deviation from a socially optimal level (above the market level by a constant, because of a labor-leisure distortion, as in Barro and Gordon) and in inflation's deviation from a socially desired trend rate, with relative weight on inflation.
- **Discretion (Section III)**: wage setters set wage inflation high enough that the bank's marginal gain from raising employment is offset by the marginal cost of more inflation (13): the time-consistent inflation is too high when the optimal employment exceeds the natural level. The bank fully offsets demand and money-demand shocks (u and v do not enter the price surprise (15)) but lets supply shocks move prices only partly. Expected loss (14) splits into a distortion term no regime can reduce, a mean-inflation term, and a stabilization term.
- **Conservative central banker (Section IV)**: the appointed agent minimizes (17), the social loss with the inflation weight raised by a parameter ε. Equations (18)–(20) give mean inflation, the price response to supply shocks and the stabilization loss as functions of ε; the regime is judged by the social loss (21).
- **Theorem**: with a labor-market distortion, the loss-minimizing ε is strictly positive and finite (proof via (22a)–(22c): the inflation term falls with ε throughout; the stabilization term is minimized at ε = 0). At ε = 0 the bank already stabilizes optimally, so raising ε gives a first-order inflation gain at a second-order stabilization cost; as ε grows, inflation approaches the target and the marginal inflation gain becomes small relative to the stabilization cost. Without a distortion, the best agent shares society's loss.
- **Intermediate targets (Section V)**: rewards and punishments that put direct weight on a nominal variable — tying remuneration or budget to hitting the target, bonuses, a nominal income, public justification of misses, effects on the bank's independence — all reduce time-consistent inflation. Section IV is formally **inflation-rate (price-level) targeting**; nominal GNP targeting (24)–(28), money supply targeting (29)–(31) and nominal interest rate targeting (32)–(34) are analyzed the same way.

## Main results / findings

- **Inflation-rate targeting**: with no productivity shocks there is no trade-off, and ε should be as large as possible; with important supply shocks, other targets may lower mean inflation at a lower stabilization cost.
- **Nominal GNP targeting**: the optimal weight is positive and finite; it responds to demand shocks exactly as discretion does (stabilize the price level), differently to supply shocks. With wages indexed to the price surprise there is a special configuration where fixing nominal GNP is what the unconstrained bank would do. If the target is set at the socially optimal rather than the natural employment level (politically easier), the target has an **inflationary bias** that does not vanish as its weight grows.
- **Money supply targeting**: positive, finite optimal weight except in a special case with no aggregate demand shocks, where an infinite weight — "equivalent to a k percent rule" — lowers mean inflation to the social optimum at no stabilization cost. Because money demand is interest-elastic, setting the target on the assumption that next period's inflation will be at the socially desired rate overestimates real money demand and raises time-consistent inflation (a problem that "does not arise under a permanent k percent rule").
- **Nominal interest rate targeting is counterproductive**: committing to a low nominal rate raises expected inflation and the expected nominal rate, because once wages are set the bank can lower rates through money growth; "The central bank has to target high interest rates if it wants low interest rates." Given expected inflation, a nominal-rate target is a real-rate target; the interest rate is "not ... a suitable instrument for precommitment", though it can still be an information variable (Poole 1970).
- **Comparing regimes (Section VI)**: rigid regimes rank by conventional stabilization criteria (Appendix, (A1)–(A4)); optimally flexible regimes have no closed form and their ranking need not match the rigid ranking, so "it can be misleading to analyze separately the stabilization and credibility problems of the central bank."
- **Institutions (Sections V and VII)**: the model explains independent central banks, governors chosen "from conservative elements of the financial community", and financing the bank independently of seigniorage. Reputation can ameliorate but, except in special cases, not eliminate the credibility problem, so the result should extend to a dynamic setting.

## Limitations and caveats

- **Equations are badly damaged by OCR** throughout (1)–(34) and (A1)–(A4): Greek letters, hats and bars are lost or misread (the inflation-weight parameter ε prints as "E" or "e", the optimal employment level with a hat prints as "h", "h'" or "&"; parts of (5), (11)–(12), (22a)–(22c), (23), (25)–(28), (30)–(31), (33)–(34) and the Appendix are fragments). The conditions in footnotes 14 and 15 for an infinite optimal weight are unreadable. The note describes the model in words and does not restore any equation.
- The comparison that drives the interest-rate result ("A comparison of equations (13) and (33) reveals that ...") is garbled at its conclusion; the meaning follows from the next sentences.
- **No reference list in the file**: bracketed citations (Gray [1976], Canzoneri et al. [1983], Poole [1970], Backus and Driffill [1985]) cannot be resolved from the clean_md. The JSTOR cover page precedes the text.
- Author's scope: the model abstracts from long lags in transmission; preferences of the appointee are assumed known with certainty; the game-theoretic structure is one period, without reputation; complete contemporaneous information is assumed (an earlier version's appendix on incomplete information and the numerical rank-reversal examples are "available on request", not in the file); no closed-form solution for the optimal weights.
