## Context and motivation

A survey of inflation targeting twenty years after New Zealand introduced it in 1990, written by a member of the Riksbank's executive board. By 2010 about 25 industrialized and emerging-market economies had adopted it (about 10 industrialized and 15 emerging-market and developing countries). The chapter covers its history and macroeconomic effects (Section 2), a theory of inflation targeting as forecast targeting (Section 3), practice — the development of the regime, publishing policy-rate paths, the Riksbank, Norges Bank, preconditions in emerging markets (Section 4) — and the future: price-level targeting and the lessons of the financial crisis (Section 5).

## Core argument / thesis

Inflation targeting is characterized by (1) an announced numerical inflation target, (2) an implementation that gives a major role to an inflation forecast — **forecast targeting** — and (3) a high degree of transparency and accountability. In practice it is never "strict" but always **flexible**: central banks also stabilize resource utilization, with objectives well approximated by a quadratic loss in the inflation gap and the output gap (possibly plus policy-rate changes). Its theory is therefore a theory of optimal policy with one twist: the object of choice at each decision is not a policy function chosen once and for all but a **policy-rate path**, picked so that the conditional forecasts of inflation and resource utilization "look good". The regime has been a success in stabilizing both inflation and the real economy, and flexible inflation targeting that uses all financial information relevant to the forecasts remains the best policy after the financial crisis — but financial stability needs its own policy and instruments.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Targets and accountability (Section 1)**: advanced-country targets are around 2 percent for CPI or core CPI, as a range (1-3 percent in New Zealand), a point with a tolerance interval (2 percent ±1 percentage points in Canada) or a point (2 percent in Sweden and the U.K., 2.5 percent in Norway); the form "does not seem to matter in practice", and range edges are "soft edges". Emerging-market targets are a few points higher. Transparency rests on the view that monetary policy is largely the "management of expectations"; credibility — private inflation expectations close to the target — gives room to be flexible. Accountability devices: New Zealand's Policy Target Agreement, the U.K. letter for deviations larger than one percentage point, parliamentary hearings, external reviews.
- **Linear-quadratic setup (3.1)**: a state-space model with predetermined and forward-looking variables (3.1)–(3.4), with a standard New Keynesian example (3.5)–(3.9); target variables as linear functions of the state (3.10); an intertemporal quadratic loss (3.11)–(3.13). Optimization under commitment in a timeless perspective gives the optimal instrument rule (3.17); certainty equivalence holds. No real central bank announces an instrument rule and commits to it: the optimal one is too complicated to communicate and a simple one such as a Taylor rule "too simple and imperfect".
- **Projections and the forecast Taylor curve (3.2–3.5)**: the policy problem is to choose, among feasible projections (conditional mean forecasts), the one minimizing an intertemporal forecast loss modified by a Lagrange-multiplier term that delivers the timeless-perspective commitment (3.26)–(3.29). The **forecast Taylor curve** (Figure 3.1) plots discounted sums of squared inflation-gap and output-gap forecasts; the efficient frontier is its boundary and the optimal projection is the tangency with an isoloss line. Projections from arbitrary instrument rules are generally inefficient.
- **Targeting rules (3.6)**: an optimal targeting rule is a first-order condition equating marginal rates of substitution (from the loss function) and transformation (from the transmission mechanism) between target variables; it is invariant to everything else in the model, including additive judgment and shock properties, hence more robust than the optimal policy function. In the simplest NK model it has the projection form (3.33). For large operational models the targeting rule becomes impractical, and policymakers need not know it: they only need to compare the projections. The old constant-interest-rate rule (raise rates if the two-year forecast overshoots) is called "a far-from-optimal targeting rule" now abandoned (footnote 31).
- **Implementation and determinacy (3.7)**: the bank announces the policy-rate path and target-variable projections; if credible, the private sector's expectations equal them and the equilibrium is implemented. Determinacy may require an out-of-equilibrium commitment, e.g. raising the rate if inflation or the targeting-rule deviation exceeds its projection (3.34); the Taylor Principle ensures determinacy in Svensson and Woodford's (2005) example.
- **Discretion and degrees of commitment (3.8)**: the discretion equilibrium is computed by the Oudiz–Sachs iterative algorithm (3.35)–(3.46); under discretion the bank's alternative paths would not be credible. Which description is more realistic is unresolved; Schaumburg and Tambalotti's quasi-commitment finds most of the welfare gain from commitment accrues at relatively low levels of credibility.
- **Uncertainty and judgment (3.9–3.10)**: with an imperfectly observed state, certainty equivalence and the separation principle hold even with forward-looking variables (Pearlman 1992; Svensson and Woodford 2003), against Orphanides' (2003) argument for ignoring noisy output-gap data — the optimal weight on a noisy indicator simply falls. Model uncertainty is handled with Markov jump-linear-quadratic (MJLQ) models, moving from "mean forecast targeting" to "distribution forecast targeting"; Bayesian optimal policy vs. adaptive optimal policy. Judgment enters as the bank's estimate of "add factors" to model equations (Optimal Policy Projections, Svensson and Tetlow 2005).
- **Policy horizon (4.1)**: with linear models, forecast gaps decay as sums of exponentials of eigenvalues, so there is no fixed horizon at which inflation reaches target; the lower the output weight, the faster the inflation gap closes. "Half-time" (footnote 50) is proposed instead of a fixed horizon, and most targeters now speak of the "medium term".

## Main results / findings

- **History (2.1)**: New Zealand's Reserve Bank Act of 1989 (target, independence, accountability) came mainly from dissatisfaction with Muldoon-era meddling — the Bank was to be "Muldoon-proof" — and from a shift to a monetary view of inflation, not from academic research. Canada followed in 1991, the U.K. and Sweden in 1992 and 1993 after currency crises, Finland and Australia in 1993. Table 2.1 lists approximate adoption dates (Roger 2009), including Brazil at 1999 m6. No country has abandoned the regime except to join the euro area. Money-growth targeting was abandoned because the money–inflation relation proved unstable; the Bundesbank is "arguably better described as an implicit inflation targeter".
- **Inflation (2.2.1)**: in OECD subgroups the effect of inflation targeting on average inflation and its variability is insignificant (Ball and Sheridan 2005 and others), but Gertler notes most non-targeters there behaved like targeters, so the classification is blurred; studies of emerging markets (Batini and Laxton 2007, Gonçalves and Salles 2008, Lin and Ye 2009) find significant effects on average inflation and typically on its variability.
- **Expectations (2.2.2)**: an explicit target anchors expectations. Gürkaynak, Levin and Swanson (2006): U.S. far-ahead forward inflation compensation responds strongly to news; the U.K.'s sensitivity disappears after the Bank of England's 1997 independence; Sweden's is insensitive throughout. Table 2.2 (IMF 2008): in inflation-targeting emerging economies the response of expected inflation 1, 3 and 5 years ahead to actual inflation is zero, positive for non-targeters.
- **Output (2.2.3)**: no evidence that targeting harms growth or its volatility (Ball and Sheridan, 20 OECD countries); Goncalves and Carvalho (2009), 30 OECD countries: a targeter saves around 7 percent in output losses per percentage point of disinflation; in emerging markets targeting reduces output volatility. Summary: experiences are similar among developed economies, better for targeters among developing ones.
- **New Zealand's evolution (4.1)**: from a short 2—4 quarter horizon and the direct exchange-rate channel to the aggregate-demand channel and the Forecasting and Policy System (1997), with an interest-rate forecast published from 1997. The November 1999 Briefing adopts flexible targeting: band widened from 0 to 2 percent to 0 to 3 percent, horizon lengthened from 6 to 12 months to 12 to 24 months, softer band edges, and a move from an MCI target to a cash rate. Svensson's 2001 review concluded that the MCI period (mid 1997 to March 1999; weight of 0.5 on the exchange rate) was "a significant deviation from best international practice" and contributed to too tight policy during the Asian crisis.
- **Publishing a policy-rate path (4.2)**: the constant-interest-rate assumption gives biased, possibly explosive or indeterminate, and internally inconsistent forecasts; market-implied paths reduce but do not remove the problems. Deciding on and announcing a policy-rate path (New Zealand 1997, Norges Bank 2005, the Riksbank 2007, the Czech National Bank 2008) "solves all the above problems" and is "the most consistent way of implementing inflation targeting".
- **The Riksbank (4.3)**: a 2 percent CPI target announced in January 1993 to apply from 1995 (tolerance interval abolished in June 2010); independent since 1999 with a six-member board; policy-rate path published since February 2007, attributed minutes since June 2007. Figure 4.1 (July 2009): three repo-rate paths with their CPIF and output-gap forecasts and **mean squared gaps**; the High path is dominated, the board chose Main, and Svensson dissented for Low. Figure 4.2 (February 2009): judgment overrode the Ramses DSGE and BVAR forecasts — the repo rate was cut by 100 basis points to 1 percent, far below the model paths, with a gloomier output view.
- **Norges Bank (4.4)**: a 2.5 percent target from March 2001, explicitly flexible; publishes its own path (officially from November 2005, alternative paths already in March 2005, Figure 4.4), uncertainty fans (Figure 4.3), published criteria for judging paths, alternative scenarios and an "interest-rate account" decomposing path revisions by shocks, which lets the public check whether the bank responds to news or re-optimizes. It is the only bank to announce the output-gap weight it uses in computing optimal policy (see Limitations for the garbled values).
- **Emerging-market preconditions (4.5)**: Batini and Laxton's survey of 21 targeting and 10 non-targeting central banks finds that no targeter had all preconditions before adopting, and success depends more on commitment to build institutions afterwards; de Mello (2008), including Brazil, agrees. Freedman and Ötker-Robe list six conducive elements, among them price stability as the overriding goal and absence of fiscal dominance.
- **Price-level targeting (5.1)**: inflation targeting lets past misses stand, giving the price level a unit root; a price-level target (possibly a path rising at two percent) makes it trend-stationary, reduces long-run price-level uncertainty, and provides automatic stabilization — a fall below a credible target raises inflation expectations and lowers the real rate, valuable at the zero lower bound. Real effects remain under debate; the Bank of Canada shows renewed interest.
- **Financial crisis (5.2)**: the crisis was caused mainly by regulatory and supervisory failures, distorted incentives, information problems and special circumstances (low world real rates, U.S. housing policy), not by monetary policy. Fed policy after 2001 was appropriate ex ante given the deflation risk, and somewhat higher rates would have made little difference: Assenmacher-Wesche and Gerlach (17 countries, 1986-2007) find the GDP effect of policy shocks is about a third of the property-price effect, so lowering property prices by 15 percent would cost 5 percent of GDP; the IMF (2009) finds monetary policy differences explain about 5 percent of cross-country variability in house-price appreciation. The "Greenspan put" was more a communication issue. Lessons: price stability is not enough for financial stability, interest-rate policy is not enough either; financial-stability policy (supervision, regulation, macro-prudential tools such as variable capital, margin and loan-to-value requirements) is distinct from monetary policy in objectives, instruments and authorities. Financial conditions are **indicators**, not targets; "leaning against the wind" is consistent with flexible inflation targeting only as a way to stabilize inflation and resource utilization at longer horizons, and the tradeoff is "very unfavorable" in normal times. Several banks extended forecast horizons from two to three years.

## Limitations and caveats

- **Equations are largely unreadable**: italic and Greek symbols are dropped by the extraction, so (3.1)–(3.50) and the in-text formulas (e.g. the strict loss function and the instrument rule in Section 3's preamble, the optimal targeting rule (3.33)–(3.34), the forecast-gap decay formula in 4.1 and the half-time definition in footnote 50) print as operators and brackets without variables. The text around them is readable; the note describes the equations in words and does not restore them.
- **Decimal points are lost** in several numbers: Table 2.2's entries print as bare digit strings without decimal points, so only the text's statement (zero for targeters at 1, 3 and 5 years, positive for non-targeters) is usable; the Norges Bank values in 4.4 (the output-gap weight, the discount factor and the smoothing weight) print the same way, and are not reported here.
- **Figures 2.1–2.3 and 3.1, 4.1–4.4** survive only as axis ticks and labels; no magnitudes can be read from them. The note uses the text's descriptions.
- A sentence in 4.3 is cut (the description of what the attributed July 2009 minutes show breaks off before "repo-rate was a feasible alternative or not").
- **The file ends mid-sentence** in 5.2.3 ("...resource utilization around a normal level" is cut at "resource utilization"), and the reference list announced in the contents is not in the file, so the many citations cannot be resolved from the clean_md. The NBER copyright line on the cover is cut.
- By the author's own statement the theory is "an idealization" — "potential best-practice inflation targeting", not a description of all targeters; the Riksbank and Norges Bank are chosen because he knows them best, and he was a Riksbank board member at the time.
