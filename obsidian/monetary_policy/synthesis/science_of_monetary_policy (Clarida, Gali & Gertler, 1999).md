## Context and motivation

Interest in how to conduct monetary policy had revived for two reasons: empirical work since the late 1980s showing that monetary policy "significantly influences the short term course of the real economy", and theoretical frameworks that import dynamic general equilibrium methods from real business cycle theory while adding nominal price rigidities. The paper summarizes what this research had learned, organizing it around a stripped-down New Keynesian model, and concentrates on results "robust across a wide variety of macroeconomic frameworks". It states up front that temporary nominal price rigidities are the key friction, which is why "New Keynesian Perspective" is in the title.

## Core argument / thesis

A small forward-looking model — an IS curve and a Phillips curve with a cost-push shock, with the short-term interest rate as the instrument and a quadratic loss in the output gap and inflation — yields a set of reasonably general principles, stated as thirteen numbered Results. Under discretion: a short-run tradeoff between output and inflation variability exists only if there is cost-push inflation; the optimal policy "embeds inflation targeting" (gradual convergence of inflation to target); the nominal rate should rise more than one-for-one with expected inflation; demand shocks should be offset and shocks to potential output accommodated. Because inflation depends on expected future output gaps, there are gains from commitment "even if central bank objectives over output are perfectly aligned" — a result the authors present as new. Empirically, US policy before Volcker accommodated inflation, while under Volcker and Greenspan it followed the implicit inflation targeting the theory recommends.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Baseline model (2.1)**: an IS curve (2.1) from the log-linearized consumption Euler equation — the output gap depends inversely on the real rate and positively on the expected future gap, plus a demand shock; iterated forward (2.5), the gap depends on the whole expected path of real rates. A Phillips curve (2.2) from Calvo staggered price setting — inflation depends on the output gap and on *expected future* inflation, plus a "cost push" shock; iterated forward (2.6), "there is no arbitrary inertia or lagged dependence in inflation". The gap's coefficient falls with the degree of price rigidity (footnote 13). The cost-push shock lets inflation vary independently of excess demand; footnote 14 reads it as deviations of real marginal cost from proportionality with the output gap. The instrument is the nominal rate, which Bernanke and Mihov (1998) find describes Fed procedures since 1965, except non-borrowed reserves targeting in 1980-82; no LM curve is needed.
- **Objective (2.2)**: a quadratic loss in output-gap and inflation deviations (2.7), loosely justified by Rotemberg and Woodford as a second-order approximation of utility. US policymakers' working definition of price stability is inflation "between one and three percent"; the Bundesbank had an official target of two percent.
- **Discretion vs. rules (2.3)**: under discretion the central bank reoptimizes every period and takes expectations as given — the resulting policy is "time consistent"; under a rule it commits to a plan "etched in stone". Following Kydland and Prescott (1977), credibility matters because targets depend on expected future policy.
- **Optimal policy under discretion (section 3)**: a two-stage problem; the first-order condition (3.3) is "lean against the wind" — contract the output gap when inflation is above target, more aggressively the larger the inflation gain per unit of output loss and the smaller the weight on output. Inserting the desired gap into the IS curve gives the optimal interest rate rule (3.6), in which the nominal rate responds to expected inflation with a coefficient above one and offsets demand shocks.
- **Commitment (section 4)**: (i) with an output target above potential (4.1), discretion produces the classic inflationary bias — inflation "persistently above target, and no gain in output" (Result 5); a conservative central banker in the sense of Rogoff (1985) reduces it (Result 6). (ii) With no output bias, committing to a rule within the family the discretionary optimum belongs to (4.5) improves the short-run tradeoff, because a tough rule also lowers expected future gaps; the optimum (4.12)–(4.15) looks like discretion with a lower weight on output than society's, and the implied rate rule (4.16) responds more strongly to expected inflation (Result 7). (iii) The unconstrained optimum under commitment (4.17)–(4.19), following Currie and Levine (1993) and Woodford (1998), turns the level rule for the output gap into a difference rule — keep contracting as long as inflation is above target — which a conservative central banker under discretion would not replicate, and whose implied interest rate rule has an expected-inflation coefficient below one, opening the door to indeterminacy (Result 8). The appendix shows this policy makes the price level stationary.
- **Practical complications (section 5)**: with imperfect information the optimal rules are certainty-equivalent in forecasts (Result 9), which is Svensson's case for targeting the inflation forecast; broad money targets failed because money demand was unstable. Poole's (1970) instrument-choice argument favors the interest rate when money demand shocks are large (Result 10). Brainard-type parameter uncertainty dampens the policy response (Result 11) but does not explain the partial adjustment of estimated rules; Rotemberg and Woodford's alternative is that lagged dependence gives leverage over long rates. Non-smooth preferences rationalize "opportunistic" disinflation, equivalent to targeting an inflation zone (Result 12).
- **Endogenous persistence (section 6)**: a hybrid IS (6.1) and Phillips curve (6.2) with lagged output and inflation, nesting the baseline and the backward-looking Svensson–Ball model. Results 1–4 survive (Result 13); inflation inertia makes the tradeoff less favorable (Figure 3), policy must respond to forecasts of inflation far ahead, the rule (6.6) also responds to the lagged gap, and commitment (or a conservative central banker) speeds disinflation. With one-period delays in policy effects, the rule (6.9) still moves the nominal rate more than one-for-one with expected inflation.
- **Simple rules (section 7)**: Taylor's rule (7.1) is consistent with the principles above — more than one-for-one response to inflation, response to the gap rather than output — and fits US policy over 1987-1992 with parameters Taylor chose by informal judgment. The authors estimate a forward-looking version (7.2), responding to expected inflation a year ahead and the current gap, with partial adjustment (7.3), by GMM.

## Main results / findings

- **Table 1, estimated policy reaction functions (US)**, with standard errors, for the pre-Volcker (60:1-79:2) and Volcker-Greenspan (79:3-96:4) samples. The column headers are garbled; the text identifies the first as the coefficient on the inflation gap, the second as the coefficient on the output gap and the third as the smoothing parameter. Pre-Volcker: 0.80 (0.09), 0.52 (0.12), 0.76 (0.04). Volcker-Greenspan: 1.96 (0.20), 0.07 (0.10), 0.66 (0.03). The pre-Volcker inflation coefficient is "significantly less than unity" — policy accommodated rising expected inflation, and this rule permits self-fulfilling ("sunspot") fluctuations; post-1979 it is significantly above unity, and the output gap matters only for its predictive power for inflation.
- **Figures 4–6**: the ex post real rate was zero or negative for most of the 1970s and turned positive after 1979; the Volcker-Greenspan rule's target sits "systematically well above" the actual rate in the pre-Volcker period; forward- and backward-looking rules fit post-1979 data roughly equally, though the data reject the backward-looking one. Clarida, Galí and Gertler (1998) find similar post-1979 rules for the Bundesbank and the Bank of Japan.
- **Target rules (7.2)**: real-world inflation targeting is the gradualist policy of Result 2; its rationale is a clear nominal anchor (avoiding pre-Volcker mistakes) and a higher effective weight on inflation, which Results 6 and 7 say is optimal. Price-level targeting is rejected (bygones are not bygones, measurement error, small gains per McCallum) and nominal GDP targeting criticized (no anchor under trend shifts, equal weights, may stifle recoveries).
- **Indeterminacy (7.3)**: with sticky prices nominal indeterminacy vanishes, but real indeterminacy arises if the nominal rate does not rise enough to raise the real rate, or if the response is overly aggressive (Bernanke and Woodford); the aggressiveness required for the latter "greatly exceeds the estimates obtained in practice".
- **Transmission lags**: "a lag of six to nine months in the effect of a shift in interest rates on output", and "around a year and a half" on inflation.
- **Open questions (section 8)**: the determinants of inflation persistence (Galí–Gertler and Sbordone: the baseline Phillips curve works with real unit labor costs instead of the gap); open-economy extensions; the zero lower bound ("the only way a central bank can reduce the real interest rate is to generate a rise in expected inflation"); interest rate smoothing; financial stability; transitions to new regimes.

## Limitations and caveats

- **The equations are unreadable in the extraction.** The source PDF's text layer was font-encoded (the tracker records a Caesar-shift encoding); the prose was decoded, but in almost every displayed equation and many inline symbols the variables still print as substituted Latin letters (the output gap appears as `"y`, inflation as `Wy`, the inflation weight's symbol as `h`). This affects (2.1)–(2.7), (3.1)–(3.8), (4.1)–(4.19), (5.1)–(5.10), (6.1)–(6.9), (7.1)–(7.3) and the appendix. Equation (3.2), (5.6) and (7.3) are reduced to their numbers. Some footnotes (10, 14, 30, 51, 75) print the equations in a different, partly legible encoding. The note describes the model in words and does not restore any equation.
- **Numbers inside equations or encoded text are lost**: Taylor's parameter values for (7.1) (printed as "W $ A, " $ cA` W $ /` and l $ /"), the typical estimates of the smoothing parameter for quarterly data ("on the order of cE or c_"), and the three inertia values of Figure 2 in the text (the figure's own labels read φ=.01, φ=.5, φ=.99, matching "low", "medium" and "high" inertia).
- **Ligature loss**: the "ff" ligature is dropped throughout ("e\nects", "o\nset", "trade-o\n"), splitting words across lines; the text is readable but word searches in the clean_md may miss them.
- **Figures 1–6** survive only as titles and axis/series labels.
- **Table 1** column headers are encoded; the attribution of columns above follows the text's discussion ("the parameter W, the coefficient on the inflation gap"; the output gap coefficient "not significantly different from zero" post-1979).
- No reference list, title page or publication metadata in the file; cited works can only be identified by author and year.
- Model limits stated by the authors: closed economy, the zero lower bound assumed not to bind, rational expectations with no learning about regime changes, persistence in the baseline coming only from serially correlated shocks; footnote 25 restricts attention to Markov perfect equilibria (no reputational equilibria).
