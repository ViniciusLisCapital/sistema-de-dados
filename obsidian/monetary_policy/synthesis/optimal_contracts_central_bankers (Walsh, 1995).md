## Context and motivation

Kydland and Prescott (1977) and Barro and Gordon (1983) showed that discretionary policy has an inflation bias when the monetary authority wants output above its equilibrium level. The literature that followed treats central banks as responding to incentives, but mostly as many principals (citizens) choosing one agent whose objective function they cannot specify — Rogoff's (1985) conservative central banker, Lohmann (1992), Waller (1992). In practice governments, not citizens, choose central-bank heads, and they can shape the bank's incentives through appointment and reappointment rules, reporting requirements, legislated goals and budget procedures — a "contract" between government and central bank. New Zealand considered a bonus for its central-bank head if the inflation target was met (not in the Reserve Bank Act of 1989 as passed). Instead of analyzing ad hoc targeting rules, the paper derives the optimal incentive structure directly, in a principal–agent framework, using a state-contingent wage contract as "a useful fiction".

## Core argument / thesis

The trade-off between inflation bias and suboptimal stabilization found by Rogoff (1985), Garfinkel and Oh (1993) and others is an artifact of arbitrary restrictions on the contracts considered, not a feature of the policy problem. In the standard one-period Barro–Gordon model the inflation bias is constant across states of nature, so a contract only has to raise the central banker's marginal cost of inflation by a constant: a transfer **linear** in realized money growth or inflation eliminates the bias while leaving the bank free to respond optimally to shocks — "full credibility and flexibility are simultaneously achieved" — even when the bank has private information. The optimal contract looks like an inflation-targeting rule, although such rules are usually thought suboptimal with supply shocks.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Model (Section I)**: social/government loss (1) quadratic in output's deviation from a target above equilibrium output and in inflation (weight β); a Lucas-type supply relation (2) with nominal contracts set before an i.i.d. supply shock; an incentive to surprise requires the target gap k > 0 (3); inflation equals money growth plus a control error or velocity shock, minus a direct effect of the supply shock (4). The central bank sets money growth after observing a private, noisy signal of the supply shock.
- **Optimal commitment policy (5)**: money growth proportional to the forecast of the supply shock, with zero average inflation; it is time-inconsistent if run by a bank that just minimizes (1).
- **Contract**: the bank (sharing the government's preferences) maximizes the transfer minus the social loss (6), subject to a participation constraint. Contracts contingent on the private signal are infeasible, so the transfer can depend only on observables. Under discretion (7) the bank's response to the signal already equals the optimal one; only the average bias (shown as ak/β in the text) is wrong. The transfer (9) — a constant minus a term linear in money growth — implements the optimal policy; with a nonzero inflation target it becomes a penalty on money growth above the target and a reward below it. A transfer linear in realized inflation does the same.
- **Why Rogoff's trade-off appears**: Rogoff's scheme is equivalent to a contract that only raises the weight on the **quadratic** inflation term, so the extra marginal cost of inflation depends on the level of inflation and distorts the response to the signal; a linear term avoids that.
- **Performance contracts (Section II)**: if the banker cares only about her transfer, the general quadratic contract in inflation and output (10)–(14) has redundant parameters; the optimal one (15)–(17) equals the linear inflation transfer minus the social loss. Any optimal performance contract must involve output; a contract solely in inflation (18) gives suboptimal policy unless there are no supply shocks, money has no real effect, or the government places no weight on output stabilization.
- **Imperfect monitoring, competency and effort (Section III, after Laffont and Tirole 1986)**: the government observes only a broad aggregate (instrument plus control error — think M2), banker types differ in ability, and costly effort improves the signal's quality. With known type, less able bankers exert more effort but not enough to compensate (20). With unobserved type, a truth-telling mechanism (21)–(23) induces less effort than with known type (except for the best banker), more efficient bankers earn rents, and the transfer (24) penalizes squared deviations of inflation from a target that depends on the bank's announced type and on the signal — "an inflation-targeting rule with a reporting requirement". The Appendix proves (24) implements the effort function and the money rule.

## Main results / findings

- **Linear inflation (or money-growth) contract** eliminates the inflation bias and keeps optimal stabilization; Canzoneri's (1985) concern that private central-bank information makes reputational solutions hard to enforce is irrelevant under it, because the contract depends only on publicly observable money growth or inflation and the government gains nothing from observing or eliciting the signal. It also works if the bank privately observes the velocity shock.
- **Inflation-only goals** (New Zealand's Reserve Bank Act of 1989, which makes inflation control the sole objective; price stability as the only macroeconomic goal proposed for the European Central Bank) are socially optimal only if the banker's utility also reflects society's preference over output, directly or through a contract that depends on output.
- **Contingent inflation target**: when the bank cares only about its transfer and both type and instrument are private, the optimal contract is a contingent inflation-targeting rule with a reporting requirement; its weight on squared deviations is independent of the signal and only affects effort, while the signal sets the target. Its informational requirements "may be severe".
- **Institutional reading**: existing structures may mimic the optimal contract — inflation-targeting rules with reporting requirements, and making the governor's employment contingent on prespecified inflation targets, as under the New Zealand Act (agreement on a target, "currently 0-2 percent", with failure as grounds for dismissal). Walsh (1993b) shows a dismissal rule can sustain the optimal policy when wage contracts are limited.
- **Summary claim**: the difficulty of combining optimal responses to new information and zero average inflation is "not inherent in the basic framework"; apparent trade-offs among price stability, stabilization, flexibility and credibility "may simply reflect the suboptimal incentives the central bank faces under ad hoc targeting rules."

## Limitations and caveats

- **Equations are damaged by OCR** throughout: Greek letters print as Latin letters or symbols (inflation as "7", "T" or "r"; the signal θ as "@", "0", "6" or "9"; the supply shock as "€", "«" or "¢"), and fractions are split over lines in (5), (7)–(8), (11)–(14), (20), (22)–(23) and the Appendix (A1)–(A10). The text's own statements of the results are readable; the note describes the equations in words and does not restore them.
- Footnote markers are scrambled ("3A5", "'4", "Yt", "261", "281f") and several footnote numbers are lost; the author's name prints as "Cart E. WALSH". There is **no reference list in the file**, so citations (Lohmann 1992, Waller 1992, Garfinkel and Oh 1993, Walsh 1993a, b, 1994) cannot be resolved from the clean_md. The JSTOR cover page precedes the text.
- The derivation of the transfer function under unobserved type follows "an earlier version of the present paper", available on request and not in the file.
- Author's caveats: the state-contingent wage contract is a device for deriving incentives, and its implementability is questioned — a government could make side-payments to undo legislated incentives, the social loss may dwarf any feasible transfer, and the government's output target may be its private information; the analysis is one-period, assumes homogeneous preferences (Waller 1992 shows a conservative banker may not then be optimal) and that government and banker share preferences ex ante.
