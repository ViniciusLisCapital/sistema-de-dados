## Context and motivation

Simple policy rules have gained attention as a route to more transparent and effective policy, and Taylor's (1993) rule in particular because it described the federal funds rate "rather surprisingly well". But such analyses assume the data the rule needs — nominal output, real output, potential output — are known when policy is set, whereas they are "not known with any accuracy until much later". Rules reacting to within-quarter data are not operational (McCallum), revisions change what the "appropriate" setting looked like, model-based rule evaluations assume the reacted-to variables are known with certainty, and reaction functions estimated on ex post revised data may misdescribe historical policy and contaminate estimated policy shocks. The paper measures how large these informational problems are, using Taylor's rule as the example.

## Core argument / thesis

Taylor-rule prescriptions computed from data available in real time differ considerably from those computed with ex post revised data, and within-year revisions of the prescription have a standard deviation exceeding that of the quarterly change in the federal funds rate. Reaction functions estimated on revised data can give misleading descriptions of historical policy: with real-time Federal Reserve staff (Greenbook) forecasts, simple **forward-looking** rules describe policy in 1987-1992 better than Taylor-type contemporaneous rules, a fact "largely obscured" by revised data. The informational problem does not refute the stabilization benefits of rules, but evaluations must model the measurement errors explicitly.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **The rule family**: the funds rate as a linear function of inflation and the output gap (real-output-plus-inflation targeting, from the Bryant, Hooper and Mann 1993 model comparison, where it beat exchange-rate, money and nominal-income targets). Taylor's parameterization (1): response coefficients of one half, inflation target and equilibrium real rate of two percent, inflation as the four-quarter change in the implicit output deflator, the gap from a log-linear trend through real GDP — the rule with intercept 1, 1.5 on inflation and 0.5 on the gap.
- **Real-time database**: vintages indexed by the quarter in which an estimate is available. Within-quarter estimates come from the Greenbook, prepared before each FOMC meeting and public with a five-year lag; the February, May, August and November Greenbooks give observations dated at the middle month of each quarter, after the first NIPA estimate for the previous quarter. Potential output in real time uses the Federal Reserve's Q* series (method of Clark 1982 and Braun 1990), because Taylor's detrending over 1984–1992 cannot be replicated in real time. Real-time data differ from Taylor's in three ways: measurement concepts then in use (GNP and 1982 prices before 1992), unrevised contemporaneous forecasts, and real-time potential.
- **Comparisons**: Taylor's rule rebuilt with data as of 1993:1 (his original) and 1994:4 (revised; Taylor 1994), with Taylor's potential and with Q*; then the real-time rule using Greenbook forecasts and Q*; then the rule recomputed with each of the next four quarters' estimates (Section 3), with the output-gap revision split into revisions of real output and of potential.
- **Estimated reaction functions (Section 4)**: the rule embedded in a partial-adjustment specification with smoothing parameter ρ, estimated by least squares and by IV (four lags of the funds rate, inflation and the gap as instruments, Newey-West standard errors), on revised and real-time data, at target horizons from one quarter back (an operational backward-looking rule) to four quarters ahead.

## Main results / findings

- **Taylor vs. Q* potential** (Table 2): with revised data the two rules differ on average by 47 basis points, with a standard deviation of "merely 6 basis points" and a mean absolute demeaned difference of 5 — an intercept shift, since Q* lets potential exceed actual output on average.
- **Real time vs. revised** (Figure 2, Tables 1–2): the real-time prescription was "consistently lower" than the actual funds rate and more volatile. The level gap comes mainly from the output gap: the mean real-time gap estimate is about one percentage point lower than the estimate based on 1994 data (Table 1). The standard deviation of the difference between the revised-data and real-time prescriptions is 58 basis points, with a maximum "approaching a staggering 200 basis points". The rule's fit to the funds rate: standard deviation of 52 basis points with revised data, 68 with real-time data, against 54 for the quarterly change in the funds rate — a naive no-change forecast would have beaten a real-time observer using the rule.
- **1992 "financial headwinds"**: the explanation that the Fed held rates below the rule because of headwinds is contradicted in real time — throughout 1992 the funds rate was above the real-time rule; "The residual is of the wrong sign", because the recovery looked much worse in real time than after revisions (the NBER recognized the March 1991 trough only in December 1992).
- **Within-year revisions** (Tables 3–4, Figures 4–5): the standard deviation of the first revision of the output gap is 66 basis points, and still 48 basis points from t+3 to t+4, a full year later; revisions to real output alone explain most of the gap revisions; four-quarter inflation revisions vary less than gap revisions. The prescription four quarters later differs from the original with a standard deviation of 67 basis points and a maximum of 170. Since the first revision already uses actual preliminary data, rules reacting to lagged data face "the same informational problems" — "simply a matter of degree".
- **Taylor-type estimates** (Tables 5–6): revised data give an inflation response close to 1.5 and a gap response close to 0.5; real-time data give a much lower inflation response and a worse fit, and with partial adjustment the inflation coefficient is "nowhere near one and could well equal zero" (printed 0:10 by least squares and 0:01 by IV in the real-time column, Tables 5 and 6). Taken seriously this would imply an unstable inflation process (footnote 18: an inflation coefficient above one is required for stability); the more likely explanation is a misspecified, myopic rule. The partial-adjustment coefficient is "large and tight", confirming considerable smoothing.
- **Forward-looking rules with real-time data** (Tables 7–8): at the three- and four-quarter-ahead horizons the inflation response is "surprisingly close to the 1.5 value" and significantly greater than one, the gap response is large and tightly estimated (above 0.5), and the fit is somewhat better than for contemporaneous or backward-looking rules; the data cannot distinguish among forward horizons.
- **Forward-looking rules with revised data** (Table 9): the inflation coefficient rises with the horizon, from (insignificantly) negative backward-looking to exceeding 3 four quarters ahead, but forward rules fit worse than the contemporaneous Taylor-type rule and ρ rises with the horizon — so revised data "could easily overshadow" the better description forward-looking rules give.
- **Conclusions**: rules on less-revised data (unemployment, CPI) are not immune, since policy needs the unemployment gap relative to an uncertain natural rate and an uncertain CPI bias; transparency of simple rules is weakened because disagreement over the outlook or pending revisions makes deviations hard to detect; identification of policy shocks with revised data, e.g. in VARs, becomes "a haphazard enterprise"; rule evaluations should add auxiliary equations relating true to real-time inflation and gap, whose errors "cannot be assumed away".

## Limitations and caveats

- **The file is the December 1997 working paper**, not the 2001 published version the file name and tracker refer to; changes between the two cannot be checked here. The sample is short (1987:1 to 1992:4), which the author notes limits accurate estimation.
- **Tables are damaged**: decimal points print as colons ("1:5", "0:79") and minus signs as "(cid:0)", often on a separate line from their number, so the sign of some entries is ambiguous. Tables 5–6 print standard errors on unlabeled lines under each estimate. In Tables 7–9 the column labels for the horizons are lost (only a stray minus sign survives), so which column is which horizon must be inferred from the text (columns run from one quarter back to four ahead). The notes to Tables 8 and 9 are cut off mid-sentence. Figures 1–5 survive only as axis labels and legends. The note cites only table entries whose meaning the text confirms.
- **Text artifacts**: ligatures print as "(cid:11)", "(cid:12)", "(cid:13)", "(cid:14)" (ff, fi, fl, ffi) and Greek letters as "(cid:25)" (π), "(cid:26)" (ρ), "(cid:3)" (asterisk); subscripted vintages print run together ("Rtjt", "ytj94:4"). Equations are readable with these substitutions; none is restored here.
- No reference list in the file, so citations (Taylor 1993, 1994; Clark 1982; Braun 1990; Clarida, Gali and Gertler 1997a) cannot be resolved from the clean_md.
- Author's caveats: Greenbook forecasts may not represent the FOMC's views and are conditioned on a specific policy path; the linear specification may miss nonlinear rules (Orphanides and Wilcox 1996); Q* is an experimental series; "revisions" also cover redefinitions and rebenchmarks.
