## Context and motivation

Natural-rate models with rational expectations (Sargent and Wallace 1975) imply that the systematic part of monetary policy is irrelevant for real activity, and Barro's own evidence supports the neutrality of anticipated money. Yet the same evidence shows countercyclical monetary policy (rises in unemployment followed by faster money growth), and inflation and money growth were high and variable. Within the natural-rate framework "it is difficult to reconcile this observed countercyclical monetary behavior with rationality of the policymaker." Existing models let the policymaker choose a rule once and for all, which excludes "the essentially game-theoretic situation that arises when policy decisions are made on an ongoing basis." The paper builds a **positive** theory of inflation in which excessive average inflation and activist policy are outcomes of rational, public-spirited policymaking when commitment is impossible.

## Core argument / thesis

When the policymaker sets inflation each period taking the public's expectations as given — and the public knows it — the rational expectations (Nash) equilibrium has unemployment at its natural rate, as under any rule, but inflation **above** the zero rate a precommitted rule would deliver. The policymaker always has an incentive to surprise (unexpected inflation lowers unemployment below a natural rate that is inefficiently high), so the public expects inflation high enough that the marginal cost of more inflation just offsets the marginal gain from a surprise. The outcome is worse than under a rule, yet the policymaker "does as well for the public as possible" given that precommitment is precluded. Hence "Discretion is the sub-set of rules that provides no guarantees about the government's future behavior", and the value of long-term agreements between government and public "underlies the argument for rules over discretion."

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Model (section 1, based on Kydland and Prescott 1977, pp. 477-80, and Gordon 1980)**: unemployment equals the natural rate minus a constant Phillips-curve slope times unexpected inflation (equation 1); the natural rate follows a persistent process with a long-run mean and i.i.d. real shocks (2). Period cost (3) is quadratic in the gap between unemployment and a **target below the natural rate** (k times the natural rate, with k between 0 and 1, because taxes, unemployment compensation and the like make the natural rate inefficiently high) and in inflation. "k < 1 is a necessary condition for activist policy to arise." The policymaker minimizes the expected present value of costs (4) and controls inflation directly.
- **Expectations mechanism**: agents form expectations from the same information as the policymaker, by forecasting the policymaker's best action; within a period expectations are fixed while inflation is chosen. With future expectations independent of current inflation, the problem reduces to one period.
- **Equilibrium (equations 8–10)**: the first-order condition gives chosen inflation as a function of expected inflation and the expected natural rate; consistency (expected = actual) yields the equilibrium inflation rate, increasing in the Phillips-curve slope, in the cost ratio of unemployment to inflation, in (1−k) and in the expected natural unemployment rate. Unemployment equals the natural rate. "Zero inflation is not a reasonable expectation for individuals to hold."
- **Contrast with a once-and-for-all rule (equation 11)**: if the reaction function is chosen once, expected and actual inflation move together, the policymaker sees unemployment as invariant to the rule, and zero inflation is optimal — Kydland and Prescott's "optimal" solution, against their "time-consistent" one, which corresponds to equation (10) (the footnote attached to equation 11, whose number is lost).
- **Money growth as instrument (section 2)**: with money demand proportional to output and falling with expected inflation (12)–(13), and a control error on money growth, the authority sets money growth to hit the same expected inflation; past money and velocity shocks are offset later, and money growth (not inflation) reacts positively to lagged unemployment.
- **Revenue from money creation (section 4)**: maximizing seigniorage without commitment has no equilibrium (Calvo 1978: systematic surprises, possibly infinite money growth); with inflation also a cost, equilibrium inflation is high enough that the marginal cost of inflation balances the revenue gain from a hypothetical surprise — no surprise revenue is actually collected, but its potential sets inflation.
- **Government bonds (section 5)**: unexpected inflation is an ex post capital levy on nominal debt; equilibrium inflation and nominal rates are high enough to deter it. Indexing public debt is a form of commitment that would lower equilibrium inflation, unless indexation also lowers the perceived cost of inflation.
- **Reputational equilibria (section 6)**: agents expect zero inflation unless they observe otherwise, after which they expect the discretionary rate forever (a trigger strategy). The policymaker either cheats once and gets the discretionary outcome from period 2 on, or keeps inflation at zero to preserve "credibility".

## Main results / findings

Implications of the discretionary equilibrium (section 1):
1. Average inflation exceeds the value that would be optimal with precommitment (zero here); a shift away from a regime with nominal precommitment — a gold standard or fixed exchange rates — raises average inflation and money growth.
2. A rise in the long-run natural unemployment rate raises average inflation; "A significant rise in U is generally thought to have occurred in the U.S. over the last 10-15 years."
3. Inflation responds positively to lagged unemployment (given persistence of the natural rate): "The rational policymaker acts countercyclically."
4. The countercyclical response rises with the Phillips-curve slope and with the relative cost of unemployment to inflation.
5. Unemployment is invariant to the systematic part of inflation — policy has no effect on real activity.

- No equilibrium exists if inflation carries no direct cost (footnote 7).
- "Cruel choice": advising stable prices to a discretionary policymaker "is analogous to advising firms to produce more output with given inputs"; zero inflation follows only with precommitment.
- **Reputation is fragile** (section 6): a known finite horizon unravels it; a probabilistic end raises the effective discount rate and makes it harder to sustain; a run-up in the natural rate can make cheating worthwhile; with imperfect monitoring policymakers have incentives to cheat and to "proclaim the end of a previous regime". It is "a fragile approximation to the rules equilibrium", though casual observation suggests reputation sometimes works.
- **Rules vs. discretion (section 7)**: the meaningful distinction is precommitment — not the number of arguments in a reaction function, nor ignorance favoring simple rules. Footnote 19: discretion with artificially manipulated preferences — infinite weight on inflation, zero weight on unemployment, or treating the natural rate as optimal (k = 1) — can replicate the rule, and outcomes may improve if the policymaker's preferences diverge from society's.
- **Institutions (section 8)**: excess inflation and "apparently unrewarding countercyclical policy" are rational within a discretionary regime; the economist "has no useful day-to-day advice" to give the Fed, and the central issue is designing institutions — commodity standards, legal restrictions on paper money — that precommit money and prices. The move away from the gold standard and fixed exchange rates might be labeled "a mistake."

## Limitations and caveats

- **Equations are badly damaged by OCR.** In equations (1)–(20) and footnotes 4, 9 and 13 the Greek letters and sub/superscripts are lost or misread (inflation prints as "ir", "1r", "n"; the natural rate as "U", "U"", "IJ"; expected values and time subscripts are split across lines). Equations (9)–(10), the equilibrium inflation formula, and (14)–(18), the money-growth solution, cannot be read reliably. The note describes their content in words from the surrounding text and the listed implications, and does not restore them.
- **Section 6 is garbled at its key sentence**: the comparison of the rules equilibrium with the "inferior" period-by-period outcome prints both as "U = U" with the inflation values lost; the meaning follows from the rest of the section.
- **Footnote numbers are scrambled** (two footnotes print as "13", one as "lO"; the numbers of footnote 5 and of the footnote on equation 11 are lost, the latter run into footnote 7; the footnote on depreciating public debt in wartime begins mid-sentence). The abstract's first lines are out of order. There is no reference list in the file.
- The file is the November 1981 NBER working paper; the 1983 published version is not in the file, so any changes between them cannot be checked here.
- Authors' scope: a model simple enough to allow unanimity about desirable policy; inflation's costs are assumed, not derived; the policymaker's objective is assumed to match society's; costs of setting up and enforcing rules are ignored; no learning about the policymaker; the positive theory of how monetary institutions are chosen is left open.
