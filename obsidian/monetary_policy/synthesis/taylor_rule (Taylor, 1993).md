## Context and motivation

Econometric evaluation of policy rules with rational-expectations models had produced a rough consensus that good monetary rules move the short-term interest rate in response to the price level (or inflation) and real income. Yet "Policymakers do not, and are not evidently about to, follow policy rules mechanically": a quarterly formula cannot tell a temporary price blip from a permanent one, cannot read potential output, and cannot handle special episodes such as the October 19, 1987 stock-market break. Taylor's question is how to "preserve the concept of a policy rule even in an environment where it is practically impossible to follow mechanically the algebraic formulas economists write down" — since, he argues, "policy rules have major advantages over discretion in improving economic performance".

## Core argument / thesis

A policy rule need not be a fixed instrument setting nor a mechanical formula: it can be operated "more informally by policymakers who recognize the general instrument responses that underlie the policy rule", while pure discretion sets instruments "from scratch each period with no attempt to follow a reasonably well-defined contingency plan". The paper proposes a representative interest-rate rule — equation (1), what later became known as the Taylor rule — shows that it "fits the actual policy performance during the last few years remarkably well", and proposes using it as one input to FOMC decisions (or its general properties as guidance), with judgment for cases where a literal reading misleads.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Semantics (section 1)**: feedback rules, automatic stabilizers, and fixed or crawling pegs are all policy rules. The time-consistency literature (Kydland and Prescott 1977, Barro and Gordon 1983, Blanchard and Fischer 1989) gives the precise distinction: the rule is the "optimal", "rules" or "precommitted" solution, discretion the "inconsistent", "cheating" or "shortsighted" one; the advantage of rules is "like the advantage of a cooperative over a noncooperative solution in game theory". The 1990 Economic Report of the President's term "systematic policy" is offered as an alternative label. A rule must last "a reasonably long period of time" to deliver credibility.
- **Three kinds of policy issue**: (1) design of a rule, (2) transition to a new rule, (3) day-to-day operation of a rule in place. Actions that look discretionary can be transitions or part of a rule's operation.
- **Design evidence (section 2)**: the Bryant, Hooper and Mann (1993) comparison of nine multicountry models (seven estimated rational-expectations models) finds interest-rate rules reacting to the price level and real output perform better than rules focused on the money supply or the exchange rate. Taylor's own multicountry rational-expectations model of the G-7 ranks rules by price and output stability, with an estimated shock distribution.
- **The representative rule (1)**, printed in the extraction as `r = p + .5y + .5(p - 2) + 2`, with r the federal funds rate, p "the rate of inflation over the previous four quarters", y "the percent deviation of real GDP from a target", and trend real GDP growing 2.2 percent per year from 1984.1 through 1992.3. The rate rises if inflation exceeds "a target of 2 percent" or real GDP rises above trend; with both on target "the federal funds rate would equal 4 percent, or 2 percent in real terms". Lagged inflation proxies expected inflation, so the rule is "written in 'real' terms"; the 2-percent equilibrium real rate is close to the assumed 2.2 percent steady-state growth rate; the coefficients on the output gap and inflation are equal and are "round numbers that make for easy discussion".
- **Transitions (section 3)**: changing the inflation target in the rule (e.g. from 5-percent to 2-percent) changes only the intercept — "none other than the problem of disinflation". Transitions need special treatment because rational expectations is "probably stretching things" right after a rule changes (people learn, possibly in a Bayesian way) and because wage, investment and loan commitments are rigid, so transitions "should be gradual and announced publicly". In the Taylor (1975) learning model, disinflation from above-target inflation calls for maximum credibility (announce and start); in the unusual opposite case information should be released gradually.
- **Operation (section 4)**: two options — add the specific rule to the FOMC briefing books (staff forecasts of the federal funds rate implied by the rule, with bands across variants in coefficients or using GDP growth instead of its level), or state only the rule's general principles, as the 1990 Economic Report did; that characterization gives only the signs of the responses, with magnitudes depending on "the interest sensitivity of aggregate demand", and no target for inflation or output.

## Main results / findings

- **Fixed vs. flexible exchange rates (Taylor's G-7 simulations)**: output fluctuations are "much larger in the United States, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, and the United Kingdom when exchange rates are fixed"; the standard deviation of output "nearly doubles in Germany and Japan"; Japan and Germany have "more than twice as much price volatility" under a fixed dollar rate. Canada has slightly less output volatility under fixed rates but worse price stability, so "the flexible exchange-rate system dominates for all the countries I considered". Central banks should set rates on domestic conditions, "paying little attention to exchange rates", and some positive weight on real output beats a pure price rule, though whether the output weight should exceed the price weight is unclear.
- **Fit to the Fed (Figure 1, 1987-1992)**: the rule tracks the actual federal funds rate closely, with "a significant deviation in 1987 when the Fed reacted to the crash in the stock market by easing interest rates". The Fed acted "as if" it followed a rule much like the one research recommends.
- **Case study, the 1990 oil shock**: Iraq invaded Kuwait on August 2, 1990; oil went from $21 per barrel at the end of July to $28 on August 6 and a peak of $46 in mid-October (monthly averages $17 in July, $36 in October). The CEA consensus: a one-year temporary 50 percent oil-price rise could raise the GDP deflator by about 1 percent and later lower output by about the same. Read literally, rule (1) called for a higher rate; but the December 1991 futures price rose only about $4 against $25 for spot, signalling a temporary shock, so a rate rise "would be inappropriate-despite the literal interpretation of equation 1" — at the cost of a deviation from the rule. Fiscal side: the Gramm-Rudman-Hollings law did not allow the automatic stabilizers to work and had to be revised.
- **Case study, German unification and the bond market**: in early 1990 ten-year Treasury yields rose by 75 basis points. Larger German increases and the expected rise in German capital demand and budget deficit (West German surplus of .2 percent of GDP in 1989 turning, as printed, into "a deficit of 3-percent of GDP in 1990") pointed to real, not inflationary, causes; forward-looking model simulations put the consistent interest-rate increase at about one percentage point, so policy "need not be adjusted".
- **Implicit inflation target**: with velocity stable in the long run, the Fed's 1992 money-growth ranges imply a price-level target growing at 0 to 4 percent per year; the 2-percent implicit target "is probably very close to price stability".

## Limitations and caveats

- **Equation (1) is damaged by OCR**: the variable names switch case between the equation (`p`, `y`) and their definitions (`P`, `Y`), and the output-gap definition prints as "Y = lOO(Y - Y*)/Y*", with the same symbol for the gap and for real GDP. The note quotes the equation as printed and describes it from the surrounding text; it does not restore it.
- **Figures 1-3** (federal funds rate vs. the rule; real GDP and the 2.2 percent trend; four-quarter GDP-deflator inflation) survive only as captions and scrambled axis ticks; the fit cannot be read off them, only the text's description of it.
- **Several numbers in section 4 are OCR-garbled**: the secular real output growth ("2i percent"), the Fed's 1992 money growth range ("2: percent to 63 percent"), and the 1991 M2 target-range mean ("43 percent", after 7 percent in 1987 and 5 percent in 1990). They are not reproduced here beyond the clean parts.
- The design results come from Taylor (1993), the multicountry model book, and from Bryant, Hooper and Mann (1993); the paper reports them qualitatively, with no table of coefficients or variances. The two transition exercises (to a zero-inflation rule and to a balanced full-employment budget) are said to be examined in Taylor (1993) and are not in this file.
- No reference list in the extraction; citations appear only in the text and footnote 1.
- The rule is a description of US policy over a short window and is explicitly not meant to be followed mechanically; the oil-shock case shows the literal rule giving the wrong answer for a temporary price-level shock.
