## Context and motivation

Since the subprime crisis began in August 2007 the Federal Reserve has lent directly in private credit markets — wider discount-window collateral and eligibility, backstop funding for commercial paper, purchases of agency debt and mortgage-backed securities — and its holdings of private assets went "from virtually nothing to nearly a trillion and half". With the short rate at the zero lower bound it "must rely exclusively on its new unconventional balance sheet operations". Operational models had not kept pace: Christiano–Eichenbaum–Evans (CEE) and Smets–Wouters (SW) assume frictionless finance, and friction models such as Bernanke–Gertler–Gilchrist (BGG) or Christiano–Motto–Rostagno do not consider central bank intermediation. The paper builds a quantitative model in which unconventional policy can be analysed "in the same general manner" as conventional policy. It does not model the subprime crisis itself, but its key element: deteriorating intermediary balance sheets that disrupt the flow of funds, raise spreads and tighten lending, with falling asset values feeding back on balance sheets.

## Core argument / thesis

Financial intermediaries face an agency problem with depositors that endogenously limits their leverage, so the economy's credit flow is tied to intermediary equity. A loss of intermediary capital raises the spread between the return on capital and the riskless rate and deepens a downturn. The central bank, which "can commit to always honoring its debt", is not balance-sheet constrained: it can borrow from savers and lend to firms (directly or through intermediaries) when private intermediation is disrupted, at an efficiency cost per unit lent. In a simulated crisis this credit policy significantly moderates the contraction, and the welfare gains can be substantial if the efficiency costs are modest.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Framework (section 2)**: the CEE/SW monetary DSGE model (habit formation, variable capital utilization, investment adjustment costs, Calvo pricing with indexation to lagged inflation, retailers as the source of nominal rigidity) plus financial intermediaries. Unlike BGG, where firms borrow directly from households and intermediaries are a veil, here only intermediaries face financing constraints; non-financial firms borrow from them frictionlessly.
- **Households and bankers (section 2.1)**: each household has workers and bankers with perfect consumption insurance; a banker stays a banker with probability θ, so bankers have finite horizons and never become self-financing; exiting bankers' earnings return to the household, which gives new bankers start-up funds (a fraction ξ of assets intermediated by exiting bankers).
- **Intermediary constraint (section 2.2, eqs. 5–18)**: a banker can divert a fraction λ of assets; depositors can then recover only the rest. The incentive constraint — franchise value at least λ times assets (eq. 9) — binds in equilibrium and gives assets = leverage ratio × net worth (eq. 13), with leverage independent of bank-specific factors so it aggregates (eq. 15). Net worth grows with the excess return times leverage plus the riskless return (eq. 14), so it is most sensitive to the ex post return on assets when leverage is high.
- **Credit policy (section 2.3, eqs. 19–20)**: the central bank issues riskless government debt and lends at the market lending rate, funding a fraction ψ of intermediated assets, at an efficiency cost τ per unit (costs of raising funds via government debt or of identifying good investments). Lending through private intermediaries is equivalent, because intermediaries cannot walk away from obligations to the government. Total leverage becomes private leverage divided by 1 − ψ.
- **Capital quality shock (section 2.4)**: an exogenous factor shrinks the effective quantity of capital, standing in for a loss on intermediary assets.
- **Policy (section 2.7, eqs. 36–38)**: a Taylor rule with interest-rate smoothing; credit policy follows a feedback rule raising ψ with the spread over its steady state, with feedback parameter ν. Government spending is financed by lump-sum taxes and the net earnings of government intermediation.
- **Calibration (section 3.1, Table 1)**: eighteen parameters, fifteen conventional and three financial. Table 1: β 0.995, h 0.700, χ 5.584, inverse Frisch elasticity 0.333, α 0.330, steady-state depreciation 0.025, utilization elasticity 1.000, inverse elasticity of net investment to the price of capital 2.500, ε 11.000, price-fixity probability 0.750, indexation 0.500, Taylor coefficients 1.500 (inflation) and -0.500 (output gap, as printed), G/Y 0.200. Financial parameters λ 0.383, ξ 0.003, θ 0.972, chosen to hit a steady-state spread of one hundred basis points (pre-2007 mortgage and BAA spreads over government bonds), a leverage ratio of four and an average banker horizon of a decade.

## Main results / findings

- **Conventional shocks (Figure 1)**: a one percent negative TFP shock (autoregressive factor 0.95) is modestly amplified, mainly through investment, whose decline is "on the order of fifty percent" larger than without frictions, as falling asset prices weaken intermediary balance sheets and raise the premium. An unanticipated twenty-five basis point rise in the short rate is amplified more, because it has a relatively large effect on investment and asset prices, which "triggers the financial accelerator mechanism". A transfer of one percent of intermediary net worth to households has no effect without frictions but raises the premium and lowers output and investment with them.
- **Crisis (Figure 2)**: a five percent decline in capital quality (autoregressive factor 0.66), roughly a ten percent fall in the effective capital stock over two years. Without frictions output falls only modestly and investment picks up. With frictions intermediary capital falls by a magnified amount, the spread "skyrockets", output falls about three percent relative to trend initially and about six percent later, and does not recover until roughly five years after the shock; slow deleveraging of intermediaries keeps the spread elevated — the model formalizes "how the need for financial institutions to deleverage can slow the recovery".
- **Credit policy (Figure 3)**: with ν = 50 (an intervention of roughly the size seen in practice) the central bank balance sheet rises by about ten percent of the capital stock; with ν = 500 by about twenty percent. Both significantly moderate the contraction, mainly by damping the rise in the spread and so the fall in investment; the aggressive policy adds little over the moderate one.
- **Welfare (section 3.3, Figure 4)**: taking the Taylor rule as given and choosing ν optimally, with welfare as recursive household utility (second-order approximation) and gains cumulated and normalized by one year's steady-state consumption. With a fifteen percent markup and τ = 0 the gain is 6.50 percent; with an efficiency cost of ten basis points about 5.0 percent; it falls to zero as τ approaches forty basis points. Below forty basis points the optimal policy comes close to fully stabilizing the markup. With steady-state distortions of thirty-three or fifty percent (markup plus taxes) the gains exceed fourteen percent at τ = 0 and stay above ten percent at twenty basis points; with perfectly elastic labor supply they rise roughly twenty percent across the board.
- **Credit policy versus equity injections (section 4)**: abstracting from efficiency costs, equity injections into intermediaries are equivalent; the choice turns on efficiency costs — low for securitized high-grade assets such as mortgage-backed securities (favouring direct intermediation), high for C&I loans needing monitoring (favouring capital injections).

## Limitations and caveats

- **The clean_md is the April 2009 draft**, not the 2011 published article: numbers, figures and sections may differ from the published version, and nothing here should be read as the published results.
- **Figures 1–6 survive only as axis ticks and labels**; magnitudes are known only from the text. Figure 6 ("Credit vs. premium policy") is not discussed in the text.
- Internal inconsistencies of the draft, reported as written: the elastic-labor results are attributed to "figure 6", but the matching figure is Figure 5; the introduction promises the normative analysis in "section 4" and concluding remarks in "section 5", but they are sections 3.3 and 4; the 6.50 result is stated as "percent of one years recession" and the next as "percent of steady state consumption"; the Taylor rule (eq. 36) uses ι coefficients while Table 1 calls them κ, and the smoothing parameter ρ has no value in Table 1; the text says the credit feedback parameter "exceeds unity", while the simulations use 50 and 500.
- Equations are legible but some fractions and the investment Q relation (eq. 28) are split across lines; the capital quality factor appears as both ξ and ψ in section 2.4, the latter also being the credit-policy share.
- The financial parameters are described as "meant to be suggestive"; the economy-wide leverage of four is "a rough guess" (the mortgage sector, about one third of assets in 2007, was between twenty and thirty to one).
- Authors' caveats: a single crisis is analysed, not a recurring rare disaster; no asset heterogeneity yet (needed to choose between credit policy and equity injections); no wage rigidity, hence a high labor supply elasticity; the simple preference structure may misstate the utility value of leisure in a recession.
