## Context and motivation

In Brazil a single variable — the Brazilian EMBI spread, the yield on dollar bonds over Treasuries — summarizes what markets think of the economy, and every financial variable moves with it: the exchange rate (through capital flows and the trade surplus needed to offset a sudden stop), the Selic (through the exchange rate's effect on inflation expectations), domestic term rates, and so the cost of servicing a public debt about half of which was dollar-denominated or dollar-indexed and much of the rest linked to the Selic. In 2002 the spread rose to a peak of 2,400 b.p. and the debt ratio jumped with the depreciation, then both reversed after the election. The fiscal theory of the price level and Sargent and Wallace's "unpleasant arithmetic" explain how an unsustainable fiscal policy can make monetary tightening perverse, but they rest on the price level balancing the budget constraint, which does not work when much of the debt is in or indexed to foreign currency. The paper asks what does the work instead, and answers: default risk. It is the companion of Blanchard's paper on the same episode, and reaches a consistent conclusion by a different route.

## Core argument / thesis

Default risk is the mechanism through which an inflation-targeting central bank in an emerging market can move from "monetary dominance" to "fiscal dominance". International financial shocks raise the EMBI spread, and how much they raise it depends on domestic fiscal policy: when the primary surplus is large enough to keep the debt ratio stable, the spread's response to global risk is muted; when it falls short, the response is amplified. If the primary surplus responds to the debt-stabilizing level ("Ricardian" rule), a Selic increase partly offsets the depreciation and inflation targeting works. If the primary surplus is fixed, a global shock can set off a vicious circle — higher Selic, higher debt service, higher debt, higher spread, more depreciation, higher expected inflation, higher Selic — in which monetary policy loses control of inflation. Brazil in 2002 had "probably fallen into a bad equilibrium" but was "just over the edge": the fiscal change announced in January 2003 was enough to reverse it.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **The spread depends on global risk, amplified by weak fiscal fundamentals (equation (1), Table 2).** The EMBI spread responds to its own lag, to the US BAA corporate spread (global risk appetite, following Calvo) and to changes in it. The response to the US spread is a logistic (LSTAR) function of the gap between the actual primary surplus and the debt-stabilizing surplus, or alternatively of the debt ratio relative to an estimated threshold: it rises non-linearly as the surplus falls short or the debt passes the threshold. Fiscal variables are instrumented (the debt-stabilizing surplus depends on the exchange rate, which moves with the spread).
- **The Selic acts on the spread only through debt.** Adding the Selic to the spread equation gives an insignificant coefficient, as do reserves and the current account: monetary policy affects default risk indirectly, via the level of debt.
- **Risk premia move together.** A policy rule on one-year-ahead expected inflation (equation (2)) is simulated forward to predict the Selic path; the gap between swap rates and that path, assuming no expectational errors, is a term premium containing policy-rate volatility and credit risk (equation (3)). Swap credit risk is smaller than bond credit risk because no principal is exchanged. An exchange rate risk premium is built from UIP with survey expectations and 12-month swaps (equation (4)). All of them, and CDS spreads, are highly correlated with the EMBI.
- **Why the exchange rate and interest differential co-move "wrongly" (equations (5)–(9)).** With a rule on expected inflation, expectations that respond to past depreciation, and UIP with a risk premium, the reduced form makes the depreciation depend negatively on the lagged interest rate and positively on the risk premium — following McCallum, a reaction function that responds to what the risk premium does to the exchange rate is one explanation of the UIP puzzle.
- **The full model (equations (5), (7), (10), (11)).** A reduced-form exchange rate equation with the interest differential and the EMBI, the expectations equation, the policy rule, the non-linear spread equation, and a fiscal rule in which the primary surplus adjusts with weight (1 − ϕ) toward the debt-stabilizing level (ϕ = 0 Ricardian, ϕ = 1 exogenous surplus). A rise in the US spread depreciates the currency; the central bank's reaction dampens it, so the net effect is ambiguous; but it also raises the debt-stabilizing surplus (depreciation inflates dollar debt, domestic rates rise). With ϕ = 0 the fiscal side absorbs the shock and the central bank keeps control of inflation; with ϕ = 1 the surplus gap widens, the spread's sensitivity rises, and the economy moves along an unstable path.
- **What carries the result**: short debt duration and a large dollar-linked share (so rates and the exchange rate feed straight into debt), the non-linear response of the spread to fiscal fundamentals, and a fiscal rule that does not respond to debt. Output is taken as given — "not an innocuous assumption" — because the evidence of an interest-rate effect on Brazilian output is weak.

## Main results / findings

- **Non-linearity confirmed (Table 2)**: the debt threshold above which the spread's sensitivity to the US corporate spread increases is estimated at about 54 per cent of GDP, the level of net public debt in April 2002, stable when the sample starts in 1999; in the shorter sample the elasticity is significantly higher. The result survives replacing the US corporate spread with US long rates or the Federal Funds rate.
- **The BCB rule is aggressive and inertial (Table 3, 2000:01 to 2004:01)**: the response of the Selic to expected inflation deviations is 4.03, greater than unity, so real rates rise with expected inflation; persistence is 0.92. The output gap and the exchange rate change add nothing (Favero and Giavazzi 2002), and on daily data neither the EMBI nor expected depreciation enters.
- **Default risk is in all domestic premia (Table 4, 1999:2-2003:1)**: the correlation of swap term premia with the EMBI rises with maturity, high at 12 months although the EMBI is on 10-year bonds; the 12-month exchange rate risk premium is also highly correlated with it, "indicating that default risk is an important determinant of exchange rate fluctuations". At the October 2002 peak the 12-month swap carried a large risk premium on top of falling expected Selic rates; by May 2003 it had fallen to 275 b.p.
- **Exchange rate equation (Table 5, 1999:02 to 2003:12)**: partial effects of the expected sign — a one percent increase in the Selic appreciates the exchange rate by 0.16 percent, a higher EMBI depreciates it. Because both are endogenous, the net effect of policy cannot be read from this equation; in the bad equilibrium the default-risk response can dominate.
- **Simulations from June 2002 (Figure 4)**: under the fixed-surplus rule — the one actually followed from 1999 to the 2002 election, with no reaction of the surplus to the debt-stabilizing level — the Selic rises above 30 percent, the spread rises, tightening cannot stop the depreciation, expected inflation settles around 12 percent and the debt ratio explodes. Under an "almost Ricardian" rule (ϕ = 0.75) the spread stabilizes after the initial shock and monetary policy stabilizes expected inflation, at the cost of a temporarily tighter primary surplus. Inflation is not explosive even in the bad case, because the non-linearity eventually dies out.

## Limitations and caveats

- **Authors' caveats**: output is held fixed in a short-run model; the simulation "clearly defies the Lucas critique"; impulse responses are uninformative because the non-linearity drives the difference between regimes; the term premium measure assumes no expectational errors; the conclusion that Brazil was in the bad equilibrium is stated as a belief ("we believe we have identified") about "at least a short period during 2002".
- **What the text shows**: the pre-1999 sample is judged of limited use because both policy regimes changed; the debt-level version of Table 2 uses data from 1991. The claim that the EMBI measures default probability treats it as uncontaminated by risk aversion, which the paper models only through the US corporate spread.
- **Inconsistencies in the source**: the text gives a correlation of 0.70 between the 12-month swap term premium and the EMBI, while Table 4 prints 0.82; the simulation description says expectations rise "In June 2003" although the simulation starts in June 2002; section 4.3 says the surplus would not need to rise so much "for ϕ = 1.0", although ϕ = 1 is defined as the exogenous-surplus case; the October 2002 decomposition prints the total spread and the risk premium as "1.179 b.p." and "1.315 points", whose units cannot be read with confidence. All flagged as written; the October figures are not used above.
- **Damaged passages**: the logistic transition function after (1) and its debt-level variant are partly reduced to stray symbols (described in words above); Table 2's first panel lacks a value for γ0 and its last entries ("4.50 (01.10)", "2, 05") cannot be attributed to γ3 and σ with certainty; Table 4's header row is spaced letter by letter. Figures 1–4 survive only as axis values and series labels, and the text carries "[Insert here Figure …]" placeholders, so the simulated paths are known only from the prose.
