## Context and motivation

The standard account of US monetary spillovers is "yield-oriented": a US tightening narrows rate differentials and pulls capital to the US, and a floating exchange rate absorbs the shock through expenditure switching (Mundell-Fleming). Rey (2013) instead argues that the global financial cycle reaches countries regardless of the exchange rate regime. Kalemli-Özcan argues the transmission has changed — EME capital flows are now mostly private and intermediated by domestic banks — and that US policy spills over mainly by changing global investors' risk perceptions. She uses quarterly data since 1996 (capital flows for 55 EMEs and 14 advanced economies; policy rates for 79 EMEs), excluding hard pegs.

## Core argument / thesis

US monetary policy affects credit costs in other countries "through its effect on global investors' risk perceptions" — **international risk spillovers**. EME capital flows are especially risk-sensitive, so a US tightening raises country risk premia, widens EME rate differentials and local credit spreads, and drives UIP deviations. Domestic monetary policy in EMEs is weakened because policy rates pass through imperfectly to deposit and lending rates, the wedge being the risk premium. Hence, unlike Rey, she argues exchange rate flexibility *does* help: because risk premia show up as a wedge in UIP, defending the currency requires a much larger rate rise that tightens financial conditions and raises country risk. "Emerging markets' monetary policy actions designed to limit exchange rate volatility can be counterproductive"; the case for flexible rates "is stronger under international risk spillovers".

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Stylized facts (II)**: policy-rate differentials vis-à-vis the US are much higher and more dispersed for EMEs (Chart 1). Debt flows are 67 percent of external liabilities in advanced economies (AEs) vs. 54 percent in EMEs (1996–2018). Flow–differential co-movement switched from sovereign- to private-driven flows, which are more sensitive to the VIX.
- **Panel regressions (II.A)**: capital inflows/GDP on policy-rate differentials, log VIX (global risk), log EMBI spread (country risk) and growth differentials, with country fixed effects, 46 EMEs and 13 AEs, 1996q1–2018q4. Omitting risk biases the differential coefficient down, because risk lowers inflows and is positively correlated with differentials.
- **Exogenous US shocks (II.B)**: local projections with instrumental variables (Jordà; Stock and Watson), the US 3-month (or 12-month) Treasury rate instrumented with the Gertler-Karadi FF4 surprise in Fed funds futures, controlling for lags and for the domestic policy response.
- **Simple framework (III)**: output = domestic demand + net exports; demand falls with the domestic rate and, through balance-sheet effects on dollar debt, with depreciation (f); net exports rise with depreciation (b, expenditure switching). Three key equations: a "risk-adjusted UIP" (2), with the exchange rate depending on the rate differential (coefficient d) and the country risk premium λ; λ rising with the US rate plus a country-specific premium γ (3); and an imperfect pass-through (4) in which the domestic rate equals the policy rate plus λ. A US tightening is then contractionary at home even with strong expenditure switching, provided d > 1 or d < 1/2; the paper estimates d > 1 once risk is controlled for.
- **UIP deviations (V.A)**: the deviation is decomposed (13) into an interest-differential part and an exchange-rate-adjustment part (following Kalemli-Özcan and Varela 2019).
- **Policy (VII)**: managed vs. free floats compared using the Ilzetzki et al. (2017) classification (codes 2–3 managed, 4–6 free); macroprudential limits on unhedged FX liabilities; institutional quality to lower country risk.

## Main results / findings

- **Flows and risk (Table 1)**: in EMEs the differential coefficient is −0.07 (no risk controls), −0.04 with the VIX, and turns positive, 0.28, only once the EMBI is added (which makes the VIX insignificant); in AEs it is 0.32 (insignificant) and 0.63 with the VIX. A 10 percentage point higher differential implies inflows higher by 2.8 points of GDP in EMEs and 6.3 in AEs (41 and 79 percent of the sample mean). The VIX–EMBI correlation is 30%; the VIX–policy differential correlation is 45% in EMEs and 32% in AEs (Chart 5).
- **US shock → differentials (Charts 6–8)**: after a 1 percentage point US tightening, EME 12-month government bond differentials rise by 2.5 points after three quarters (2.0 with the 12-month Treasury as instrument), while AE differentials fall by about 0.5 after one quarter and 1.7 after six. Adding the EMBI makes the EME response insignificant — the rise is country risk. Other countries' policy is not "on hold" when the Fed moves. Long-term (10-year) differentials respond the same way (Chart 22).
- **Imperfect pass-through (Tables 2–3)**: regressing 3-month and 12-month deposit rates and 12-month lending rates on the policy rate gives 0.7, 0.5 and 0.8 in EMEs (β = 1 rejected) vs. 0.9, 0.9 and 1.0 in AEs; adding the VIX and EMBI raises the EME coefficients to 0.9. The implied negative correlation between risk and policy rates means EMEs on average loosen in risk-off episodes and tighten in risk-on ones.
- **Flows and spreads (Table 4)**: inflows lower lending spreads in EMEs (−0.025, and −0.027 controlling for the policy rate) and in AEs (−0.012), but in AEs the effect vanishes once the policy rate is controlled (−0.001): in EMEs capital flows move credit costs beyond what monetary policy does.
- **Exchange rates (Table 5, Charts 10–12)**: higher differentials go with depreciation in EMEs (1.07) unless capital flows are controlled (−2.33, appreciation); expected depreciation coefficients (0.26 to 0.38) are well below one. In AEs UIP deviations co-move with exchange rate adjustment; in EMEs with interest rate differentials (Chart 11). A 1 point US tightening raises the EME UIP deviation (excess return on local currency) by about 3 points, not in AEs.
- **Economic significance (VI)**: cites estimates that US policy explains 4 to 17 percent of VIX variation (Rey; Miranda-Agrippino and Rey), up to 30 percent (Bruno and Shin), over 20 percent of risk perceptions (Bekaert et al.). Benchmarking to the ECB rate, the VIX–differential correlation drops to 0.15 for EMEs, suggesting "a special role for U.S. monetary policy".
- **Managed vs. free floats (VII.A–B, Chart 13, Chart 14, Table 6)**: the VIX–policy-divergence correlation is 0.414 under managed floats and 0.092 under free floats. A 1% US tightening depreciates EME currencies by 15.7% after 8 quarters (6% for AEs); controlling for the domestic policy response, the depreciation fades in about four quarters. Lagged log VIX lowers GDP growth in managed floats (−0.005) and only weakly in free floats (−0.004, 10%); a one-standard-deviation VIX rise cuts quarterly growth by 0.2 percent in managed floats, a "Lehman-like" 133 percent rise by 0.8 percent; for EMEs with free floats the effect is insignificant (Table 13).
- **FX debt and leverage (VII.C, Table 7)**: with ORBIS firm data for 42 countries, firm leverage falls with the VIX everywhere and with depreciation only in high-FX-debt countries; a "Lehman-like" VIX rise lowers leverage by 5.3% (high FX debt) and 2.6% (low FX debt). Risk spillovers work "even in the absence of balance sheet effects". FX debt is 14.3% of corporate debt in frequent managers of the exchange rate vs. 1.9% in free floaters — limiting volatility can raise FX borrowing.
- **Policy conclusion (VII.D, VIII)**: deal with unhedged FX debt directly through macroprudential policy "rather than to try to use a relatively blunt instrument like monetary policy"; structurally, reduce country risk by improving institutions (property rights, corruption, government stability, "an emphasis on central bank independence") to attract long-term, less risk-sensitive flows.

## Limitations and caveats

- **Inconsistencies as printed**: section VI.A restates the EME differential response as 2.3 points "after three quarters" and the UIP response as 3 points "after three quarters", while II.B and V.B give 2.5 (or 2.0) points and "about 3%p after 2 quarters"; the AE count is 13 in Chart 1's notes but 14 in the text; the VIX–differential correlation for AEs is 32% in section II but "the original 0.35" in VI.B, and the ECB-benchmark AE correlation is −0.18 in the text but −0.266 in Chart 21.
- **Garbled notation**: regression equations print with "(cid:…)" glyphs and subscripts split across lines, and the solved output equation (5) and the bounds in (10) lose their fraction layout; the model is described here in words. Charts survive only as axis labels and printed correlations; impulse-response paths are known only from the text.
- The paper notes it does not test "monetary autonomy" or compare fixed and flexible regimes, only managed vs. free floats; the growth regressions use lagged VIX rather than local projections for lack of observations; the leverage split assumes domestic leverage is in local currency and external leverage in foreign currency.
