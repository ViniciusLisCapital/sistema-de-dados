## Context and motivation

Opinion on monetary policy has swung between extremes: the 1920s belief in Fed "fine tuning", the post-Depression view that money did not matter (liquidity trap, interest-insensitive spending, fiscal policy as the alternative), and a revival driven by the failure of postwar cheap-money policies (the Federal Reserve–Treasury Accord in 1951, bond-price pegging formally abandoned in 1953), the Haberler–Pigou wealth channel, the re-evaluation of 1929–33 (the quantity of money fell by one-third because the Fed "forced or permitted a sharp reduction in the monetary base") and disillusionment with discretionary fiscal policy. Friedman fears the pendulum "may well have swung too far": monetary policy is now asked to do things it cannot do, which prevents it from making the contribution it can. The address sets out what it cannot do, what it can do, and how it should be conducted.

## Core argument / thesis

The monetary authority controls nominal quantities and can peg a nominal quantity (an exchange rate, the price level, nominal income, a monetary total) or its rate of change, but "It cannot use its control over nominal quantities to peg a real quantity" — the real interest rate, the unemployment rate, real income. Attempts to hold the market rate of interest or unemployment below its "natural" rate succeed only temporarily and only by accelerating inflation: "there is always a temporary trade-off between inflation and unemployment; there is no permanent trade-off." What monetary policy can do is keep money from being a source of disturbance, provide a stable background (a predictable price level), and offset only major disturbances. It should be guided by a magnitude it controls — a monetary total — and avoid sharp swings, ideally by a publicly stated steady rate of monetary growth.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Interest rate pegging (Section I)**: buying securities to lower rates raises reserves and money; the initial liquidity effect lowers rates, but faster money growth then raises spending and income (raising liquidity preference and loan demand) and may raise prices (lowering real money), reversing the fall "in something less than a year" and returning rates to their former level in "a year or two", likely overshooting. A fourth effect — the Fisher price-expectation effect — makes faster money growth correspond to **higher** nominal rates; it is slow to develop and disappear (Fisher estimated "several decades" for full adjustment). Hence high and rising rates go with rapid money growth (Brazil, Chile, the recent United States) and low rates with slow growth (Switzerland, the United States 1929 to 1933); interest rates are "a misleading indicator" of whether policy is tight or easy, and the rate of change of money is better (footnote 2: in principle it is money supply relative to demand, but demand is empirically highly stable).
- **Wicksell plus Fisher**: the authority can make the market rate less than the natural rate "only by inflation" and higher only by deflation; once inflation is anticipated the nominal natural rate itself rises, so holding the market rate down requires ever faster inflation.
- **Natural rate of unemployment**: the level consistent with equilibrium in the structure of real wage rates, "ground out by the Walrasian system of general equilibrium equations" with the actual structural characteristics of labor and commodity markets (imperfections, stochastic variability, information and mobility costs). It need not equal unemployed = vacancies (footnote 3), is not immutable, and is raised by man-made features (minimum wages, the Walsh-Healy and Davis-Bacon Acts, union strength).
- **The Phillips curve's defect**: Phillips failed to distinguish nominal from real wages, as Wicksell failed to distinguish nominal from real interest rates. Restated in terms of anticipated real wages "it all falls into place"; a nominal-wage Phillips curve is stable only for periods with stable anticipated inflation, and its level rises with the average rate of price change (footnote 5).
- **Dynamics of an unemployment target below the natural rate** (a 3 per cent target in the example): faster money growth first raises output and employment because product prices respond faster than factor prices — real wages fall ex post for employers while anticipated real wages rise for employees. As anticipations adjust, real wages return toward their initial level and unemployment goes back; holding it at 3 per cent requires accelerating inflation; a target above the natural rate leads to accelerating deflation.
- **Targeting the natural rate itself fails too**: the authority cannot know it, it changes, and responding to every deviation of the market rate makes money growth "analogous to a random walk".

## Main results / findings

- **Temporary vs. permanent trade-off**: the temporary trade-off comes "not from inflation per se, but from unanticipated inflation, which generally means, from a rising rate of inflation"; "A rising rate of inflation may reduce unemployment, a high rate will not."
- **How long is temporary** (a personal judgment, no systematic evidence for unemployment): the initial effects of a higher unanticipated inflation last "something like two to five years", then reverse; full adjustment takes "say, a couple of decades", as for interest rates. For much larger changes, as in South America, adjustment is "greatly speeded up".
- **Brazilian illustration**: when Brazilians anticipated prices rising "more than 75 per cent a year", wages had to rise at that rate to keep real wages unchanged; when Brazil brought the price rise down to "about 45 per cent a year" there was "a sharp initial rise in unemployment" because wages kept rising faster than the new rate of price rise — the expected result of any attempt to reduce inflation below that widely anticipated.
- **What monetary policy can do (Section II)**: (1) prevent money itself from being a major source of disturbance — every major U.S. contraction was produced or greatly exacerbated by monetary disorder, every major inflation produced by monetary expansion; (2) provide a stable background, conserving the limited flexibility of prices and wages for relative-price adjustment (a gold standard or a pegged exchange rate without sterilization could do this, but subordinates the country to other authorities' policies); (3) offset major disturbances only — "clear and present danger" — since we cannot recognize minor ones or predict the effects of offsetting them.
- **How to conduct it (Section III)**: (1) guide policy by magnitudes the authority controls; taking interest rates or unemployment as the criterion is "like a space vehicle that has taken a fix on the wrong star". Among controllable guides, exchange rates are undesirable for the United States (better to let floating rates adjust the 5 per cent of resources in trade and keep policy for the 95 per cent); the price level is most important in its own right but its link to policy actions is indirect, with long and variable lags, risking "false stops and starts"; so "a monetary total is the best currently available immediate guide", and "it matters much less which particular total is chosen than that one be chosen." (2) Avoid sharp swings: "Too late and too much has been the general practice" (1966; also 1919 and 1920, 1937 and 1938, 1953 and 1954, 1959 and 1960), because authorities act on today's conditions while effects come "six or nine or twelve or fifteen months later."
- **Prescription**: publicly adopt a steady rate of growth in a specified monetary total; Friedman's estimate for rough price stability is "something like a 3 to 5 per cent per year rate of growth in currency plus all commercial bank deposits" or slightly lower for currency plus demand deposits; footnote 6 suggests "something like 2 per cent" might be better still, per his unpublished "The Optimum Quantity of Money". A steady rate producing moderate inflation or deflation is preferable to erratic swings. Periods of stable money growth have been periods of relative economic stability, in the United States and elsewhere.

## Limitations and caveats

- **OCR noise throughout**, mostly split or joined words ("VolumeL VIII", "occurredd espite", "onalyb y", "acceleratin(gi nflation"). Goldenweiser's interest rate prints as "a 212 per cent interest rate", where a fraction has been lost; read the clean_md text rather than this number. Footnote 6's marker prints as "a", footnote 4's marker sits on its own line, and page headers ("FRIEDMAN: MONETARYP OLICY") are interleaved with the text.
- **No reference list in the file**: bracketed citations ([1]–[7], e.g. Mill [7, p. 488], Goldenweiser [4, p. 1 17]) cannot be resolved from the clean_md.
- The address is verbal: no model is written down, and the timing of the unemployment effects is explicitly "a personal judgment", with no systematic evidence offered for employment (unlike interest rates).
- Friedman's own caveats: the natural rate is not estimable "accurately and readily" and changes over time; the timing estimates apply to inflation changes of the U.S. order of magnitude; the case against price-level guidance is stated for "the present stage of our understanding".
