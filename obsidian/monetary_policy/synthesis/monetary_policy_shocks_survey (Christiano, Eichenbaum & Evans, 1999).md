## Context and motivation

Evaluating changes in monetary institutions or rules requires structural models, and there are many competing ones. The chapter follows the "Lucas program" (Lucas 1980): subject models to shocks "for which we are fairly certain how actual economies ... would react". Monetary policy shocks are good candidates because different models respond very differently to them. The program has three steps: isolate policy shocks in actual economies, characterize the economy's response, and run the same experiment in the models. Focusing on policy *actions* would not do, because actions partly reflect responses to non-monetary developments. Three identification strategies exist: estimating the Fed's feedback rule (the chapter's main focus), reading data that signal exogenous policy actions (Romer & Romer's narrative records, funds-rate changes in some periods, or all money movements), and long-run neutrality restrictions (not discussed in detail).

## Core argument / thesis

The literature has not converged on one set of identifying assumptions, but "there is considerable agreement about the qualitative effects of a monetary policy shock": after a contractionary shock "short term interest rates rise, aggregate output, employment, profits and various monetary aggregates fall, the aggregate price level responds very slowly, and various measures of wages fall, albeit by very modest amounts". Policy shocks account for only a modest share of output volatility and even less of the price level. Identification schemes that yield impulse responses inconsistent with *every* model one wishes to discriminate between should be rejected — in practice, a set of sign and shape restrictions. The key contribution of the shock literature "may be this: it has clarified the mapping from identification assumptions to inference about the effects of monetary policy shocks." The findings say nothing about the systematic component of policy (footnote 4, section 4.6).

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Policy shock (section 2, equation 2.1)**: the instrument St (funds rate or a monetary aggregate) equals a linear function f of the information set Ωt plus σs·εts. Three interpretations of the shock: shifts in the preferences of the monetary authority (FOMC members' weights on unemployment and inflation), strategic considerations that make expectation shocks self-fulfilling (Ball 1995; Chari, Christiano & Eichenbaum 1998), and technical factors such as measurement error in preliminary data (Bernanke & Mihov 1995; equations 2.2–2.4).
- **VAR identification (section 3)**: the reduced-form VAR (3.1) maps to structural shocks through A0 (3.2–3.3); impulse responses need A0 (3.6), and the data give only k(k + 1)/2 equations for k² unknowns. With uncorrelated shocks (D = I, 3.7), identification requires linear restrictions plus positive diagonal elements; the order and rank conditions give only local identification, and global identification must be checked case by case. A worked example shows the order and sign conditions can hold while the system is not identified.
- **Recursiveness assumption (section 4.1)**: the policy shock is orthogonal to the variables in the Fed's information set — the time-t variables the Fed sees do not respond to the time-t shock. Equivalent to OLS residuals of St on Ωt, mapped into a VAR with the block-triangular A0 of (4.1). Proposition 4.1: the family of admissible A0 is not unique, but every member gives the same response to the policy shock, and with the lower-triangular normalization the response is invariant to the ordering within the blocks before and after St. The responses to non-policy shocks "simply reflect normalizations".
- **Three benchmark schemes (section 4.2)**, quarterly, 1965:3–1995:2: Ωt has current and four lags of real GDP, the GDP deflator and a commodity price index (PCOM), and four lags of the funds rate, nonborrowed reserves, total reserves and money. The **FF** shock takes St = funds rate; the **NBR** shock takes St = nonborrowed reserves (innovations to broader aggregates mostly reflect money demand); the **NBR/TR** shock (Strongin 1995) adds current total reserves to Ωt, since reserve demand is interest-inelastic in the short run. Output and prices are assumed not to move in the impact period. Confidence intervals by bootstrap with 500 draws (footnote 23).
- **Federal funds market model (section 4.5.2)**: Bernanke & Mihov's demand for total reserves (4.4), demand for borrowed reserves (4.5) with a coefficient γ on nonborrowed reserves, and an NBR rule (4.6–4.7) that responds to reserve-demand shocks. Each benchmark scheme is a special case (restrictions 4.15, 4.20, 4.24), exactly identified, hence untestable without an extra restriction.
- **Non-recursive system (section 5, Sims & Zha 1998)**: the Fed sees only crude materials prices and money contemporaneously (5.1); a money demand equation with unit coefficients on prices and output (5.2); goods-market variables respond to the policy shock only through crude materials prices (5.3). The policy-shock responses are identified even though the last five equations are not.
- **Narrative approach (section 7)**: Romer & Romer (1989) episodes (December 1968; April 1974; August 1978; October 1979), plus the 1966 credit crunch (Kashyap et al.) and August 1988 (Oliner & Rudebusch), entered as a dummy in the VAR (7.1); and the Boschen & Mills index, a discrete rating from -2 (very tight) to +2 (very loose) read from FOMC minutes.

## Main results / findings

**The benchmark shocks (section 4.2.1).** FF and NBR shocks have correlation 0.51; the standard deviation of FF shocks is 0.71 at an annual rate, of NBR shocks 1.53%, with an implied funds-rate shock of 0.39. Policy was relatively tight before each recession and eased around the trough. Correlations are 0.65 between NBR/TR and FF shocks and 0.82 between NBR/TR and NBR shocks (footnote 27). Two versions of the FF model (M1 level vs. M2 growth) give shocks correlated only 0.85 — in 1993Q4 a 20 basis point versus an 80 basis point contraction — yet the same inference: "while inference about the qualitative effects of a monetary policy shock appears to be reliable, inference about the state of monetary policy at any particular date is not."

**Responses to a contractionary FF shock (Figure 2).** A persistent rise in the funds rate and drop in nonborrowed reserves (a strong liquidity effect); total reserves barely move at first and eventually fall "by roughly 0.3 percent", the Fed insulating them by raising borrowed reserves; M2 drops at once and persistently; after a delay of 2 quarters real GDP declines in a hump with "the maximal decline occurring roughly a year to a year and a half after the policy shock"; commodity prices fall after a delay; the GDP deflator "is flat for roughly a year and a half after which it declines". Implied short-run money demand elasticities are roughly -0.1 (M1) and -1.0 (NBR), against -8.0 for the long-run analogue in Lucas (1988). NBR and NBR/TR shocks give the same picture except for the impact response of total reserves and M1. Results are robust to monthly data (Figure 3, payroll employment and the PCE deflator).

**Other aggregates (section 4.3, a review of variants).** Unemployment rises after about two quarters; retail sales and profits fall immediately while manufacturing inventories rise; residential investment falls first and most (Fisher 1997); small firms account for a disproportionate share of the fall in manufacturing sales (Gertler & Gilchrist 1994); manufacturing employment falls through job destruction (Campbell 1997). Real wages fall modestly, which casts doubt on models stressing nominal wage stickiness per se; profits drop sharply, which casts doubt on models stressing sticky prices without labor market frictions. On credit: deposits fall at once, banks first sell securities and then loans fall (Bernanke & Blinder 1992); consumer and real estate loans fall but commercial and industrial loans do not; business net borrowing rises for roughly a year before falling (Christiano et al. 1996a). On exchange rates: a US tightening brings persistent appreciation and "significant, persistent deviations from uncovered interest rate parity in favor of US investments" (Eichenbaum & Evans 1995); for non-US G7 countries recursive shocks give an initial depreciation unless identification changes (Grilli & Roubini 1995), and non-recursive schemes give appreciation (Cushman & Zha 1997; Kim & Roubini 1995; Clarida & Gertler 1997).

**Robustness (section 4.4).**
- Excluding current output from Ωt makes a contraction raise output before lowering it; with no coherent model for that, the authors reject the assumption as confounding policy and non-policy shocks. Excluding the price level changes nothing.
- **Price puzzle (Figure 5)**: schemes that set St = funds rate and omit commodity prices from Ωt imply a rise in the price level lasting several years — Sims's conjecture is that such shocks are confounded with news of future inflation available to the Fed. Including commodity prices largely removes it; the puzzle is statistically significant for the funds-rate shocks in columns 1 and 2 (the share of bootstrap draws with a positive average price response over 2, 4 and 6 quarters is 96.4%, 97.2% and 98.0% for column 1; footnote 41), less severe for NBR shocks, and nearly absent for the benchmarks.
- **Money-based shocks (Figure 6)**: M0 and M1 shocks give imprecise responses (M0 point estimates fit a simple neoclassical inflation-tax model; M1 point estimates fit no known model); M2 shocks resemble the benchmarks except that NBR and M2 move in opposite directions after a delay.
- **Federal funds futures (section 4.4.4)**: a shock built from the funds rate minus the previous month's futures rate (Rudebusch sample 1989:04–1995:03) has a standard deviation of 0.16 against 0.22 for the benchmark, and correlation 0.34 (an R² of 0.12), rising to approximately 0.45 when estimated over the same sample. Over that sample both shocks are small and responses imprecise, so there is "no evidence to support the notion that inference is sensitive to incorporating federal funds market data".
- **Subsamples (section 4.4.5, Table 1)**: responses are much smaller after 1988 (the "vanishing liquidity effect"), but the evidence fits the hypothesis that only the size of the shocks changed — larger in the early 1980s, 20 versus 12 basis points in the second and fourth subsamples. Full-sample liquidity effect: FF/NBR -0.94 and FF/M1 -2.17 for FF shocks — a one percent policy-induced rise in nonborrowed reserves lowers the funds rate by roughly one percentage point; NBR shocks give smaller estimates (-0.23, -1.14). Differences across subsamples are within sampling uncertainty.

**Discriminating between schemes (section 4.5).** Coleman, Gilles & Labadie's reading of a negative NBR shock as a positive money-demand shock or as news of future monetary expansion is rejected because total reserves, M1 and M2 fall for over two years. Bernanke & Mihov's test rests on γ = 0, which theory (Goodfriend's borrowed-reserve model, 4.28) and evidence contradict: lagged NBR helps forecast the funds rate (F-statistic 3.48, none of 500 bootstrap draws exceeds it). Unrestricted, the estimated γ is positive and significant in the NBR model (0.481 on BM's monthly data, Table 2a) and all sign restrictions hold, so "BM's claim to have rejected the benchmark NBR model is unwarranted"; the FF model is not rejected (p-value 0.052) but its estimated reserve-demand elasticity is negative.

**Volatility (section 4.6).** FF shocks account for 21%, 44% and 38% of the 4, 8 and 12 quarter ahead forecast error variance of output; NBR shocks only 7%, 10% and 8%; NBR/TR shocks are in between. No measure explains much of the price level even at three years. Policy shocks explain little of total reserves and M1 under FF and NBR/TR, and never more than 50% of M2 — against identification strategies that treat monetary aggregates as dominated by policy shocks. Small shock contributions do not imply a small role for systematic policy.

**Sims–Zha (section 5.1.2, Figure 11).** M2 growth falls, the T-bill rate rises, prices and output decline after a delay, real wages barely move — qualitatively like the benchmarks. Impact on the policy rate is about 40 basis points against about 70 for an FF shock. Making goods-market variables predetermined leaves inference "virtually unaffected".

**Estimated rules are hard to interpret (section 6).** When the policymaker reacts to data different from the econometrician's (real-time measurement error, an innovation, or a forecast as in Clarida, Galí & Gertler), the estimated coefficients convolve behavioral parameters with projection parameters — yet the policy shock can still be correctly identified. That is why the chapter reports impulse responses, not rules.

**Narrative approach (section 7, Figures 12–14).** With one exception (October 1979 for NBR) each Romer episode is followed within one or two quarters by contractionary FF and NBR shocks, but the VAR finds many contractions the Romers do not. Responses to a Romer episode are qualitatively like an FF shock, larger (maximal funds-rate impact roughly 100 basis points against roughly 60) and less precise — little evidence against no effect on output, unlike Romer & Romer's own significant results. The Boschen & Mills index delays the funds-rate peak to 14 months and shows a price puzzle. Overall, qualitative inference is "quite robust" to these measures.

**Using the results in models (section 8).** Models solved with estimated reaction functions often have multiple equilibria. Christiano et al. (1997b) propose instead an "exogenous monetary policy rule": the estimated response of money growth to a policy shock, under which the model's responses should match the VAR's if the model is right. The authors are "open minded but skeptical" about using identified shocks to infer the effect of changing the systematic part of policy without a structural model, since that ignores the Lucas critique.

## Limitations and caveats

- **The reference list is not in the file**: it ends at the conclusion on page 145, where the contents place the references.
- **Tables 3 and 4 (variance decompositions, pages 125–126) are unreadable** — only stray characters survive. The shares quoted above come from the text, which gives output only for the FF and NBR measures; the NBR/TR, M0, M1 and M2 figures cannot be read.
- **All figures (1–14)** survive only as panel titles and scattered tick marks; the responses described above come from the text and captions.
- **Equations are damaged by OCR**: the definition of the solution set (3.8) is missing; the A0 matrices (4.1), (5.3) and (5.4), the money market solution (4.8)–(4.11) and the parameter mappings (4.16)–(4.27) are scrambled into symbols, and (4.28) and the money demand equation in section 4.2.2.1 are partly garbled.
- **Table 1 has lost signs and characters**: some intervals lack minus signs, and the FF/NBR entry for 79:10–94:12 is printed as 0.71 with an interval that is negative throughout; sample labels are corrupted ("65:01~4:12", "79:1044:12"). Table 2a's β entry for the restricted NBR model carries an OCR artifact. Read entries against the text.
- The VARs are US only, quarterly 1965–1995 (monthly variants); the open-economy and disaggregated results in section 4.3 are reported from other papers with different samples and identification, and the reader is referred to them for details.
- Authors' own caveats: measurement error as the source of shocks can violate the recursiveness assumption (section 2); the recursive assumption that output and prices do not respond within the quarter is "certainly arguable"; Romer dates are treated as fixed in the bootstrap; the funds futures comparison is limited by the short sample.
