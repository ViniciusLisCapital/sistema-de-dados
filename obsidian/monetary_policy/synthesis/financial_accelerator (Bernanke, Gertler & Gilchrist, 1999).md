## Context and motivation

Both the real business cycle model and IS-LM adopt the Modigliani–Miller assumptions, so financial structure is "both indeterminate and irrelevant to real economic outcomes"; apart from the term structure, credit-market conditions do not affect the real economy. An alternative tradition from Fisher and Keynes treats deteriorating credit conditions (insolvencies, real debt burdens, collapsing asset prices, bank failures) as a cause and not only a reflection of downturns — the Great Depression, Scandinavia, Latin America, Japan, East Asia, the slow US recovery from 1990–1991. The chapter aims to bring credit-market imperfections into a standard model "in a relatively straightforward yet rigorous way", for two reasons: frictions may explain how small shocks (modest real-rate changes, oil price movements) have large real effects and why spreads and credit move over the cycle, and the micro evidence on consumption, investment, inventories and employment assigns a large role to balance-sheet factors.

## Core argument / thesis

The model exhibits a **financial accelerator**: "endogenous developments in credit markets work to amplify and propagate shocks to the macroeconomy." The key link is between the **external finance premium** (the cost of external funds over the opportunity cost of internal funds) and borrowers' **net worth** (liquid assets plus collateral value of illiquid assets less outstanding obligations): with financing needs held constant, the premium depends inversely on net worth because agency costs rise when borrowers contribute little of their own wealth. Procyclical net worth (profits, asset prices) makes the premium countercyclical, enhancing swings in borrowing, investment and production. Embedded in a Dynamic New Keynesian (DNK) model with money, Calvo price stickiness, investment delays and heterogeneous firms, the mechanism has "a significant influence on business cycle dynamics" under reasonable parametrizations.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Agents (section 2)**: households (infinitely lived; work, consume, hold money and deposits); risk-neutral entrepreneurs with a constant survival probability (finite horizons, so they never become fully self-financed) who buy the whole capital stock each period with net worth plus borrowing; retailers, monopolistic competitors who differentiate wholesale goods costlessly and are the only source of nominal stickiness; a government running fiscal and monetary policy. Separating retailers from entrepreneurs keeps capital demand linear in net worth, which allows aggregation.
- **Contract (section 3, Appendix A)**: Townsend's costly state verification — lenders pay an auditing (bankruptcy) cost, a fraction of the realized payoff, to observe a borrower's idiosyncratic return. The optimal contract is standard risky debt with a default threshold; the intermediary diversifies idiosyncratic risk and earns the riskless rate, and the risk-neutral entrepreneur bears all aggregate risk, so the loan rate and default probabilities rise when the realized return to capital disappoints (countercyclical loan rates). Only non-rationing equilibria are considered.
- **Capital demand (eqs. 3.8–3.9)**: capital expenditure is proportional to net worth, with a factor increasing in the expected return to capital over the safe rate; equivalently, the ratio of the cost of external finance to the safe rate (the external finance premium) falls as the share of capital financed by own net worth rises. Constant returns make the proportionality factor independent of firm-specific factors, so it aggregates (eq. 4.5).
- **Net worth dynamics (eqs. 4.7–4.10, 4.13)**: net worth is surviving entrepreneurs' equity plus a small entrepreneurial wage. An unexpected one percent change in the return to capital changes equity by the ratio of gross capital holdings to equity — above one for leveraged firms — so asset-price surprises move net worth more than proportionately, and in general equilibrium there is "a kind of multiplier effect": higher asset prices raise net worth, which raises investment, which raises asset prices further. Adjustment costs in capital production make the price of capital variable, so asset prices feed net worth (as in Kiyotaki & Moore 1997).
- **Log-linearized model (section 4.2, eqs. 4.14–4.27)**: four blocks — aggregate demand (resource constraint, consumption Euler equation with unit intertemporal elasticity, investment arbitrage with the premium depending on net worth relative to the value of capital, eq. 4.17), aggregate supply (production, labor market, a forward-looking Calvo Phillips curve with prices set before aggregate shocks, following Bernanke & Woodford 1997), state variables (capital and net worth, the latter weighted by the capital-to-net-worth ratio), and a policy rule in which the nominal rate responds to lagged inflation and the lagged rate (eq. 4.25), plus AR processes for government spending and technology. Without frictions eq. 4.17 collapses to the expected return on capital equal to the riskless rate.
- **Extensions (section 4.2.1)**: investment chosen j periods in advance (j = 1 in the simulations), so asset prices respond at once and investment with a delay; two sectors with different access to credit combined by a CES aggregator, sector-specific capital and adjustment costs.
- **Calibration (section 5.1)**: discount factor 0.99, labor supply elasticity 3.0, capital share 0.35, household labor share 0.64 (entrepreneurial labor 0.01), depreciation 0.025, G/Y 0.2, technology and government shock persistence 1.0 and 0.95, elasticity of the price of capital to the investment-capital ratio 0.25 (reasonable range 0.0 to 0.50; King & Wolman's 2.0 rejected as implausibly high). Financial targets: a risk spread of two hundred basis points (historical prime minus six-month bill), an annualized business failure rate of three percent, a capital-to-net-worth ratio of 2 (leverage 0.5); hence an entrepreneurial death rate of 0.0272 (quarterly), idiosyncratic log-productivity variance 0.28, bankruptcy cost share 0.12 (Carlstrom & Fuerst use 0.20). Calvo non-adjustment probability 0.75 (four quarters between price changes); policy rule smoothing 0.9 and inflation coefficient 0.11, a long-run rise of one hundred and ten basis points per hundred basis points of inflation.

## Main results / findings

- **Partial equilibrium (Figure 1)**: in the calibrated contract a firm can self-finance capital up to K < 4.6; with expected returns 2 percentage points above the risk-free rate it chooses K = 9.2, a leverage ratio of 50%. A 15% increase in net worth shifts the cost-of-funds curve right, lowers the premium at the old capital level and expands capital demand.
- **VAR evidence (Figure 2)**: quarterly VAR (GDP, deflator, commodity prices, funds rate, then a spread). After a funds-rate shock output declines after about two quarters and the price level after about six; output stays down after the funds rate returns to trend; the prime–bill and commercial paper–bill spreads rise fairly quickly, leading the output downturn. The prime-rate spread response is twice the commercial-paper spread response, consistent with lower-quality borrowers facing larger spread movements (footnote 27).
- **Monetary shock in the model (Figure 3)**: for an unanticipated 25 basis point (annual) cut in the nominal rate, the accelerator barely changes the path of the nominal rate but makes the initial output response "about 50% greater" and the investment effect "nearly twice as great"; output and investment after four quarters are about where the baseline is after two. The premium falls sharply and reverts slowly as net worth decays; its response "matches the VAR evidence reasonably well". Persistence comes without an unusually high labor supply elasticity: the countercyclical premium flattens the marginal cost curve. The main discrepancy is that output responds immediately in the model but with a delay in the data. With the accelerator, "smaller countercyclical movements in interest rates are required to dampen output fluctuations."
- **Other shocks (Figure 4)**: the accelerator magnifies and propagates technology and government spending shocks by about as much as monetary shocks. A transfer of wealth to entrepreneurs (about 1% of their wealth, about 0.05% of households') has essentially no effect in the baseline but, through the feedback from investment to the price of capital, raises entrepreneurial wealth by more than 2% and output by 1% at an annual rate, persistently — reminiscent of Fisher's (1933) debt-deflation, and of currency crises in which foreign-currency loans transfer wealth away from domestic borrowers when the exchange rate collapses.
- **Investment delays (Figure 5)**: with a one-quarter planning lag the 25 basis point cut produces a hump-shaped output response, more accentuated with the accelerator; persistence is closer to the data, the initial response is still too strong, and the premium still responds immediately because asset prices anticipate the investment boom.
- **Heterogeneous firms (Figure 6)**: sector premiums of 3% and 1% per year (average 2%, weight .5125; roughly half of output from credit-constrained firms), substitution parameter 0.9, adjustment-cost elasticity lowered to 0.1, investment delays of one period (constrained sector) and two (unconstrained). Aggregate effects are roughly the same as in the one-sector model, with smoother humps; investment of firms with poor credit access rises "nearly three times as much" as that of firms with good access, consistent with the micro evidence (Gertler & Gilchrist 1994 and others). The authors attribute the contrast with Fisher (1996) to endogenous net worth.
- **Literature (section 6)**: the model combines Bernanke & Gertler (1989) — costly state verification, overlapping-generations entrepreneurs, cash-flow propagation and nonlinearity — with Kiyotaki & Moore's (1997) asset-price channel. Unlike Carlstrom & Fuerst (1997), whose agency problem sits with capital producers and works through the supply price of capital, here borrowers own the capital stock, so asset prices hit net worth directly and amplification is larger.

- **Equations are heavily damaged by OCR** throughout sections 3–4 and the appendices: Greek letters, subscripts and superscripts are replaced by stray characters (e.g. eq. 3.8 reads "QaK/~, = *p(st)N/+j"; eq. 4.13 survives only as its last term; eq. 4.24 is reduced to "yRK t,"; the coefficient definitions after eq. 4.27 and the derivations of Appendix A.1–A.3 and B are largely unreadable). The model is described above from the surrounding prose; no equation is reconstructed here.
- **Figures 1–6 survive only as axis fragments and garbled legends** ("With -- o u t W ith Fina F n in c a ia n l c A ia c lA ce c l c e e r l a e t r o a r t or"); impulse-response magnitudes are known only where the text states them.
- The symbols of some parameters are lost in the calibration paragraph (e.g. the steady-state riskless rate prints as "R = 3-1", the capital-to-net-worth ratio as "~K"), though the values are legible. The expected entrepreneurial lifetime and expected price duration expressions print as "1@)" and "1@0-".
- **Inconsistency in the text**: section 5.2.2 says an exchange rate collapse redistributes wealth "from domestic borrowers to foreign lenders"; section 7 says "to domestic lenders" while giving the same foreign-currency reason. Reported as written.
- Section 5.2.3 says the one-quarter investment plan is "as in Section 5.2"; the delay is introduced in section 4.2.1.1.
- Authors' caveats and future work: no banking sector (the bank lending channel is left out for lack of dynamic work; intermediaries' own net worth could be added); debt is real, not nominal, so debt-deflation redistributions are not evaluated; closed economy; frictions limited to investment (households follow a standard Euler equation); the initial output response to monetary shocks is large partly because of high intertemporal substitution and possibly too-short investment delays; the more policy stabilizes output, the smaller the accelerator's role.
