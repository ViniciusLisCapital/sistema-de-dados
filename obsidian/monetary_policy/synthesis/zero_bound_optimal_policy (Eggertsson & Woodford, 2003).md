## Context and motivation

Japan's call rate had been within 50 basis points of zero since October 1995 and essentially zero for most of the previous four years, while growth stayed anemic, prices kept falling and the monetary base, relative to GDP, became "more than twice as large" as in the early 1990s. In the United States the federal funds rate was down to 1 percent. Two views were in circulation: deflation as a "black hole" (Krugman), which argues for a high inflation target in normal times; and the view that base expansion, purchases of long bonds or other assets can work without lower rates. The Bank of Japan used the zero bound as an argument against inflation targeting ("setting an inflation target of, say, 2 percent wouldn't carry much credibility"). The paper develops Krugman (1998) in a fully dynamic model with staggered (Calvo) prices, to ask how much the zero bound constrains policy and what optimal policy looks like when it binds.

## Core argument / thesis

- The zero bound is a genuine constraint — expanding the monetary base or buying other assets "does little if anything to expand the set of feasible paths for inflation and real activity" under a credible policy — but it restricts outcomes "to a much more modest degree than the deflation pessimists presume".
- "The key to dealing with this sort of situation in the least damaging way is to create the right kind of expectations regarding how monetary policy will be used after the constraint is no longer binding". Spending depends on the whole expected path of short real rates, so a credible promise of later inflation and of nominal rates kept low for longer stimulates demand now.
- Optimal policy is **history-dependent**: a commitment to keep the rate at zero after the natural rate turns positive again and to let the price level end higher than it would have been. It can be implemented by a **gap-adjusted price-level target** that is raised whenever the zero bound prevents hitting it, with no need to estimate the natural rate. A purely forward-looking policy — including any strict inflation target and discretion — does much worse.
- "In fact, the management of expectations is the key to successful monetary policy at all times, not just in those relatively unusual circumstances when the zero bound is reached."

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Irrelevance proposition for open-market operations (first section)**: a model with money in the utility function (non-separable, with a satiation level of real balances at which the marginal utility of money is zero), Calvo pricing, complete markets, an interest rate rule in the spirit of the Taylor rule, a base-supply rule (the multiplier ψ governing quantitative easing), a rule for the composition of the central bank's portfolio and a fiscal rule for total government liabilities plus debt management. **Proposition**: the set of equilibrium paths for prices, output, interest rates, asset prices and total liabilities is independent of the base-supply rule, of the central bank's portfolio and of the composition of public debt. Reason: beyond satiation, extra money provides no liquidity services, and in general equilibrium the marginal utility of income in a state depends on consumption in that state, not on the composition of the portfolio — so there are no portfolio-balance effects even though assets carry different risk and term premiums. The result holds only if the operations do not change expected future interest rate policy or the path of total government liabilities; a permanent "helicopter drop" changes both and is outside it. Any effect of asset purchases must come through expectations.
- **Log-linear model (second section)**: a forward-looking IS relation (24) and New Keynesian AS relation (25), driven by the natural rate of interest and a cost-push term; the constraint is that the nominal rate cannot fall below the rate paid on base money (zero in the calibration). Calibration (footnote 36): quarters, σ = 0.5 (a deliberately low interest sensitivity, so as not to exaggerate the contraction), κ = 0.02 (Rotemberg and Woodford 1997), β = 0.99, giving a long-run real rate of 4 percent a year.
- **Experiment**: the natural rate falls unexpectedly to –2 percent a year and returns to +4 percent with probability 0.1 each quarter, so it is expected to stay negative ten quarters on average.
- **Optimal commitment (third section)**: minimize the discounted sum of squared inflation and weighted squared output gap (28), derived from household utility; Lagrangian method extended to the zero bound (Jung, Teranishi & Watanabe 2001); lagged multipliers in the first-order conditions make policy history-dependent. Solved numerically (Appendix).
- **Optimal targeting rule (equations 32–34)**: each period there is a predetermined target for the gap-adjusted price level (price level plus λ/κ times the output gap); the bank sets the rate to hit it if possible, otherwise sets it at zero; next period's target rises with the current shortfall. Outside the trap the shortfall is zero and the rule is a fixed gap-adjusted price-level target. **Simpler rule (35)**: a constant target for the gap-adjusted price level.
- **Self-fulfilling deflation (fourth section)**: with a Ricardian fiscal rule, permanent deflation with the rate at zero is also an equilibrium. It is excluded by a commitment that keeps total nominal government liabilities from contracting: e.g. a balanced-budget rule (Schmitt-Grohé and Uribe), or a central bank commitment to supply base money in proportion to the **target** price level rather than the actual one while the bound binds (equation 37), plus a fiscal commitment to a nonnegative asymptotic present value of debt. This gives quantitative easing a role, consistent with the proposition because the fiscal assumption differs.

## Main results / findings

- **Strict inflation targeting (Figure 2)**: with a zero inflation target the shock produces a 14 percent output gap and 10 percent annual deflation; with a 1 percent target, 4 percent annual deflation and an output gap of 7 percent; a 2 percent target lets the real rate reach –2 percent and closes the gap, which is the Phelps–Summers–Fischer argument for a positive target (Krugman suggested 4 percent for Japan in 1998). But a higher target costs distortions at all times, and any purely forward-looking policy, with a two-state natural rate, amounts to a strict inflation target between zero and 2 percent.
- **Optimal commitment (Figures 3–5)**: commit to an output boom and inflation once the natural rate turns positive, with the price level eventually stabilized (unlike a positive inflation target). Some contraction and deflation still occur, because the promised inflation is itself costly — "it is not optimal to commit to so much future inflation that the zero bound ceases to bind". In the fifteen-quarter scenario (natural rate negative in t = 0 through 14), the optimal policy keeps the rate at zero for five more quarters (t = 15 through 19), while forward-looking policy raises it at once in quarter 15; the contraction and deflation of the strict target are "largely avoided".
- **Expected discounted losses, relative to a strict zero-inflation target** (table in "A Simpler Proposal"): strict target π* = 0: 100; π* = 1: 24.1; π* = 2: 32; constant price-level target: 0.0725; optimal rule: 0.036. Losses under the simple rule are twice those of the optimal rule but small. A price-level target is an "automatic stabilizer": a larger shock causes more deflation and therefore automatically more expected subsequent inflation.
- **Communication matters**: the differenced version of rule (35) (36) gives the same equilibrium outside the trap but mandates deflation during the recovery, doing worse than a strict zero-inflation target. "It is crucial to communicate to the public that the government is committed to a long-run price-level target."
- **"Keep some powder dry?"** Under the optimal rule anticipated trouble does not raise the target; but when the fall in the natural rate is foreseen four quarters ahead (Figure 9), optimal policy loosens preemptively and hits zero before the natural rate turns negative (by quarter 3), and the later deflation is milder.
- **Discretion has a deflationary bias**: Markov discretion equals the strict zero-inflation outcome of Figure 2 — the problem identified by Kydland and Prescott. Actions that change the central bank's future incentives can help: tax cuts financed by debt or money (if the bank cares about debt costs, Eggertsson 2003a), purchases of real assets, and above all purchases of foreign assets, which give an incentive to inflate later to avoid capital losses. Japan's gross debt is 140 percent of GDP but net debt only 67 percent, and the BoJ is not seen as caring about the debt burden. Purchases of long bonds or sales of funds-rate options (Tinsley) only bind the bank to low rates, not to a higher price level; with ten-year yields in Japan below 1 percent while prices fall, the market may expect low rates because deflation continues.
- Rapid base expansion of the kind tried in Japan "over the past two years" has "no obvious benefit" if it is expected to be withdrawn; the base expansion implied by rule (37) is "quite modest". Asset purchases and FX intervention matter as signals (e.g. long-rate levels or depreciation as an indicator of whether the commitment is believed).

**Comments.**
- *Friedman*: Japan had 1 percent real growth or less in seven of the last eleven years and falling prices in eight of the last nine. A true price-level target also means undoing inflations with deflations; the model omits the mechanisms that make deflation harmful (debt defaults), a foreign-currency margin, direct fiscal effects and portfolio effects — conclusions should read "in this model". Akerlof, Dickens and Perry's 2 to 3 percent inflation could make a positive target "win-win". The real problem is the transition to credibility ("Tinker Bell's dust"), and he is uncomfortable with policy reduced to "the management of expectations".
- *Gertler*: by iterating the IS curve, a commitment to lower post-trap rates raises the output gap directly, not only through expected inflation (equation 6) — "The analysis of the former, however, is new." The simple price-level rule depends on a purely forward-looking Phillips curve; with a hybrid curve (Galí and Gertler 1999) past inflation should matter, and time consistency remains. A transitory fiscal expansion can push the natural rate itself positive; credit-market frictions (a premium χ) raise the chance that the bound binds, so financial restructuring belongs in the mix.
- *General discussion*: Duesenberry doubts that expectations and credibility are the crucial elements; Sims objects to the fiscal assumption (real total liabilities pegged regardless of money vs. debt) and, with Friedman, prefers an asymmetric commitment; Brainard argues that setting today's long rate differs from announcing a rule for future short rates when investment is irreversible.

## Limitations and caveats

- **All display equations are garbled.** Equations 1–37 of the paper (utility, budget constraint, Euler equation, money demand and complementary slackness, the price-setting and profit functions, the policy and fiscal rules, the IS and AS relations 24–25, the loss function 28, the Lagrangian and first-order conditions 29–31, the targeting rules 32–36 and the base rule 37), the deflationary-equilibrium conditions, and equations 1–9 of Gertler's comment survive only as scattered symbols and digits. The Appendix's solution method (A1–A8 and the recursion matrices) is likewise mostly illegible. The note describes the rules in words from the text; their exact form cannot be read from the file.
- Figures 1–9 survive only as captions, axis ticks and legends; the values quoted above come from the text. Figure 1's data (call rate, base-to-GDP ratio) are not in the file.
- Model scope, stated by the authors and discussants: a single closed economy with a representative household, an exogenous natural rate, no capital, and a purely forward-looking Phillips curve; the log-linear approximation is "hoped" (not verified) to be accurate for disturbances of several percentage points and an opportunity cost of money of several points. Taxing money (Gesell) and state-contingent fiscal policy are set aside. The irrelevance proposition rests on complete markets/representative agent and on asset purchases not changing future policy; portfolio-balance evidence is cited from the literature (Operation Twist, Agell and Persson), not estimated here.
- The file contains no reference list; it ends with Sims's second remark in the general discussion.
