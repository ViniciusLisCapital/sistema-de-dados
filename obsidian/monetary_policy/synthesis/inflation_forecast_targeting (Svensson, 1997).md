## Context and motivation

New Zealand, Canada, the U.K., Sweden, Finland, Australia and Spain have adopted explicit inflation targets: a quantitative target (typically 2 percent per year), usually a tolerance interval (typically ±1 percentage point), and no explicit intermediate target (Spain, in the ERM, also has an exchange rate target). Inflation targeting's general advantages — focus on the goal, ex post measurement of performance and of credibility, more accountability, a potential commitment mechanism against the inflation bias — run into two problems. **Implementation**: central banks control inflation imperfectly; current inflation is predetermined, lags are "long and variable", and shocks hit within the "control lag". **Monitoring**: with a control lag of 1.5-2 years, policy cannot be judged until inflation is observed, and that inflation reflects shocks policy could not respond to — so a bank can blame deviations on outside factors. Sceptics conclude that money growth or exchange rate targeting is safer.

## Core argument / thesis

Inflation targeting implies **inflation forecast targeting**: the central bank's own inflation forecast at the control-lag horizon becomes an explicit intermediate target. Setting the instrument so that this forecast equals the target is the optimal policy, and making the forecast explicit simplifies both implementation and monitoring. The forecast is an "ideal" intermediate target — most correlated with the goal, more controllable, more observable and transparent. The weight on output stabilization only governs **how fast** the forecast is brought back to the target. Money growth or exchange rate targeting is generally inferior and yields more inflation variability, and a commitment to a **target rule** is preferable to a commitment to an **instrument rule** (McCallum, Taylor).

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Model (2.1)–(2.3)**, annual: the change in inflation rises with lagged output and a lagged exogenous variable; output is serially correlated, falls with the lagged (pseudo-)real repo rate and rises with the exogenous variable, which is AR(1). The repo rate moves output with a one-year lag and inflation with a two-year lag — the control lag; that the instrument affects inflation later than output is "the crucial property", consistent with VAR studies. No discretionary inflation bias is built in (footnote 6).
- **Single goal (Section 2)**: the bank minimizes the discounted expected squared deviation of inflation from the target (2.4)–(2.5). Because the year-t repo rate only affects inflation from t+2 on, the problem splits period by period (2.8) (Appendix A), and the first-order condition is (2.9): the two-year inflation forecast equals the target. Certainty equivalence lets the inflation loss be replaced by a loss on the forecast (2.10). The implied reaction function (2.13)–(2.14) has the form of a Taylor rule, but also responds to the exogenous variable and its coefficients "generally differ from 0.5"; the instrument responds to current inflation because current inflation predicts future inflation, not because it is targeted. Realized inflation deviates from the target only by shocks within the control lag (2.15)–(2.16), so accountability should ideally be for forecast deviations.
- **Monitoring (Section 3)**: if the bank has a private implicit target that deviates from the official one by an AR(1) term (3.1)–(3.3), whose positive mean can stand for a Barro–Gordon bias, it sets its forecast above the official target (3.5) and the repo rate lower (3.6). The public, knowing the model and observing the state and the instrument, can infer the deviation from the instrument or from its own forecast; modelling public criticism as an extra loss on the forecast's deviation, intense criticism pushes the forecast towards the official target (footnote 14 reads this as an inflation contract on the forecast). Best practice is for the bank to publish its model, information, assumptions and judgements (Inflation Reports); otherwise outside and "Consensus Forecasts" serve. Beyond the control lag, forecasts depend on the planned instrument path, so discrepancies between the bank's repo plan and the forward rate curve signal credibility problems.
- **Ideal intermediate target (Section 4)**: by definition the forecast has the highest correlation with future inflation, is more controllable than inflation (same instrument effect, smaller variance), is observable now, and makes policy transparent: forecast above target → tighten, below → ease. Predicting policy becomes predicting inflation.
- **Money growth targeting (Section 5)**: adding a money demand function (5.1), money growth is itself imperfectly controlled with a lag, so money targeting is money growth forecast targeting. A **conditional** money growth target (5.8) reproduces the optimal reaction function but is "less direct and less transparent"; an **unconditional** target equal to the inflation target (5.11) gives the reaction function (5.12)–(5.13), with average inflation on target but more variable inflation and persistent deviations. It is optimal only if money growth is a sufficient statistic for future inflation and money demand has no shocks (5.14), which cannot hold in this model; the same applies to exchange rate targeting. Footnote 20 reads the Bundesbank's money target as the unconditional one, applied flexibly.
- **Output stabilization (Section 6)**: with loss (6.1) and weight λ on output, the condition becomes (6.6) and, equivalently, (6.8): the two-year forecast's deviation from target is a fraction c of the predetermined one-year forecast's deviation, with 0 ≤ c < 1, c = 0 when λ = 0 and c rising towards one as λ grows (Appendix B). The forecast is returned to target **gradually**, the slower the larger the weight; the reaction function (6.11)–(6.12) has smaller coefficients. This is a variable short-run target around a constant long-run one.
- **Bands (Section 7)**: bands (typically 2 percentage points wide) read naturally as confidence intervals. With a single goal the band reflects only control and forecast errors and there is no room for a band on the forecast; with multiple goals an implicit forecast band widens with λ, so a wide band can signal a large output goal. Sanctions (New Zealand's governor can be relieved of his post) should ideally depend on forecasts, but until forecasts are verifiable Svensson prefers basing them on realized inflation.
- **Target vs. instrument rules (Section 8)**: the target rules (2.9) and (6.8) depend only on the Phillips curve and the loss function; the implied instrument rules also depend on aggregate demand. Target rules are "less complex and more robust", revise the reaction function automatically with new information, and are easier to identify, motivate and verify.

## Main results / findings

- The optimal policy under inflation targeting is to set the instrument so that the inflation forecast at the control-lag horizon equals the target; the instrument then depends on all information relevant for forecasting inflation.
- The intermediate target should be the bank's own structural forecast, not the consensus of outside forecasters, to avoid the instability, multiplicity or non-existence of equilibria shown by Woodford (1994).
- A positive weight on output stabilization means gradual adjustment of the forecast, not a different regime: implementation and monitoring become "a bit more complex, but not overly so". This optimal steady leaning is "very different" from the opportunistic approach to disinflation (footnote 25).
- Inflation targeting implies money growth or exchange rate targeting if and only if such targeting is optimal; otherwise those targets are inefficient.
- Transparency: central banks keep secrets "mostly for no good reasons"; full revelation of the forecast is the best incentive for quality and against self-serving bias. Svensson finds it unlikely that central banks have much private information about the economy relative to sophisticated observers; their private information mostly concerns their own implicit goals and instrument plans.
- Inflation targeting allows base drift of the price level; price-level targeting may become a realistic and desirable alternative once inflation targeting is mastered (Svensson 1996d, not in the file).

## Limitations and caveats

- **OCR font damage throughout**: the "fl" ligature prints as "‡" (In‡ation), "fi" and "ffi" as "…" and "¢" (de…nition, di¢cult), and Greek letters and operators as Latin-1 symbols (π as "¼", α as "®", β as "¯", the minus sign as "¡", ≥ as "¸", ≤ as "·"). Equations are split over several lines, notably (2.6)–(2.7), (2.13)–(2.14), (2.17), (3.8), (5.6)–(5.9), (6.7) and Appendix B (B.6)–(B.9). The text's own statements of the results are readable; the note describes the equations in words and does not restore them.
- Equation (2.17) and (3.8), equilibrium output, print with fractions whose numerators and denominators are scrambled; (6.7) and the last line of (B.6), which should state the same coefficient k, do not print identically. Section 4 names the forecast "¼t+1j2", which does not match the two-year forecast used everywhere else in the text. These are reported, not corrected.
- The model is deliberately simple: annual, backward-looking, with no explicit role for private expectations (footnote 7 notes it violates the natural-rate hypothesis in the strict sense); output stabilization is analyzed with the exogenous variable switched off (6.3). Results on bands, model uncertainty and price-level targeting are referred to other Svensson papers (1996b, c, d) not in the file.
- The paper carries no reference list in the file, so citations (Haldane 1996, King 1994, Walsh 1993, Woodford 1994, etc.) cannot be resolved from the clean_md.
- More sophisticated evaluation (comparing the bias and variance of the bank's and outsiders' forecast errors) "requires more than the current few years of data" from inflation-targeting regimes.
