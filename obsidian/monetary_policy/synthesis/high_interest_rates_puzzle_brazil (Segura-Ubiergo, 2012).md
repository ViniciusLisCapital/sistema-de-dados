## Context and motivation

Brazil's ex post short-term real rate (the policy rate adjusted for inflation) fell from about 40 percent in the 1980s to its lowest level in 2009, yet stayed well above other emerging-market inflation targeters (Figures 1–2). High real rates were widely cited as a binding constraint on growth. Looking only at Brazilian data would show rates as historically low; the puzzle is why, at any point in time, they were higher than in comparable countries and what would let Brazil converge. The paper leaves aside the separate question of high bank intermediation spreads.

## Core argument / thesis

- Brazil's real rates came down because fundamentals improved: inflation volatility collapsed, the fiscal and external positions strengthened, and inflation targeting added a credibility effect.
- The main remaining lever is **domestic saving**: an investment–saving logic with imperfect capital mobility (Feldstein–Horioka) makes the equilibrium real rate higher where domestic saving is low. Raising saving — especially **public** saving through a better fiscal balance — would move Brazil toward its peers.
- Even after all measured fundamentals, Brazil keeps a country-specific premium of about two percentage points, pointing to unmeasured factors such as credit-market segmentation (subsidized public lending) and indexation-driven inflation inertia.

## Key mechanisms / model

**Five families of explanations reviewed (Section II).**
- *Fiscal*: two kinds of fiscal dominance (Coates and Rivera): Type I, monetary financing of deficits — relevant during hyperinflation, not in the last fifteen years; Type II, crowding out when deficits are financed in local-currency markets, with the Treasury and open-market operations competing on the yield curve. Evidence on public debt is mixed: gross debt and real rates do not move together (Figure 3) and debt is not robust in panel regressions, sometimes with the wrong sign. Lower deficits (high primary surpluses, the 2000 fiscal responsibility law) are what plausibly helped. A history of defaults means a premium even at moderate debt (Rogoff).
- *Low domestic saving*: if investment demand exceeds domestic saving, the equilibrium real rate rises; across inflation targeters, saving and real rates are strongly negatively related, with Brazil and Turkey above the fitted line (Figure 5).
- *Institutions*: "jurisdictional uncertainty" (Arida, Bacha and Lara-Resende) is rejected as an explanation because many emerging markets with weaker institutions have lower rates and the cited evidence does not support it; lack of formal central bank independence is hard to test, and changes at the BCB board did not cost credibility.
- *History of inflation*: decades of high inflation and hyperinflation required very high real rates to disinflate; the fall in inflation volatility after 1995, and especially with inflation targeting, "tamed" expectations.
- *Transmission*: subsidized public credit (BNDES, housing, agriculture) means the central bank controls only part of the credit market, so the policy rate must rise more to restrain total demand and the free-market equilibrium rate is pushed up; the savings-account (poupança) remuneration acts as a floor for the policy rate; a relatively high inflation target raises nominal, not real, rates; indexation creates inflation inertia, short-maturity indexed debt weakens the wealth channel, and low credit/GDP weakens the credit channel.

**Econometric model (Section III).** A panel error-correction model for the ex post real rate in 15 emerging-market inflation targeters, annual data 1980–2009 (unbalanced), estimated by robust OLS with fixed effects: changes in the real rate depend on its lagged level (adjustment toward a long-run relation) and on levels and changes of per-capita GDP at PPP, the current account balance, private saving, the general government balance (proxy for public saving), the federal funds rate, inflation volatility and an inflation-targeting dummy. Long-run effects are the level coefficients divided by the adjustment coefficient. Inflation and the real exchange rate are excluded because the real rate Granger-causes them.

What carries the result: the identification of saving effects from cross-country and time variation in a panel of peers; the assumption that saving, the fiscal balance and inflation volatility are not driven by the real rate (supported by Granger tests); and the reading of the fixed effect as a Brazil-specific premium.

## Main results / findings

- **Signs match the theory** (Table 1, 1980–2009): higher private saving, a better fiscal balance and a stronger current account lower the real rate; higher inflation volatility and a higher US policy rate raise it; per-capita income is not significant.
- **Public saving beats private saving**: the effect of saving raised through the fiscal balance is "about three times higher" than an equivalent rise in private saving, and the fiscal coefficient is larger in every robustness specification (panel-corrected and White standard errors, Arellano–Bond GMM, GLS).
- **Inflation volatility** was the big historical driver: its fall lowered Brazil's real rate by over two percentage points, but it has converged to peers and offers little further gain.
- **Inflation targeting** lowers real rates in the short run (the change variable is starred in Table 1, with a p-value of 0.080), but the level effect is not significant — no permanent effect once fundamentals are controlled for.
- **Global conditions** matter: a higher fed funds rate raises emerging-market real rates in the short and long run.
- **The saving gap is the lever**: raising Brazil's domestic saving from its sample average (16.5 percent of GDP) to Mexico's (22.6 percent) would lower its real rate by over 2 percentage points, closing almost half the gap with peers; closing it fully would need Korea- or Thailand-level saving (about 30 percent of GDP), never seen in Brazil.
- **What does not fit**: Brazil's fixed effect implies real rates "about two percentage points higher" than peers for reasons the model does not identify (Table 1 notes; Figure 10). Public debt, a common explanation, does not hold up.

**What would contradict it.** A rise in domestic (especially public) saving without a fall in Brazil's real rate relative to peers; or evidence that the residual premium disappears once subsidized credit or indexation is measured — which would confirm rather than contradict the paper's conjecture.

## Limitations and caveats

- Author's own: subsidized public lending (BNDES transfers expanded only from 2008) and indexation could not be tested for lack of comparative data; the poupança floor and the credit-segmentation effect are argued, not measured; data end in 2009 because of post-crisis asymmetric policy adjustments and data gaps; the panel is unbalanced; about 4 percent of observations are outliers handled by robust regression; the split between private and public saving effects varies with the estimator.
- From the text: the dependent variable is the ex post real rate, which mixes the policy stance with inflation surprises; the model explains the average level across peers, so its implications for Brazil rely on Brazil sharing the panel's coefficients (the reason advanced inflation targeters are excluded). The rejection of jurisdictional uncertainty rests on cross-country comparison and one cited study.
- **Damaged passages.** Equations [1] and [2] (the error-correction model and its OLS form) lost their symbols; the model is described in words from the surrounding text.
