## Context and motivation

The liquidity trap had faded from macroeconomics after the 1930s: postwar nominal rates stayed well above zero, and Friedman and Schwartz's reading of the Depression implied that a trap "cannot happen, did not happen, and will not happen again". Japan in the 1990s contradicted that: money market rates near zero, a central bank claiming it could do no more, and an economy stagnant since 1991 sliding into recession. Policymakers were reaching for the textbook IS-LM answer (pump-priming fiscal expansion). Krugman asks what the theory looks like once three things modern macro added are put in — intertemporal choice with rational expectations, an open economy, and financial intermediaries — and then applies it to Japan's output gap, saving-investment balance, banks and policy options.

## Core argument / thesis

- A liquidity trap is a **credibility problem in reverse**. Money neutrality says that an increase in the money supply in the current *and all future* periods raises prices proportionally, whatever the state of banks or firms. If base expansion does nothing, it must be because the public expects it not to be sustained: the market believes the central bank will stabilize prices "given the chance", so any current expansion is read as transitory.
- A trap arises when the real interest rate needed to clear the goods market at full employment is **negative** while the nominal rate cannot fall below zero and prices are expected to be stable. The economy "needs" expected inflation.
- So monetary policy is not powerless: it works "if the central bank can credibly promise to be irresponsible, to seek a higher future price level". Raising the expected future price level does what a rate cut would do.
- Two side conclusions overturn common beliefs: capital mobility does not guarantee escape from a trap, and in a trap the failure of broad money and bank credit to grow is not evidence that monetary policy is too tight or that banks are the core problem.

## Key mechanisms / model

**The minimalist model (equations 1–4).** One good, a representative agent with constant relative risk aversion, a cash-in-advance constraint, and from period two on constant output and a constant money supply (so a fixed future price level). Two relations determine the first period: the money side (with a positive nominal rate, the price level is proportional to money) and the Euler equation (given the future price level, a higher current price level means expected deflation and a lower nominal rate, because the economy delivers its equilibrium real rate regardless). Normally a monetary expansion raises the price level and lowers the nominal (not the real) rate.

**The trap with flexible prices.** Pushing money beyond the point where the nominal rate hits zero just swaps money for zero-interest bonds: no effect on prices or the rate. With the long-run money supply held fixed, a larger current money supply amounts to lower expected inflation, and the zero bound caps how much deflation the economy can be made to expect. If the required real rate is negative — when future output is expected to be sufficiently below current output (condition 5) — price stability is not attainable: the price level falls now so that it can rise later. No unemployment results, only an impotent central bank.

**The Hicksian trap with sticky prices (equations 6–7).** With the first-period price level predetermined, output adjusts to consumption demand; the IS curve comes from the Euler equation. Money raises output only until the rate reaches zero; if capacity lies beyond that point, no open-market operation reaches full employment. The trap occurs when people expect deflation, or when expected future income is low relative to today's capacity, so a negative real rate is needed to make them spend.

What carries the result: (i) the expectation that the future price level (or money supply) is unchanged by today's expansion — relax it, and a rise in the expected future price level shifts the IS curve out (equation 6); (ii) a negative full-employment real rate; (iii) for unemployment, nominal rigidity.

**Why the required real rate can be negative with positive returns to capital.** An equity premium can open the gap; more fundamentally, the return on capital includes the expected change in its price (Tobin's q). An overlapping-generations example with land (equations 8–9): if the next generation of workers is smaller, the price of land is expected to fall, and the return on buying it can be negative even though its marginal product is positive — the demographic channel.

**Open economy (equations 10–11).** With tradables and nontradables and perfect capital mobility in tradables, the real rate in tradables is pinned by the world, but the real rate in nontradables must adjust to the path of nontradable output. With a small traded share, the domestic consumption-based real rate can be negative even when the world rate is positive, so exporting saving does not ensure escape. A temporary monetary expansion lowers the nominal rate, depreciates the currency and raises nontradable output, but both effects are finite at a zero rate. Under log utility, monetary expansion has no effect on the current account.

**Banks and monetary aggregates ("cash in advance meets Diamond-Dybvig", Appendix A).** With a positive rate, deposits are a multiple of reserves and base growth raises deposits and prices proportionally. At a zero rate consumers and banks are indifferent among cash, bonds and deposits; extra base may be held as currency instead of bonds, as currency instead of deposits, or as excess bank reserves. Only the first raises a broad aggregate; the others shrink deposits and bank credit. So in a trap base expansion is expected to barely move broad money and possibly to *reduce* bank credit, even with healthy banks.

**Fiscal policy.** The representative-agent framework is Ricardian by construction (Krugman calls this an accidental by-product of the modeling, not an empirical judgment). Under Ricardian equivalence in a trap, tax cuts, spending vouchers and even a helicopter drop of money (equivalent to a lump-sum transfer of bonds when money and bonds are perfect substitutes) do nothing, and public works have a multiplier of exactly one (footnote 11). A temporary stimulus can have permanent effects only if multiple equilibria exist; otherwise fiscal support must be sustained, which raises the question of debt.

**Managed inflation.** The prescription is an "inverted" inflation target — legislation requiring the Bank of Japan to achieve inflation of not less than some rate over some years — sustained long enough to lower the long-term real rate. Interest rates "need never fall": what is required is a promise not to raise them when the economy expands and prices rise. Because expected inflation does in a trap what rate cuts do normally, it is no more a beggar-thy-neighbor policy than any monetary expansion under floating rates. In the intertemporal open model the current-account effect of a brief expansion depends on relative risk aversion and the traded share (equation 12, "beggar thy neighbor coefficient"): zero when relative risk aversion is one, small and decreasing in openness otherwise.

## Main results / findings

The paper's evidence is a reading of Japanese and US data and of others' model estimates, not estimation of its own.

- **Japan is in a trap**: money market rates below 1 percent since 1995 (Table 4) with deflationary pressure and a slump. The output gap is larger than official estimates: an Okun's-law relation for 1982–91 (Figure 4) and the OECD's own earlier potential-growth estimates point to a gap well above the OECD's; Krugman argues it will "exceed 5 percent" by the end of 1998. Hodrick-Prescott-type methods understate gaps in a sustained slump because they fold the slump into potential — applied to the interwar United States, the filter says output was above potential by 1935 (Figure 5).
- **Excess saving reflects weak investment more than rising saving**: Japan's consumption share did not fall in the 1990s (Table 5). The puzzle is why the trap came so late; high investment was sustained by high potential growth and the late-1980s bubble, and the prospective decline of the working-age population lowers expected q.
- **Monetary aggregates behave as the trap predicts**: from 1994 to 1997 the monetary base rose much faster than M2+CD while bank credit stagnated (Table 7) — "to be expected even if the banks are financially healthy". The 1930s US divergence of base and M2 (Figure 3) is reread the same way.
- **Banks are less causal than assumed**: insolvent banks with guaranteed deposits have an incentive to *overlend* (the jusen and credit cooperative cases). A credit crunch appeared only in late 1997, triggered by the announcement of new capital adequacy standards — the crunch came when the government began to deal with the banks, as in the US in 1990–92. Cleanup is right on microeconomic grounds and should be fast, but will not lift the economy out of the trap; normally its demand effect would be offset by easier money, which the trap prevents.
- **The Depression recovery fits the expectations story**: Romer's evidence of sharply negative ex ante real rates during 1933–41 is reread as a rise in inflation expectations with nominal rates at the floor, not as a fiscal jolt.
- **Orders of magnitude**, offered explicitly as a stimulus to research rather than an estimate: using the median of US model estimates of the long-rate cut needed to raise GNP (Table 8), Krugman suggests "4 percent inflation for fifteen years", with an implied yen depreciation "on the order of 20 to 25 percent" and a current-account increase of "something like 1 percent of GDP" — far short of the large surpluses others envisaged.

**What would contradict it.** A credible, sustained commitment to a higher future price level that failed to raise demand; or evidence that Japan's full-employment real rate is positive (Rogoff's main doubt), in which case conventional instruments and fiscal policy suffice.

**Comments.**
- *Dominguez*: a central bank that struggles to commit to responsibility will struggle more to commit to irresponsibility (time inconsistency); it is unclear it can commit to *moderate* inflation; negative real rates may push savings abroad and raise long rates. In the open-economy model consumers still face the world rate through tradables, so a consumption-weighted rate is not the relevant rate for saving decisions; storable nontradables (housing) and investment would undo the negative rate. Fiscal policy may be more effective than the Ricardian argument allows.
- *Rogoff*: doubts that Japan's medium-term full-employment real rate is negative with investment above 20 percent of GDP; labor-augmenting technical progress could offset demographics. The BoJ can always inflate if it wants to, but without a feedback variable it flies "partly blind". Fiscal expansion could raise the real rate and free monetary policy; he prefers a shorter, sharper inflation to fifteen years. Results can depend on how money is introduced (shopping-time and money-in-utility models carry Pigou-type effects).
- *General discussion*: B. Friedman sees Keynes's original trap as already intertemporal and questions monetary neutrality; Baily notes the risk premium on private borrowing keeps investment rates high even with a zero risk-free rate; Gordon and Blinder doubt that announcements can create inflation expectations without slack being absorbed; Gordon and others favour money-financed fiscal expansion, to which Ricardian equivalence does not apply; Stiglitz and Blinder think the paper underplays the credit crunch.

## Limitations and caveats

- Stated by the author: the models are highly stylized (endowment economies, one period of trap, no clear mapping of a model "period" to calendar time); the Ricardian bias against fiscal policy is a modeling by-product; the inflation target and depreciation figures are "leaps of faith" built from US model estimates and should "not really be taken seriously".
- From the text and discussants: the mechanism depends on how expectations are formed. If expectations follow experience (Gordon, Blinder), an announced target does not move them, and the policy collapses into whatever depreciation achieves. The open-economy negative real rate rests on exogenous traded/nontraded endowments and non-storable nontradables (Dominguez, Rogoff). The land example takes q as exogenous. The trap result is sensitive to the way money enters the model (Rogoff).
- **Damaged passages.** Most display equations are garbled: (1), (3)–(4), (6), (9), (10)–(13) and Appendix equations (15)–(21) survive as scattered symbols, so the exact forms of the IS curve, the beggar-thy-neighbor coefficient (12) and the depreciation coefficient (13) cannot be read from the file; the note describes them in words only. Figures 1–7 survive as axis labels and tick values. Tables 1–8 are extracted column by column but remain readable; their values are not transcribed here. The file ends with the general discussion; there is no reference list.
