## Context and motivation

Lower inflation in the industrial economies brought average nominal rates down and raised the odds that the policy rate hits zero. Japan had lived at the zero bound for years; the Fed cut to 1 percent in 2003 amid a "deflation scare". Theory on what a central bank can do at zero was abundant, empirical evidence scant. The question matters beyond emergencies: the more confident central banks are that tools exist to escape the bound, the smaller the inflation "buffer" they need, and the lower the inflation objective can be.

## Core argument / thesis

- A central bank at zero still has three, complementary, levers (taxonomy of Bernanke and Reinhart 2004): (1) **shape expectations** of the future policy rate; (2) **expand the balance sheet** beyond what is needed to hold the rate at zero (quantitative easing); (3) **change the composition** of the balance sheet to alter the relative supplies of securities (targeted purchases).
- The authors argue against Eggertsson and Woodford's strong result that shaping rate expectations is essentially the *only* tool: levers (2) and (3) can work if assets are imperfect substitutes, if monetary and fiscal policy are not fully separated, or as signals.
- The evidence gives "some grounds for optimism": communication moves expectations and long yields (clearly in the US, not in Japan's event studies), and large changes in relative supplies move yields. But the size and reliability of these effects are too uncertain to rely on, so the best policy remains avoidance — an inflation buffer and preemptive easing.

## Key mechanisms / model

**Why the policy rate alone does not describe the stance.** Spending responds mainly to long yields and asset prices, which depend on the expected path of short rates plus term, risk and liquidity premia. So a given policy rate can coexist with tight or easy financial conditions, and at zero the central bank can still lower long yields by convincing markets the rate will stay low longer.

**Lever 1, expectations.** Full commitment to a state-contingent rule is impractical, especially for unusual circumstances, so "talk" supplements it; if the bank pays a cost for reneging, statements also strengthen commitment. Commitments can be unconditional (calendar) or conditional on the economy — the BOJ's zero-interest-rate policy (ZIRP) "until deflationary concerns are dispelled", the FOMC's "considerable period" (2003) tied to slack and low inflation. Svensson's exchange-rate path is noted as a more verifiable form of commitment, possibly best for small open economies.

**Lever 2, quantitative easing — three channels, each with the assumption it needs.**
- *Portfolio balance (imperfect substitutes)*: extra money is traded for non-money assets, raising their prices. Requires money and other assets to be imperfect substitutes; at zero, when money demand is satiated, money becomes close to a zero-yield Treasury bill, so the choice of assets bought matters.
- *Fiscal channel*: money injections relax the government budget constraint, substituting the inflation tax for distorting taxes (Auerbach and Obstfeld). Requires the public to expect the injection *not* to be reversed — the Krugman credibility point.
- *Signalling*: a large balance sheet the bank would be slow to unwind underlines the commitment to zero rates, or marks a regime change (Roosevelt's 1933 devaluation and reflation, read through Temin and Wigmore).
- The reduced-form argument (money and inflation move together historically) is rejected: velocity need not be stable when the opportunity cost of money is zero (Lucas critique).

**Lever 3, composition.** In frictionless, complete markets relative supplies are irrelevant (EW); with transaction costs and incomplete markets, purchases can move term, risk and liquidity premia. A yield ceiling is an unlimited commitment to buy, priced as a put option given to bondholders; if investors doubt rates will stay low, the bank ends up owning the stock. Two caveats: the targeted yield can disconnect from private rates, and history is mixed (the US peg from 1942 to the 1951 Accord held largely without intervention at the long end; Operation Twist's effect was judged small, but the operation itself was small).

**Empirical tools.**
- *Event studies* (US: one-hour windows around FOMC decisions since July 1991; Japan: two-day windows around BOJ meetings since independence in April 1998). A Cholesky decomposition of three market indicators — the current policy surprise (Kuttner), year-ahead futures, the five-year yield — gives a policy-surprise factor, a "second factor" (year-ahead expectations orthogonal to the surprise) and a residual. Squared and signed factors are regressed on dummies for statements, surprise statements and path surprises, coded from before- and after-the-fact sources.
- *No-arbitrage macro-finance term structure model*: an affine model whose factors are observable (US: employment gap, core inflation, expected inflation, funds rate, year-ahead Eurodollar futures; Japan: unemployment, CPI inflation, call rate, year-ahead Euroyen futures), with prices of risk linear in the factors. It gives a benchmark yield curve given the economy and the policy stance; deviations of actual yields from it are attributed to factors outside the model (statements, supply, QE). For Japan the zero bound is imposed in simulation, since near zero the bound itself steepens the curve (lost scope for capital gains).

## Main results / findings

**United States, communication.** Two factors are needed: the year-ahead "second factor" explains most of the movement in year-ahead expectations and the largest share of the five-year yield around FOMC decisions — 68 percent of its variance in the hour-long window, sample from April 1998 (Table 1b). That factor is driven by **surprise statements**, especially those about the policy path, and moves in the right direction (hawkish surprises raise expectations and yields; Tables 2–3). Statements also *condition* how markets react to data: after "considerable period" (August 2003) ten-year yields became more sensitive to payroll surprises (Figure 2), while short-horizon implied rate volatility fell to historic lows (Figure 3). The term structure model confirms that year-ahead expectations matter for yields (Table 4), but bounds the size: the "considerable period" episode moved yields by at most about 20 basis points at two years and 7 at ten (Figure 5), and possibly less than half that.

**United States, relative supplies.** Three natural experiments — the Treasury buyback announcements (1999–2001), Japan's Ministry of Finance purchases of Treasuries from its FX interventions, and the 2003 belief that the Fed might buy ten-year notes — all show yields moving in the direction supply effects predict, and yields sitting below the model benchmark during each episode (Figures 7 and 9). The authors stress identification limits: model errors of that size are not unusual, the announcements also carried news about deflation risk or fiscal outlook, effects may not scale, and private rates did not follow (thirty-year swap spreads widened during the buyback, Figure 8).

**Japan.** The event studies find **no** reliable link between BOJ statements and year-ahead expectations — "the strongest and most important contrast" with the Fed — though the sample is small (Tables 9–11). There is some support for asset-price effects of the other levers: announcements of larger JGB purchase targets lowered JGB yields (statistically significant but small), and surprise increases in the current-account (reserves) target were associated with stock-market gains (Table 11). The term structure model, with the zero bound imposed, cannot explain how low JGB yields were, and the gap between actual and predicted curves widened after the ZIRP and after quantitative easing were introduced and narrowed when ZIRP was abandoned (Figure 11) — more persuasive than the event studies that the nonstandard policies lowered long rates. Whether lower rates strengthened the economy is outside the paper.

**What would contradict it.** Long yields unresponsive to path statements once the rate is at zero; or relative-supply effects that vanish after controlling for the news content of the announcements.

## Limitations and caveats

- Authors' own: the US evidence on balance-sheet policies is indirect (no Fed purchases in fifty years, and the funds rate stayed at least 100 basis points above zero); the event studies capture only immediate effects and only statements, not speeches; the identification of supply effects is confounded with news about deflation or fiscal prospects; magnitudes are "quantitatively quite uncertain" and may not scale; Japan's sample is small and daily data force wider windows; the coding of statement surprises is subjective; near the zero bound the VAR dynamics and risk prices may change, which the model does not capture; the Japanese VAR extrapolates persistent deflation.
- The QE channels rest on assumptions the authors flag: imperfect substitutability (which mean-variance reasoning suggests is small), monetary–fiscal coordination that Japan lacked, and credibility of non-reversal. Communication helps only if commitments are "consistent with the public's understanding of the policymakers' objectives and outlook".
- **Damaged passages.** Equations (1)–(9) (the VAR, the pricing kernel, the affine yield recursions and the option-pricing expression) are reduced to scattered symbols; the note describes the model in words only. Figures 1–11 survive as axis ticks and legends, with labels split one character per line; Table 8's events are readable. The appendix coding the statements is described as "to be provided" and is not in the file; there is no reference list.
