## Context and motivation

All earlier chapters assume a closed economy. This chapter builds an open-economy extension of the basic New Keynesian model of chapter 3 — a simplified version of Galí and Monacelli (2005) — that brings in the exchange rate, the terms of trade, exports, imports and international financial markets. It asks how openness changes the transmission of monetary policy, whether the closed-economy case for stabilizing inflation survives, what role the exchange rate should play, and how simple rules (including an exchange rate peg) compare. Open-economy modeling requires choices — large vs. small economy, asset market structure from autarky to complete markets, pricing to market vs. the law of one price, local vs. producer currency pricing — and a full treatment "would require a book of its own"; the chapter takes one example: a small open economy with complete international financial markets and the law of one price, with no cost-push shocks or wage rigidities.

## Core argument / thesis

The small open economy's equilibrium reduces to a New Keynesian Phillips curve in *domestic* inflation and a dynamic IS equation, "identical" in form to the closed economy's, with coefficients that depend on openness υ and the trade elasticity η, and a natural rate and natural output that depend on world variables. Openness adds a terms-of-trade motive to deviate from the flexible-price allocation, but in a special case (σ = η = 1, no preference shocks) a constant subsidy removes it and strict domestic inflation targeting is optimal — an open-economy "divine coincidence". Under that policy the nominal exchange rate and CPI inflation can fluctuate a lot, and that is fine: "Potentially large and persistent fluctuations in the nominal exchange rate, as well as in some inflation measures (like the CPI), are not necessarily undesirable, nor do they require a policy response aimed at dampening such fluctuations." The text stresses the result is an illustration, not "the basis for a robust policy prescription".

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Households (8.1.1)**: consumption is a CES composite of domestic and imported goods (2), with openness υ (the import share of consumption when η = 1) and η the substitutability between domestic and foreign goods; complete markets in internationally traded contingent claims give the Euler equation (10)–(11) in CPI inflation.
- **Identities (8.1.1.1)**: CPI inflation equals domestic inflation plus υ times the change in the terms of trade (13); with the law of one price the terms of trade are `st = et + p∗ t −pH,t` (14) and the real exchange rate is (1 − υ) times the terms of trade.
- **International risk sharing (8.1.1.2)**: complete markets tie domestic consumption to world output, the preference shock and the terms of trade (16)–(17).
- **UIP and the terms of trade (8.1.1.3)**: complete markets imply the familiar uncovered interest parity condition (18), and solving forward (20) makes the terms of trade "a function of current and anticipated real interest rate differentials". The text notes this is a convenient representation but "not ... an additional independent equilibrium condition".
- **Firms (8.1.2)**: Calvo pricing gives domestic inflation as a function of the markup gap (22), "not affected by the degree of openness". Export demand depends on the terms of trade and world output; trade is balanced in the symmetric steady state.
- **Demand side (8.2.1)**: goods market clearing (25) and the terms of trade (27) give an open-economy dynamic IS equation (28)–(29) with sensitivity 1/συ to the domestic real rate; if ση > 1, openness raises that sensitivity. The natural rate (30), (38) depends on expected world output growth as well as domestic shocks. Net exports (31) respond to the terms of trade with ambiguous sign; with σ = η = 1 they are invariant to it.
- **Supply side (8.2.2)**: the average markup depends on output, technology, the terms of trade and the preference shock (33); in gap form the NKPC (37) is `πH,t = βEt{πH,t+1} + κυyt`, with κυ equal to the closed-economy κ when υ = 0 or σ = η = 1, and lower with more openness when ση > 1. Natural output (35) depends on world output with ambiguous sign; with σ = η = 1 world output has no effect and preference shocks lower it.
- **Interest rate rule (8.3)**: a Taylor-type rule in domestic inflation and the output gap (39) gives a system (40) of the same form as in chapter 3, with the same determinacy condition (41).
- **Optimal policy, special case (8.4)**: with σ = η = 1 and no preference shocks, the planner's consumption/output possibilities set is `Ct = Y1−υ t (Y∗ t )υ`, so consumption's elasticity to output is 1−υ, not one. A subsidy with (1 − τ)(1 − υ)M = 1 — "smaller than necessary to offset firms' market power distortion", to exploit terms-of-trade gains — makes the flexible-price allocation efficient; optimal policy then sets the output gap and domestic inflation to zero (strict domestic inflation targeting, SDIT), implemented by `it = rn t + φππH,t` with φπ > 1 (42). The nominal exchange rate then tracks the natural terms of trade and, inversely, world prices; the CPI inherits the terms-of-trade volatility, potentially high in a very open economy whose productivity is weakly synchronized with world output.
- **Welfare (8.4.3, appendix 8.1)**: losses (43)–(44) are, up to the factor (1 −υ), the closed-economy ones of chapter 4, with domestic inflation as the relevant inflation.

## Main results / findings

- **Monetary policy shock (8.3.1, Figure 8.1)**, chapter 3 calibration with η = 1 and υ = 0.4: as in the closed economy, a tightening lowers output and domestic inflation persistently and raises the real rate unambiguously; if ση > 1 the impact is larger the more open the economy. The terms of trade improve and the nominal exchange rate appreciates in the short and long run. The exchange rate overshoots its long-run level "if and only if" a condition in συ, κυ and ρv holds, which "requires that the shock is not too persistent" and coincides with the condition for the nominal rate to rise. CPI inflation falls sharply on impact with the appreciation, then the depreciation brings it back near its initial level despite persistent domestic deflation; CPI, domestic price level and nominal exchange rate converge to the same negative long-run value. In this special case output and domestic inflation respond as in the closed economy.
- **Robustness of SDIT (8.4)**: Faia and Monacelli (2008) find the optimal policy's variation in domestic prices "almost negligible" quantitatively; de Paoli (2009a) reaches a similar conclusion except with an "(implausibly) high" elasticity of substitution, and even then losses from SDIT are negligible. Nonunitary elasticities, local currency pricing, incomplete markets, preference or cost-push shocks can make SDIT suboptimal.
- **Simple rules (8.5, Table 8.1)**, σ = η = 1, υ = 0.4 ("roughly ... the import/GDP ratio in Canada"), technology shocks only. Rules: SDIT, strict CPI inflation targeting (SCIT), exchange rate peg (PEG), flexible domestic and CPI Taylor rules (FDIT, FCIT: `it = 0.01 + 1.5πH,t + 0.125yt` and the same in CPI inflation, coefficients from Taylor (1993)). Welfare losses (percent of steady-state consumption): 0, 0.05, 0.17, 0.61, 0.43. Output gap volatility: 0, 0.66, 0.92, 0.44, 0.62; domestic inflation: 0, 0.19, 0.35, 0.69, 0.58; nominal depreciation: 1.02, 0.29, 0, 0.95, 0.59. Every simple rule shows "excess smoothness" of the terms of trade and the nominal exchange rate relative to the optimum; the peg delivers the most stable terms of trade but not smaller losses, consistent with Mussa (1986), because sticky prices cannot offset a fixed nominal rate. Both Taylor rules give substantially larger losses than the other three, mainly through domestic inflation volatility; but losses are "quantitatively small under all policy regimes."
- **Literature (8.6)**: Obstfeld and Rogoff (1995) and successors on shock transmission; Galí and Monacelli (2005) on the conditions for an open-economy divine coincidence (producer currency pricing, complete markets, log utility, unit trade elasticity); Monacelli (2005) on imperfect pass-through creating a tradeoff; Campolmi (2014) on sticky wages favoring CPI over domestic inflation targeting; Engel (2011) on local currency pricing favoring CPI stabilization; Clarida, Galí, and Gertler (2002) and others on gains from cooperation in two-country models.

- **Figure 8.1** survives only as panel labels and one axis tick.
- **Table 8.1** is extracted one value per line, and the output and output-gap rows both print as "σ(y)" (the tilde is lost); the values quoted follow the extraction's order, with the gap identified as the row that is zero under SDIT. The first "σ(y)" row and the "σ(s)" row print identical values.
- **Text vs. table**: the text says the PEG regime amplifies "both output gap and domestic inflation volatility to the largest extent", but in the table the peg's domestic inflation volatility (0.35) is below both Taylor rules' (0.69, 0.58) — the statement holds only against SDIT and SCIT. It also refers to a "CITR regime" not defined in the file (presumably strict CPI targeting). Flagged as written.
- The optimal-policy results hold only in the special case σ = η = 1 without preference shocks; the chapter assumes complete markets, the law of one price (producer currency pricing), perfectly competitive labor markets and an infinitesimal economy. The file ends in exercise 8.2(b); exercises are not solved.
