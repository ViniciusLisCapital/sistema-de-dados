## Context and motivation

Before the 1990s central banks were "shrouded in mystery—and believed they should be". The paper traces "a revolution in thinking" from Brunner's 1981 sarcasm about central banking as an "esoteric art", through Blinder's 1996 Robbins lectures (openness makes policy more effective because expectations link short and long rates), to Woodford's 2001 claim that monetary policy is mostly the management of expectations. Practice followed: the FOMC began announcing its decisions in February 1994, published a policy "bias" from May 1999, attributed votes, expedited minutes from February 2005 and expanded forecasts from November 2007; the Reserve Bank of New Zealand, the Bank of England, Norges Bank and the Riksbank went further, and the Fed is "more of a laggard than a leader". A large, mostly empirical literature followed, almost all written in the 2000s; this survey takes stock of it.

## Core argument / thesis

Communication can be "an important and powerful part of the central bank's toolkit": it moves financial markets, improves the predictability of policy, and may help achieve macroeconomic objectives. It works by **"creating news"** (moving expectations and asset prices) and **"reducing noise"** (making actions predictable), raising the signal-to-noise ratio. The arguments against transparency are "thin gruel", and communication need not imply commitment. But no consensus exists on an optimal communication strategy — practices differ widely across otherwise similar central banks and keep evolving — and more talk is not always better.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Illustrative framework (Section 2)**: long rates reflect expected future overnight rates plus a term premium (1); demand depends on short and long real rates (2); a New Keynesian Phillips curve (3) and a reaction function such as a Taylor rule (4) close the model. With a stationary economy, a credibly fixed rule and rational expectations, communication would be redundant — the bank would be transparent "without uttering a word" (Faust and Svensson's definition). Communication matters because of nonstationarity, learning, non-rational expectations or asymmetric information — "the norm, not the exception". Replacing rational expectations with an expectations equation (5) that depends on central bank signals gives three channels for a policy action: the direct effect of the overnight rate (probably small), the direct effect of signals on expected future rates, and the effect of the current rate on the whole expected path and so on long rates. Hence "any account of monetary policy that ignores central bank communication is seriously deficient". At the zero lower bound, communication about expected future rates "becomes the essence of monetary policy".
- **Learning and superior information**: when the public must estimate the reaction function, learning can produce unstable or indeterminate outcomes that communication helps avoid (Bernanke 2004; Orphanides and Williams 2004: a rise in inflation can raise the estimated long-run inflation objective). Central banks may also have better forecasts (Romer and Romer 2000).
- **Downsides**: blackout ("purdah") periods exist, and communicating during them raises volatility. Cukierman and Meltzer's case for opacity rests on only unanticipated money mattering, which the profession no longer believes; Sibert finds transparency always better because it reduces the inflation bias. Morris and Shin's coordination argument (public signals can crowd out private information) requires central bank signals to be much noisier than private ones (Svensson) and hardly applies to information about the bank's own intentions. The "cacophony problem" of committees calls for "clarity, not silence".
- **Communication is not precommitment**: Volcker (1984) and Greenspan (1989) opposed announcing decisions for fear of being seen as committed; conveying information — the decision, the target, the forecast, even New Zealand's published "forward tracks" — commits to nothing, though the conditionality may be hard to convey.
- **Practice (Section 3)**: what is communicated — objectives and strategy (the Bank of England's target set by the Chancellor, the ECB's self-defined quantitative objective, the Fed's non-quantified dual mandate), policy decisions, the economic outlook (fan charts, staff vs. committee forecasts, output gaps published by only a few banks), and the path of future rates (ECB code words like "vigilance"; FOMC bias and balance-of-risks statements; numerical paths in New Zealand, Norway, Sweden and Iceland; the Riksbank's mantra "It is a forecast, not a promise"). How — committee statements, minutes, press conferences (the ECB announces at 1:45 pm and explains 45 minutes later), reports and testimonies, and individual members' speeches; Blinder's typology of individualistic, genuinely collegial and autocratically collegial committees implies different strategies.
- **Measurement (4.1)**: volatility-based event studies (no direction needed), coded statements on dovish–hawkish scales, and market-based measures (Gürkaynak, Sack and Swanson's principal-components "path factor"; the ECB's decision/press-conference timing), each with problems of event dating, endogeneity of timing and subjective coding.

## Main results / findings

- **Predictability (4.2)**: the predictability of Fed, and other major central banks', decisions has improved markedly, and market expectations are now generally aligned with decisions. Same-day announcements since February 1994 reduced surprises (Poole and Rasche) and let the Fed move the funds rate with smaller open market operations (Demiralp and Jorda); private forecasts of the funds rate improved while macro forecasts did not, pointing to communication rather than the Great Moderation (Swanson). Higher-quality inflation reports (19 countries, Fracasso, Genberg and Wyplosz) are associated with smaller policy surprises. The post-May 1999 balance-of-risks statements predict rate moves, yet markets did not anticipate decisions better than before: they extracted the information from statements rather than from speeches and testimonies (Ehrmann and Fratzscher; Chart 1, two tightenings of 25 basis points). Almost nothing is known about published numerical rate paths; Archer finds limited market reactions to the RBNZ's tracks, and "markets do not always uncritically adopt the central bank's projection" in Norway.
- **Market effects (4.3)**: Kohn and Sack (Table 1, 1989-2003): FOMC statements raise the variance of short-to-medium rates (e.g. a 24.1 increase for 3-month-ahead funds futures), Greenspan's testimonies move the whole curve (69.3 for 1-to-2-year forward rates), his other speeches have no significant effect. Bank of England minutes and Inflation Reports move markets (the latter only in intraday data); speeches do not in some studies but do in coded ones: Ehrmann and Fratzscher (Table 2) find speeches and interviews move rates in the intended direction up to five years, with the largest effects for the ECB, 1.5-2.5 basis points on average. The FOMC "path factor" dominates at the long end (Gürkaynak et al. 2005). ECB press conferences move prices more, and volatility less, than the decision announcement — the Q&A clarifies. Minutes matter only when released before the next meeting. Oral FX interventions may be more effective than actual ones and may substitute for rate cuts at the zero bound.
- **Uncertainty and dispersion (4.5)**: more dispersed Fed communication is associated with less predictable decisions (Ehrmann and Fratzscher 2007e); for the Czech National Bank dispersion helps. Bank of England minority votes predict policy changes. Markets weight the chairman or governor more when communication is dispersed, all ECB members equally under its collegial approach. Receivers matter: the Financial Times and the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung report ECB decisions differently, and press coverage across 57 newspapers is more critical after surprises and when euro area inflation exceeds 2%.
- **Anchoring expectations (5.1)**: inflation targeting anchored expectations in several studies — Johnson (lower expected inflation after targets in Australia, Canada, New Zealand and Sweden, not the U.K.), Levin, Natalucci and Piger (expectations de-linked from lagged inflation), Gürkaynak, Levin and Swanson (Tables 3–4: U.S. and pre-independence U.K. long-run inflation compensation reacts to macro news; after 1998 the U.K.'s does not). But Castelnuovo et al. find expectations anchored everywhere except Japan regardless of regime, and forecast-error evidence is mixed (Siklos, 12 countries, vs. Johnson, 11 countries 1984 to 2000, and Corbo et al.).
- **Inflation and persistence (5.2)**: Ball and Sheridan find no effect on level, variability or persistence once regression to the mean is controlled for; Lin and Ye (22 industrial countries, 1985–1999) agree; Vega and Winkelried find benefits in a larger sample. Fatás, Mihov and Rose (42 countries over 40 years): any quantitative target — inflation, exchange rate or money — lowers inflation. Persistence is much lower under inflation targeting (Table 5, Benati: sum of autoregressive coefficients -0.33 for Canada and -0.13 for the U.K. under targeting, against 0.90 and 0.78 from 1971 to targeting), but also low under Bretton Woods.
- **Assessment (Section 6)**: predictability has improved by "reducing noise" (an issue the authors are "tempted to call ... 'closed'"); conflicting voices degrade it; official statements, reports and minutes have the clearest market effects; the few directional studies find markets move the "right" way; inflation targeting clearly helps anchor expectations but "is not the only way", and the evidence that it lowers inflation is "far less compelling". Open questions: the effects of published rate paths (the "new frontier"), optimal communication given committee structure (the Bank of England's nine individually accountable members vs. larger group-accountable committees vs. New Zealand's single decisionmaker), and communication with the general public.

## Limitations and caveats

- **Equations (1)–(5) are damaged**: Greek letters print as "(cid:...)" codes and subscripts are split onto separate lines, so the formulas read as fragments. The text's description of each is readable; the note describes them in words.
- **Table 2's column headers are scrambled** ("Bank of / Federal Reserve ECB / England"), so which column belongs to which central bank cannot be read with certainty from the file; the note cites only the text's statement about the ECB. Table 5's symbol for the persistence parameter prints as a code, though the note to the table defines it. **Chart 1** survives only as axis values and a legend.
- The **cover pages are cut** (author affiliations and some front matter missing), and **the reference list announced in the contents is not in the file**, so citations cannot be resolved from the clean_md.
- By the authors' account the literature is mostly about advanced economies and communication with financial markets; there is "almost no evidence" on effects on the real economy, and Section 3 examines mainly three central banks (Fed, ECB, Bank of England). The coding of statements is subjective and event studies face endogeneity of communication timing.
- Survey as of May 2008: it predates the crisis-era expansion of forward guidance, and published policy-rate paths had too short a history to evaluate.
