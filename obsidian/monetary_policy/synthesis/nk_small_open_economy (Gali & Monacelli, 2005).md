## Context and motivation

New Keynesian models bring imperfect competition and nominal rigidities into dynamic general equilibrium, so monetary policy rules can be studied as stabilization tools. Most open-economy work had one-period advance price setting and an exogenous money supply process. This paper lays out a small open economy (SOE) version of the Calvo staggered-price model with monetary policy modeled as an endogenous interest rate rule — which, the authors argue, "accords much better with the practice of modern central banks" — and uses it to compare three regimes: domestic inflation targeting (DIT), CPI inflation targeting (CIT) and an exchange rate peg (PEG). The rest of the world is treated as a closed economy of which the SOE is a negligible part.

## Core argument / thesis

Under complete international asset markets and the law of one price, the SOE's log-linear equilibrium reduces to a "canonical" two-equation system in domestic inflation and the output gap — a New Keynesian Phillips curve and an IS-type equation — of the same form as the closed-economy model. Openness changes only (a) coefficients, through the degree of openness α and the home/foreign elasticity of substitution η, and (b) the natural levels of output and the interest rate, which depend on foreign as well as domestic shocks; the closed economy is nested as α = 0. The regimes then line up along "a clear tradeoff between the stabilization of both the nominal and real exchange rates, on the one hand, and the stabilization of inflation and the output gap on the other": DIT stabilizes domestic prices and the output gap but requires substantially larger exchange rate volatility; the peg is the converse; CIT is a hybrid (equal to DIT as α → 0, to the peg as α → 1). In the special case σ = η = 1 with a suitable employment subsidy, DIT is optimal, and CIT and the peg lose welfare because of "excess smoothness" of the real exchange rate — but the losses are quantitatively small.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Households (2.1)**: consumption is a CES composite (2) of home and foreign goods; α is the import share of consumption in a steady state with equal prices, "a natural index of openness"; η is the home/foreign substitution elasticity. Complete, internationally traded contingent claims; money appears only as unit of account (interest-rate rule).
- **Identities (2.1.1)**: CPI = domestic prices + α times the terms of trade (10); CPI inflation = domestic inflation + α × change in the terms of trade (11); with the law of one price the terms of trade are the exchange rate plus foreign prices minus domestic prices (12); the real exchange rate is (1 − α) times the terms of trade (13).
- **International risk sharing (2.1.2)**: complete markets link home consumption to world consumption and the real exchange rate (15), giving home consumption = world consumption plus a term in the terms of trade (16).
- **UIP (2.1.3)**: the bond pricing conditions give `rt − r∗t = Et{∆et+1}` (17); with long-run PPP the terms of trade solve forward as current and expected real interest rate differentials (19) — "a convenient (and intuitive) way of representing" their dynamics, but "not an additional independent equilibrium condition".
- **Firms (2.2)**: linear technology with AR(1) productivity; Calvo pricing with reset probability 1 − θ gives newly set prices as a markup over a discounted sum of expected nominal marginal costs (21).
- **Demand side (3.1)**: output = world output + (ωα/σ) × terms of trade (25), with ωα depending on α, σ and η; an open-economy IS equation (29) in which output also depends on expected world output growth, with sign set by whether ση > 1. Net exports (30) depend on a parameter Λ whose sign is "our model's analog to the Marshall-Lerner conditions"; with σ = η = 1 trade is balanced at all times.
- **Supply side (3.2)**: domestic inflation follows an NKPC in real marginal cost (33); marginal cost rises with the terms of trade and world output through the wealth effect on labor supply and the product wage (34)–(35).
- **Canonical representation (3.3)**: natural output (39) depends on domestic productivity and world output; the SOE NKPC (40) has slope κα, equal to the closed-economy slope when α = 0 or ση = 1; if ση > 1 more openness flattens it. The IS equation in the output gap (41) has interest sensitivity ωα/σ, raised by openness if ση > 1, and a natural rate that depends on expected world output growth as well as domestic productivity.
- **The three regimes (4)**, with the world economy assumed to stabilize its own prices and output gap:
  - **DIT (4.1)**: output gap and domestic inflation zero; the nominal exchange rate moves one-for-one with the terms of trade and with the productivity differential (42)–(43), depreciating after a relative rise in home productivity; its variance (44) rises with the asymmetry of home and world shocks in size and comovement. The CPI and real exchange rate inherit the productivity differential.
  - **CIT (4.2)**: domestic prices move with the terms of trade (45) and follow a second-order difference equation (47)–(48); domestic prices fall after a relative productivity rise; nominal and real exchange rates coincide (49) and acquire endogenous persistence.
  - **PEG (4.3)**: equivalent to adopting the world currency; the same structure as CIT with a smaller root parameter, so domestic prices and the output gap adjust more strongly and persistently than under CIT; the CPI falls after a relative productivity rise.
  - All three imply a stationary price level and exchange rate, "in stark contrast" with the data; DIT and CIT are limits of Taylor-type rules as the inflation coefficient goes to infinity, and the flexible versions would give non-stationary prices and exchange rates (results "not displayed here").
- **Optimal policy (5)**: in the world (closed) economy a subsidy offsetting market power makes zero inflation and a zero gap optimal, with the policy rate equal to the natural rate (51). In the SOE there is a second distortion, the incentive to manipulate the terms of trade (citing Corsetti and Pesenti); with σ = η = 1, consumption equals a Cobb-Douglas function of home and world output, and a subsidy satisfying (1 − τ)(1 − α) = 1 − 1/ε makes the flexible-price allocation optimal, so DIT is optimal.
- **Welfare (5.2.1, Appendix 3)**: a second-order approximation (52) gives losses (53) proportional to (1 − α) times a weighted sum of the variances of domestic inflation and the output gap — the closed-economy form, with domestic rather than CPI inflation.

## Main results / findings

- **Calibration (4.4)**: σ = 1 (log utility), η = 1, ϕ = 3 (labor supply elasticity of 1/3), steady-state markup µ = 1.2 (ε = 6), θ = 0.75 (one year between price adjustments on average), β = 0.99 (riskless annual return of about 4 percent), ρa = 0.9, productivity variance set so world natural output has a 2 percent standard deviation, α = 0.4 ("roughly to the share of imports in GDP for Canada"), and a 0.77 correlation of home and world shocks estimated from quarterly Canadian and U.S. productivity data.
- **Domestic productivity shock (Figure 1, described in the text)**: under DIT the domestic rate falls persistently and, by UIP, the currency depreciates on impact and is then expected to appreciate; the larger real depreciation raises net exports. CPI inflation rises at first, then turns negative. CIT requires a more contractionary policy (an interest rate that rises at first and then falls) and a more muted depreciation, with output gap and inflation falling sharply; the peg amplifies these responses because the nominal rate cannot fall nor the currency depreciate.
- **Table 1 (standard deviations in %, both shocks)**: output gap 0.000 / 0.286 / 0.428 under DIT / CIT / PEG; domestic inflation 0.000 / 0.142 / 0.267; CPI inflation 0.242 / 0.000 / 0.160; real exchange rate 0.812 / 0.724 / 0.666; nominal exchange rate 1.353 / 0.724 / 0.000; output 2.007 / 1.971 / 1.955. With domestic shocks only, the nominal exchange rate's standard deviation is 2.007 under DIT and 1.074 under CIT. The real exchange rate is most stable under the peg — consistent with Mussa (1986), "excess smoothness" under fixed rates because sticky prices cannot compensate for the fixed nominal rate. Exchange rates are far less volatile relative to output than in the data (footnote 16 quotes relative real-exchange-rate volatility from Backus et al. of 1.91 for the U.S., 2.0 for Canada, 1.76 for Germany and 1.95 for the U.K.).
- **Sensitivity (4.4.1, Figures 2–4, described in the text)**: the ranking of regimes is robust. A higher η lowers exchange rate volatility (to zero as goods become perfect substitutes). With σ = η = 1, output gap and inflation volatility under DIT and the peg do not depend on openness, while CIT moves from DIT to the peg as α rises; more openness lowers real exchange rate volatility, "unlike the conventional wisdom". Under DIT exchange rate volatility does not depend on price stickiness θ; under CIT it goes to zero as θ → 1.
- **Table 2 (welfare loss, % of steady-state consumption, relative to DIT)**: benchmark, both shocks: CIT −0.005, PEG −0.017 (domestic shocks only: −0.011 and −0.038). Lowering the markup to 1.1 (ε = 11) and the labor supply elasticity to 0.1 together raises the losses to −0.013 (CIT) and −0.053 (PEG) with both shocks and to −0.029 and −0.118 with domestic shocks only. The peg is always worse than CIT, and inflation variance accounts for most of each loss, but "the losses continue to remain quantitatively small."
- **Openness and comovement (5.3, Figures 5–6, described in the text)**: for every α in (0, 1) CIT and the peg lose relative to DIT, with CIT always better than the peg; at α = 0 DIT and CIT coincide; as α → 1 all rules converge to DIT. The peg is the only regime whose welfare rises monotonically with openness — "a lower degree of openness makes limiting exchange rate flexibility more costly". As the correlation of home and world shocks approaches one, the cost of any regime relative to DIT vanishes, since mimicking the world central bank's rate costs nothing.
- **Extensions flagged (6)**: a more general welfare function, a two-country version (spillovers, coordination), sticky wages (Erceg, Henderson and Levin), and incomplete pass-through under local currency pricing; footnote 15 notes that lower pass-through would make CIT relatively more desirable.

## Limitations and caveats

- **Version**: the clean_md is NBER Working Paper 8905 (April 2002), not the 2005 published article the file name and tracker refer to; any differences between the two are not visible here.
- **Equations are damaged throughout**: fractions, sums and integrals are spread over several lines; the hat on real marginal cost prints as a separate "c"; the tilde of the output gap prints as a leading "e" (`eyt`), and the bar marking natural output and the natural rate is lost, so (39), (41) and the gap definition print natural and actual output with the same symbol. The equations are described in words here, not restored.
- **Figures 1–6 are not in the file**; their results are taken only from the text's description.
- **Table 2's parameter labels are garbled** ("=1.2, $=3", "=1.1, $=10"): the symbols are lost; they are read here as the markup and ϕ via the panel titles and the text (markup 1.1 with ε = 11, labor supply elasticity 0.1). Table 1 is extracted one value per line but its row and column order is consistent with the text.
- The optimal-policy and welfare results hold only for σ = η = 1 with the assumed subsidy; the model assumes complete markets, the law of one price (full pass-through), Calvo prices only (no wage rigidity) and a world economy that pursues optimal policy. The authors note the stationarity of prices and exchange rates under all three regimes is at odds with the data.
