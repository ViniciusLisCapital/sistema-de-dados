## Context and motivation

Three years into Brazil's inflation targeting regime (adopted July 1999, after the January float), inflation had met the target in 1999 and 2000 but breached the upper limit in 2001 and was expected to do so again in 2002, under an energy crisis, the September 11 attacks, the Argentine crisis, rising risk aversion and uncertainty about the incoming government — all of which depreciated the Real — and a surge in administered prices. Written by BCB research staff and a Deputy Governor, the paper asks whether the regime was nonetheless working, frames three challenges it shares with other emerging markets (building credibility, a change in relative prices, exchange rate volatility), describes the BCB's method for separating the sources of inflation, and takes positions on design questions (core vs. headline, escape clauses, band width, target setting and horizon).

## Core argument / thesis

Inflation outcomes are "not a sufficient statistic" for judging an inflation-targeting central bank; expectations, the role of the target in forming them, and the shocks hitting the economy matter more. On those criteria the regime worked: the BCB reacted strongly and forward-lookingly to expected inflation, the targets coordinated expectations even while actual inflation exceeded the band, and inflation persistence and macro volatility fell. The breaches came from supply and cost-push shocks — administered-price increases and exchange rate depreciation — whose first-round effects the BCB chose to accommodate while neutralizing their second-round effects. Credibility is "still under construction", held back by the absence of legal central bank independence, so the design should favor credibility: keep headline inflation, no escape clauses, but wider bands and realistic targets.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Credibility through a forward-looking reaction.** The Selic responds to the expected deviation of inflation from target (a weighted average of current- and next-year deviations, the weights depending on the months left in the year; two-year-ahead expectations are left out because Brazilian transmission lags are shorter and forecasts less certain) and to the output gap, with smoothing (equation (1)). Because the equilibrium real rate was falling from the very high levels of the peg era, holding the Selic constant was effectively a tightening, which is why the paper also estimates the rule on the Selic's deviation from an HP trend.
- **Accommodate the first round, offset the second.** Since 2001, and systematically since January 2002, policy lets relative-price shocks move inflation once but neutralizes their propagation. The optimal reaction to a supply shock depends on inflation aversion, the slope of the Phillips curve and the shock's persistence; temporary or small shocks call for little or no response, and a longer target horizon means a smaller response.
- **Administered prices as a distinct inflation process.** Prices little affected by domestic supply and demand or regulated by a public agency (oil by-products, fixed telephone, residential electricity, public transportation; group defined by the Copom in July 2001) differ from market prices in three ways: dependence on international (oil) prices, higher exchange-rate pass-through (oil priced in domestic currency, electricity partly linked to the exchange rate, contracts indexed to the IGP, which is more exposed to the exchange rate), and stronger backward-looking indexation through annual contractual adjustments. The first and third are outside monetary policy's reach and the second only partly within it; strong backward-looking behavior makes disinflation more costly, especially with declining targets.
- **The BCB's decomposition (section 4).** The "primary effect" of an administered-price shock is administered inflation above the target, weighted by the group's IPCA share, minus the part due to inertia from the previous year and to exchange-rate changes — inertia is excluded because policy should neutralize propagation (the BCB's rule of thumb is 18 months to offset inertial effects), the exchange rate because policy affects it and it may reflect demand shocks. The Copom then compares its forecast with a target adjusted by half of the inherited inertia plus the primary effect. Simplifications: the same inertia and pass-through for all goods.
- **Exchange rate volatility.** Emerging markets unable to borrow in their own currency face balance-sheet mismatch and self-fulfilling crises; the BCB runs a dirty float, with interventions made as transparent as possible so the public does not read exchange-rate stability as taking precedence over price stability.
- **Design trade-offs.** Core inflation is less noisy but less representative of purchasing power, and in Brazil excluding administered items would leave out over 30% of the basket and evoke the ad hoc index manipulations of the 1970s and 1980s; a trimmed mean loses communicability. Escape clauses help judge the bank but signal leniency, so a young regime should rely on bands instead. Bands must widen with model uncertainty (short low-inflation samples, possible breaks) and with the frequency and size of shocks. Targets should not be changed — except that pursuing targets seen as unattainable would itself cost credibility.

## Main results / findings

The evidence supports the credibility story on all three tests the authors run, with a short sample throughout.
- **Reaction function (Tables 2–3, monthly, from 1999 to June 2002)**: the coefficient on the expected deviation from target is above one in every specification — around 1.4-1.8 with the Selic as dependent variable and above 3.6 with the Selic gap, where it is significantly different from one. With the Inflation Report forecast and the Selic, the coefficient of 1.84 has a p-value of 0.13. Smoothing is high (the two lags sum to 0.8 or above). The output gap is insignificant with market expectations and wrong-signed with Inflation Report forecasts; the exchange rate change is not significant.
- **Expectations anchored to the target (Table 4, 2000:01-2002:06)**: 12-month-ahead market expectations stayed below the upper band limit even after actual inflation passed it in mid-2001; regressed on their own lags, the interest rate and the target, expectations respond strongly to the target (0.96), and adding past 12-month inflation gives an insignificant coefficient — expectations look forward and the target matters. The low correlation between actual and expected inflation points the same way.
- **Lower persistence (Table 5, monthly, Real Plan to 2002:06)**: in a backward-looking supply curve with interaction dummies for the targeting period, the first autoregressive coefficient falls from 0.74 to 0.15 and, with two lags, the sum of the lags falls from 0.69 to -0.19 — a lower output cost of disinflation. The unemployment coefficient does not change. Output, inflation and interest-rate volatility are lower under targeting (Table 1), though the authors note this need not mean a better trade-off, since it also depends on the shocks.
- **Pass-through is low but uneven**: the BCB structural model puts one-year pass-through at 12% for market prices and 25% for administered prices (about 16% for the IPCA); the VAR gives administered-price pass-through 2.5 times that of market prices (Table 7). The inflationary pressure came from the size of the depreciation more than from the coefficient. The exchange rate accounts for 38% of 2001 inflation and 48% of January-August 2002 inflation in the BCB decomposition. A VAR on the targeting period alone is too short to be significant and its point estimates resemble the full sample, unlike Muinhos (2001), who finds a structural break when the regime changed.
- **Relative prices and core**: relative-price dispersion Granger-causes inflation (and vice versa) (Table 6); the BCB's 20% trimmed mean core is less volatile than headline and Granger-causes it without being caused by it (Table 9), so it is useful as information, not as the target.
- **Policy conclusions**: keep the headline IPCA; the CMN's widening of the band from 2 to 2.5 percentage points for 2003 and 2004, and the upward revision of the 2003 target from 3.25% to 4%, are defended as consistent with credibility.

## Limitations and caveats

- **Authors' caveats**: three years of data; the reaction-function and VAR estimates on the targeting period alone are imprecise or insignificant; the Table 5 supply curve differs from the BCB's quarterly structural model (no forward-looking term, unemployment instead of the gap); the decomposition assumes identical inertia and pass-through across goods; the trade-off between credibility and flexibility when the forecast nears the band's upper limit has "no clear answer". Lower volatility under targeting may reflect smaller shocks.
- **What the text shows**: the regime's success is judged on expectations and on the BCB's own models, written by the BCB; the evidence that expectations are forward-looking rests on a 30-month regression. Inconsistency: section 5.3 states that exchange-rate shocks explain 24.9% and 32.8% of the forecast error variance of inflation for the whole Real Plan and the targeting period, while section 3.3 gives 23.0% for the IPCA over the whole sample (24.9% is the figure for administered prices). Flagged as written.
- **Damaged passages**: equations (1)–(6), the two-lag reaction function in footnote 12 and the trimmed-mean formula in footnote 35 are reduced to lists of symbols; their content is described above from the text. Tables 1–9 are flattened into single columns (Table 1's headers come after its numbers; Tables 2–5 list entries by row with column headers at the end), so only figures the text itself states are cited. Figures 1–13 survive only as axis values or labels; the impulse responses (Figures 11–12) cannot be read.
