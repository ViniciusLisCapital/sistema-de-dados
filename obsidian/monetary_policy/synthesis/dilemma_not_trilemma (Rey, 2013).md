## Context and motivation

International macroeconomics reasons within the "trilemma": with free capital mobility, fixed exchange rates export the centre country's monetary policy, and floating rates restore monetary independence (citing Obstfeld and Taylor 2004). Rey asks whether the scale of financial globalization and the role of global banks undermine even this: is there a global financial cycle, what drives it, and do financing conditions set in the main financial centres "set the tone for the rest of the world, regardless of the exchange-rate regime?" She also asks whether the gains from capital mobility are large enough to justify bearing that cycle.

## Core argument / thesis

There is a global financial cycle in gross capital flows, asset prices and credit growth; it co-moves with the VIX (uncertainty and risk aversion), it "is not aligned with countries' specific macroeconomic conditions", and markets receiving more credit inflows are more sensitive to it. A VAR suggests one of its determinants is monetary policy in the centre country (the US), working through the leverage of global banks, cross-border credit flows and credit growth. Hence floating exchange rates "cannot insulate economies from the global financial cycle, when capital is mobile": the trilemma "morphs into a 'dilemma'" — "independent monetary policies are possible if and only if the capital account is managed, directly or indirectly, regardless of the exchange-rate regime" (an "irreconcilable duo"). Since the gains from capital flows "have proved elusive", the paper weighs four options: targeted capital controls, internalizing the centre's spillovers, cyclical macroprudential policy, and structurally tighter leverage limits.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Gross, not net, flows (I.A)**: heatmaps of quarterly IMF flows (1990Q1–2012Q4) show strong positive correlation of gross inflows, and of gross outflows, across regions and asset types (FDI excepted, plus some Asian and African flows), but no systematic pattern for net flows. Credit ("other investment": bank loans and trade credit) is the most volatile and procyclical component.
- **The VIX as index of the cycle (I.B, II)**: inflows rise when the VIX is low; credit growth falls with the VIX everywhere; leverage rises when the VIX is low in the main financial centres. A single global factor explains an important share of the variance of a large cross-section of risky asset prices (from Miranda-Agrippino and Rey 2012) and is closely, negatively correlated with the VIX. Its structural reading: market risk appetite and realized volatility, with risk appetite tied to the leverage of VaR-constrained intermediaries.
- **Feedback loop (II, IV)**: more credit supply raises asset prices and compresses spreads; measured risk falls, value-at-risk constraints relax and leave room for more lending — "a positive feedback loop between loose monetary policy, fall in the VIX, rise in credit, capital flows and leverage and further fall in the VIX". The dollar is the main currency of global banking; European global banks channelled US dollar liquidity worldwide before the crisis, so their leverage is a key VAR variable.
- **Cross-section (III)**: fixed-effects panel regressions of stock returns, bank leverage growth and house price inflation on the VIX (logged), its change, and interactions with each country's flows (normalized by GDP), with controls (lagged GDP growth, lagged nominal effective exchange rate) and a linear trend.
- **Recursive VAR (IV.A)**: quarterly 1990–2012, Cholesky ordering US GDP, GDP deflator, global credit, global credit inflows, median EU bank leverage, Fed funds rate, VIX (last). Two lags; confidence bands from 1000 bootstrap replications. Only partial identification is needed for the shocks of interest (VIX, Fed funds, leverage).
- **Policy options (VII)**: (a) capital controls, permanent or temporary (e.g. on credit and portfolio debt in booms), as "partial substitutes" for macroprudential tools; (b) a forum of systemically important central banks to internalize spillovers (Eichengreen et al. 2012 proposal, under the BIS Committee on the Global Financial System); (c) cyclical macroprudential measures — countercyclical capital buffer, loan-to-value and debt-to-income ratios, triggered by credit-to-GDP rules or frequent stress tests; (d) a tougher leverage ratio for all intermediaries.

## Main results / findings

- **Co-movement (I)**: credit and portfolio debt inflows have a correlation of 0.52; credit inflows' correlation with the VIX (inverted scale) is 0.24 over 1990Q1–2012Q4. Table 1(a): inflow/VIX correlations are negative for equity, debt and credit in nearly all regions (equity down to −0.36) and positive for FDI in all regions (0.06 to 0.23). Table 1(b), conditioning on the world real short rate and world growth: same pattern, VIX "significantly negatively associated" with inflows except FDI. Table 1(c): credit growth negatively linked to the VIX in all areas; leverage and leverage growth negatively related in North America, Western Europe and Asia but positively in Latin America, CEE and Africa.
- **Global factor (II)**: 858 risky asset prices on five continents; one global factor explains 25% of the variance of risky returns, matching the Gulf War, 9/11, the Russian crisis and LTCM, the dot-com bust and the 2007–09 crisis (Figure 3, described).
- **Cross-section (Table 2(a))**, stock returns 1990–2013: coefficient on the VIX about −0.0952 (t = −12.64), on its change about −0.1743 (t = −15.14), significant in every column; credit-flow interactions are negative (higher sensitivity), equity-flow and debt-outflow interactions positive (lower sensitivity); adjusted R² 0.222 to 0.255. For bank leverage growth and house prices (Appendix C) the VIX coefficients are negative but no flow interaction is ever significant. The text stresses these are correlations, not causality.
- **VAR (IV.A, Figures 4a, 4b, 5)**: (i) a Fed funds increase raises the VIX after about 5 quarters and until 11; (ii) a VIX increase lowers European bank leverage; (iii) a fall in the VIX raises cross-border credit flows up to 6 quarters; (iv) a VIX increase lowers global domestic credit from 4 quarters on; (v) a VIX increase lowers the Fed funds rate; (vi) a Fed funds increase lowers EU bank leverage after 15 quarters and (vii) gross credit flows after 12 quarters; (viii) higher EU bank leverage goes with higher domestic credit from 1 quarter and (ix) a lower VIX after about 8 quarters. A VIX increase also lowers US GDP and the deflator. Robust to the 1990–2007 sample, dropping variables and dropping a lag; consistent with Bekaert et al. (2012) and Bruno and Shin (2013b).
- **Economic significance (IV.C)**: Fed funds shocks explain about 4% of the VIX variance (7-variable VAR, 1990–2012) to about 10% (4-variable VAR, 1990–2007), and 17% with US broker-dealer instead of EU bank leverage; Bruno and Shin find almost 30% beyond 10 quarters, Bekaert et al. over 20% of risk-aversion variance beyond 7 quarters (about 12% in their six-variable VAR). "Economically significant, possibly large effects."
- **Gains from capital flows (VI)**: calibrated neoclassical models give small gains (Gourinchas and Jeanne: second order; Coeurdacier et al.: "a few tenths of a percent of permanent consumption"); despite cross-border positions rising from 68% to 438% of GDP for advanced economies (1980 to 2007) and from 35% to 73% for emerging markets, robust growth or risk-sharing effects are hard to find. Large external balance sheets can also share risk: about 2 trillion dollars of valuation losses on the US net external position in the crisis acted as a transfer to the rest of the world. "We cannot take them for granted."
- **Policy assessment**: capital controls have been used mostly to prevent appreciation (Chilean encaje 1991–1998; the 2010 and 2011 Brazilian taxes on equity inflows); their effectiveness is debated and permanent controls are hard to evaluate. Macroprudential policy can weaken the link between domestic monetary policy and capital inflows without controls. International cooperation "seems out of reach" because it may conflict with domestic mandates, and "the rest of the world cannot at the same time complain of excessive capital inflows due to loose monetary policy in the centre countries and wish for a higher level of economic activity and demand stimulus in the same countries." Monetary policy "is only one of the drivers" of the cycle.

## Limitations and caveats

- **The Part III regression equation and the dependent-variable symbols are unreadable**: they print as "(cid:…)" glyph codes; the specification is described here from the surrounding text only.
- **Figures 1–5 are not in the file**: only titles, a description of Figure 3, and, after Appendix D, several hundred lines of unlabeled numbers and region labels from the heatmaps and the Figure 2 axis, which cannot be assigned to cells. No impulse-response magnitudes are available, only the text's qualitative findings (i)–(ix).
- **Inconsistencies as printed**: the text says the panel has "between 2770 and 3462" observations, but Table 2(a)'s N row runs from 2631 to 3267; several t-statistics in Appendix C tables have the opposite sign to their coefficient (e.g. the C.nonbank column); confidence bands are given as "95 and 86%". Appendix A puts Australia, Japan, Korea and New Zealand under "Emerging Asia" and China, Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand under "Asia", while the text calls one Asian group "developed economies in Asia" — the column labels may be swapped, so region-specific correlations are not cited here.
- The evidence is correlational (Parts I–III) and recursive-VAR based (Part IV); the author notes that establishing causality from cross-border credit flows would need instruments. The data end in 2012–2013.
