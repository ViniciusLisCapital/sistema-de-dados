## Context and motivation

Estimates of the effects of monetary policy are only as good as the policy measure. Conventional measures have two flaws. **Endogenous movements**: money rises in good times because the multiplier rises, and the funds rate moves day to day for non-policy reasons and, when the Fed targeted it loosely, with economic conditions — biasing estimates of the output effect of rate increases toward zero. **Anticipatory movements**: even a target series is not random, because the Fed forecasts heavily and acts on the forecast — it cuts rates when it sees a recession coming, so output need not rise after a cut even if the cut is stimulative. The paper builds a US shock series for 1969–1996 that addresses both, and compares its implications with the change in the actual funds rate.

## Core argument / thesis

Considering only **intended** funds rate changes decided at FOMC meetings, and removing the part that is the Fed's usual response to its own **Greenbook forecasts**, yields a shock series "relatively free of endogenous and anticipatory movements". With it, "policy has large, relatively rapid, and statistically significant effects on both output and inflation", "substantially stronger and quicker than those obtained using conventional indicators". The contrast — and the strong price puzzle when the actual funds rate is used — implies that endogenous and anticipatory movements have led to underestimates of the effects of policy, and that controlling for commodity prices is "not enough" to fix the funds rate as a measure.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Step 1 — intended funds rate around FOMC meetings (section I.A)**: for each meeting, the intended rate at the time of the forecast and the intended rate after the meeting's decision, from the narrative record (Record of Policy Actions, Minutes through March 1976, Transcripts from February 1981, Bluebooks) checked against internal memos giving the "expected federal funds rate" (January 1971 to December 1984 and December 1983 to September 1992), which track Rudebusch's (1995) target where they overlap. The series is built judgmentally, not mechanically: only changes decided at the meeting count, and gradual implementation is treated as an immediate change in intentions. Strong, explicit asymmetry in a no-change decision is scored as half the usual move of the era (e.g. 1/8 of a point in the mid-1970s, 1/4 point or more in the early Burns era), adjusted by the narrative. It covers episodes missing from target series, such as the Burns expansion of the early 1970s and the Volcker disinflation; the early Volcker nonborrowed-reserve period is the least certain. The series is given in Table A1.
- **Step 2 — purge the Fed's forecasts (section I.B, equation 1)**: the change in the intended rate at meeting m is regressed on the pre-meeting intended level (mean reversion), Greenbook forecasts of real output growth and inflation for the previous, current and next two quarters, the changes in those forecasts since the previous meeting, and the current-quarter unemployment forecast. Greenbooks are a good proxy for the Fed's information: issued six days before the meeting, central to deliberations, and better than private forecasts (Romer & Romer 2000); members' own forecasts differ little (average absolute differences of 0.28, 0.25 and 0.17 percentage points for growth, inflation and unemployment over 35 meetings). Forecasts beyond two quarters are excluded partly because the Greenbook assumes no policy change in the very short run. The goal is not to fit the reaction function but to remove forecast-driven moves and keep the rest — so the sample is not split, since changes in tastes or operating procedures are legitimate shocks.
- **The shock series (section I.C, Table 2)**: the residuals of (1), assigned to the month of the meeting (summed if two meetings, zero if none — a zero, not a missing value).
- **What the shocks contain**: operating procedures (quantity targeting in 1979-1982 and the early 1970's), beliefs (inflation thought unresponsive to slack in the early 1970s: negative shocks in 1971 and 1972), tastes and goals (upward spikes in 1969, 1973-1974 and 1979-1982, the dates of the 1989 Romer & Romer episodes), politics (Burns's expansionary policy at the start of the Carter administration: negative values in 1977), other objectives (the strong dollar cited for easing in late 1984 and early 1985), and random factors. Excluded, at a cost in precision but not bias: moves based on erroneous preliminary data and all intermeeting moves.
- **Comparison measures**: the change in the actual funds rate (monthly average); the change in the intended rate without forecast controls (endogeneity addressed, anticipation not); and the residual of the actual funds rate change on the forecasts (anticipation partly addressed, endogeneity not).
- **Effects (section II)**: single-equation regressions of monthly growth of non-seasonally-adjusted industrial production (equation 2: monthly dummies, 24 own lags, 36 lags of the shock, no contemporaneous shock) and of the PPI for finished goods (equation 3: 48 lags of the shock), 1970:1–1996:12; cumulative responses to a one-percentage-point shock, Monte Carlo standard errors with 500 draws. Then commodity prices as a supply-shock control, and VARs following Christiano et al. (1996) with three years of lags and the shock cumulated.

## Main results / findings

**The reaction to forecasts (Table 1; N = 263, R² = 0.28, 1969:3–1996:12).** The FOMC behaves countercyclically: the sum of coefficients on growth forecasts is 0.04 (t = 2.5) and on their changes 0.24 (t = 4.0), so a one-point rise in forecast growth at all horizons raises the intended rate by 29 basis points; the largest single effect is the change in the current-quarter growth forecast (0.15, t = 5.1); current-quarter unemployment enters negatively (-0.048). It resists forecast inflation: sums of 0.04 (t = 2.3) on levels and 0.03 (t = 0.3) on changes, seven basis points for a one-point rise. The coefficient on the initial level is close to zero (-0.021, t = 1.8), so it is the change in the rate that responds — the estimates say nothing about how the level adjusts over longer horizons. The R² of 0.28 means a substantial share of actions responded to forecasts, and many did not. Alternative specifications (quadratic trend, industrial production forecasts, all unemployment horizons, pre/post-1983 split) give shock series correlated over 0.97 with the baseline.

**The new series versus the funds rate.** Contemporaneous correlation 0.43. They diverge where forecasts matter: in 1977 and 1978 the funds rate rose while the new series shows a string of negative shocks — the Fed raised rates by less than its forecasts of rapid growth and high inflation warranted; in early 1974 the new series shows extreme tightening while the funds rate barely moved, because the Fed was forecasting severe decline.

**Output (Table 3, Figure 2).** The cumulative effect is slightly positive for four months, declines roughly linearly through month 22, and is "essentially flat at -4.3 percent in months 22 through 27", weakening to -1.7 percent at month 48. t-statistics exceed 2.5 in months 17 through 27; the interval at month 22 is (-7.0%, -1.4%), at month 48 (-6.4%, +3.0%). The positive first-lag coefficient comes from April 1980 (a shock of -3.2 percentage points; setting it to zero lowers the coefficient from 0.0038 to 0.0023). Robust to dropping October 1979–May 1981 (peak -3.4 percent), to 48 lags (-0.8 percent at month 48) and to the pre/post-1983 shock series (peak -3.9 percent). With the actual funds rate the effect is "substantially slower and considerably smaller": close to zero through month 10, less than a third as large through month 17, -2.4 percent in month 30; both intermediate measures look like the funds rate, which points to an interaction between the two corrections. Rescaling for the funds rate's greater volatility closes only about half the gap (footnote 19). Their earlier anti-inflation dates gave a 12 percent fall after 32 months, but for shocks averaging 3.6 percentage points (footnote 18).

**Prices (Table 4, Figures 4–6).** The PPI response is "virtually zero for the first 22 months" (at most 0.3 percent above baseline, insignificant), then falls: -1.9 percent after 30 months and -5.9 percent after 48, with t reaching 2 in month 29, 4 in month 37 and 5 in month 43; interval at 48 months (-8.0%, -3.7%). With the CPI excluding shelter or the PCE index the 48-month effect is -3.6 percent (t = 4.3 and 5.0). The pre/post-1983 series gives -6.6 percent. The authors read the delay as genuine rather than residual anticipation, because there is no early positive effect and adding commodity prices does not speed the response. With the actual funds rate the price level rises over the first two years and then stays flat — a strong price puzzle; at 48 months the interval is (-1.4%, +3.3%) against (-8.0%, -3.7%). Controlling for forecasts with the actual funds rate brings the price level down only after 25 months and by 1.9 percent at 48 (t = 2.8): for prices, anticipatory movements are the more important source of bias.

**Commodity prices (Figures 7–8).** With the new measure, short-run responses are unchanged (output for five months, prices for 20); later, output effects shrink slightly (-1.8 rather than -2.1 percent at 12 months, -3.2 rather than -4.3 at 24) and price effects shrink too (-4.4 rather than -5.9 percent at 48) — the opposite of what contamination by supply-shock responses would produce. With the actual funds rate, controlling for commodity prices does not even remove the price puzzle in this specification, and output effects stay two to three times weaker for two years.

**VARs (Figure 9).** The cumulated shock falls to about half its initial level after a year, so the experiment is less persistent. Output peaks at -2.9 percent (against 4.3 in the single equation) and returns to its initial level; prices fall 0.5 percent after 18 months, 2.4 after 30 and 5.0 after 48. Adding commodity prices: output peak -1.9 percent after 22 months (t = 2.7), prices -3.8 percent after 48 (t = 4.5). The funds-rate VAR with commodity prices removes the price puzzle (peak +0.1 percent after seven months) but gives -0.5 percent at 48 months and a maximum output effect of -0.9 percent. Restricting lags to one year, as Christiano et al. do, cuts the output peak to -1.5 percent. Compared with the literature (Sims; Christiano et al.; Bernanke & Mihov) — output falling after three to six months, maxima of about 1.5 percent or slightly less than 1 percent, prices flat for about a year and a half then 1 to 2 percent lower — the new measure gives similar timing but a maximum output effect of 2 to 3 percent, and prices roughly 4 to 5 percent lower after four years.

**Conclusion.** A one-point shock starts reducing industrial production after five months with a maximum fall of 4.3 percent after two years; it has little effect on prices for almost two years, then lowers inflation by 2 to 3 percentage points, leaving the price level about 6 percent lower after four years. Qualitatively "very consistent with textbook views": output moves before inflation; the main uncertainty is the price lag, from within six months to 22 months across specifications.

## Limitations and caveats

- **Table 2 (the monthly shock series) is scrambled**: the year labels for 1969–1978 and the values are separated, many entries carry OCR noise ("~=—-0.0", "--0.115"), and rows cannot be matched to months reliably from the file. Do not read individual monthly shocks from it.
- **Equations (1)–(3) are garbled** into symbols (sums, subscripts and Greek letters lost); their content is described in words above from the surrounding text.
- **Figures 1–9** survive only as axis ticks and legend fragments; all responses quoted above come from the text. One number in the text is damaged: the rise in the price level after a funds-rate increase is printed as "about | percent", so it is not quoted.
- **Table A1** (intended rate by meeting) has corrupted dates in places ("TNTT3", "T/N/82", "TINT184"); values are legible.
- Footnote 1 is split across pages, and footnote markers are rendered as "?", "'" or "°".
- Authors' own caveats: residual anticipatory actions remain if the FOMC used information beyond the Greenbook (they argue any bias is small); excluded intermeeting moves and moves on erroneous data reduce precision; the early Volcker period is the most uncertain; VAR impulse responses mix the initial shock with forecastable later policy moves (Cochrane 1998); the price lag is the main uncertainty.
