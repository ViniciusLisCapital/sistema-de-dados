## Context and motivation

The paper asks how far emerging market economies (EMEs) can use their own monetary policy to moderate the domestic impact of global financial and monetary forces — especially US policy after the global financial crisis (GFC), when ultra-low advanced-economy rates and quantitative easing pushed a portfolio shift into EME assets. It takes "a middle ground" between Woodford (2010), who finds it hard to see globalization interfering with domestic control of inflation, and Rey (2013), who argues the trilemma is really a dilemma. Its new empirical contribution is to test interest rate independence for long-term as well as short-term rates.

## Core argument / thesis

The classical monetary trilemma "remains valid": EMEs that let the exchange rate move retain substantial short-term interest rate independence and are "far better positioned" than those that fix it. But exchange rate flexibility does not insulate an economy from foreign financial shocks, long-term rates are strongly correlated across countries whatever the regime, and financial globalization "worsens the trade-offs monetary policy faces in navigating among multiple domestic objectives". The core problem "is not ineffective monetary policy per se" but "a shortage of reliable policy instruments". That shortage is aggravated by a distinct **financial trilemma** (from Schoenmaker 2013): national responsibility for financial policy, international financial integration and financial stability cannot all hold, so national prudential policies lose effectiveness when capital markets are open, raising the burden on monetary policy.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Overview (2)**: lending cycles to emerging regions recur since the 19th century; gross inflows can surge even into current-account-surplus economies. Cross-border bank credit is 10–20% of domestic bank credit in four of five regions (Figure 1), covaries with the mid-2000s boom and bust, and has fallen secularly in Latin America and emerging Europe. Offsetting improvements: liabilities shifted from debt to equity (Figures 3–4), more domestic-currency debt, inflation targets with managed floats, macroprudential oversight, large reserves enabling a lender-of-last-resort role in foreign currency, and more capital account restrictions than in advanced economies. New vulnerabilities: offshore bond issuance by EME corporates outside residence-based data, volatile foreign-held local bond markets, currency mismatch via hedging counterparties.
- **Direct interest rate linkages (3.1)**: a credible peg imports US policy at all maturities. With flexibility, short rates are linked by interest parity with a currency risk premium (1): if the EME holds its rate when the US cuts, the currency appreciates sharply, overshooting its expected future value. Intervention to resist appreciation adds reserves, money and bank credit, and carry-trade dynamics can be reinforced if the central bank is seen as merely slowing an inevitable appreciation. Long rates obey a long-term parity relation (2)–(3) involving expected short rates, currency risk premia and term premia; if expected exchange rate moves fade over time, long rates can be more correlated than short rates, and correlated term premia (e.g. US QE lowering term premia abroad) spread US policy.
- **The financial cycle (3.2)**: a US-originated fall in risk aversion compresses term premia at home and abroad; with EME long rates unchanged, the exchange rate must absorb it — "a 10 basis point fall in the term premium on a US two-year bond would require a 20 basis point currency appreciation". Booms abroad relax borrowing constraints and raise collateral values; gross inflows are matched by gross outflows when the current account is slow to move, expanding gross positions and financial stability risks. Korean corporate spreads (Figure 6) fell from mid-2004 while the federal funds rate rose — the global boom, not US policy, dominated.
- **Foreign currency credit (3.3)**: dollar bank credit to non-banks outside the US rose from 23% of total US domestic bank credit (2000) to about 35%, so "more than a third of global dollar lending by banks to non-banks now takes place outside US borders" (Figure 7); offshore dollar bond issuance rose after the GFC. Unhedged carry-trade borrowers and global non-US banks intermediating dollars transmit US funding conditions to non-dollar balance sheets.
- **Empirical tests (4)**: (a) panel regressions (4) of quarterly changes in a country's 3-month and 10-year government rates on the base country's rate change, with lags of domestic real GDP growth and CPI inflation changes, a VIX percentage-change control or time effects, and an interaction with a de facto peg indicator (5); 22 advanced economies other than the US and 34 emerging/developing, from around 1990; "natural" base countries from Klein and Shambaugh (2013); pegs = ±2% band lasting at least eight consecutive quarters. (b) Country-by-country error-correction equations (6) estimated with the Pesaran-Shin-Smith bounds approach on monthly data, US base, giving a long-run levels coefficient ξ and an adjustment speed θ.
- **Trade-offs (5)**: monetary independence means the ability to pursue a range of domestic goals. With one target (inflation) and a float, Woodford's result holds; with multiple goals — output, the exchange rate's allocative and distributional effects ("Neither in theory nor in practice is there generally a 'divine coincidence' for the exchange rate"), financial stability — the single instrument must trade them off (Fischer 2010 on Tinbergen). Even in a closed economy, absent first-best macroprudential tools, optimal policy deviates from price stability to restrain debt build-ups; openness degrades macroprudential tools and so worsens the trade-off further, though independence remains "and more so the less tightly the exchange rate is managed".

## Main results / findings

- **Short rates, full sample (text's Table 1; printed as Table 3)**: with the US as universal base the coefficient is tiny and insignificant (0.0605); with matched bases it "better than triples (to 0.201)" but stays insignificant; time effects lower it (0.0121); the VIX control raises it to 0.241, with the VIX change itself significant at 10% (0.00252). Lagged domestic output and inflation changes enter with overwhelming significance — "compatible with substantial interest rate independence at the short end."
- **Long rates, full sample**: US-base coefficient 0.354 (significant at 1%), multi-base 0.552, with time effects 0.433, with the VIX 0.636; the VIX change is highly significant (0.00298); domestic macro variables usually insignificant; adjusted R² higher than for short rates (0.048 to 0.138 vs. 0.035 to 0.061). "The LR picture is one of much less interest rate independence."
- **Pegs (text's Table 2; printed as Table 4)**: for short rates the positive co-movement is "almost entirely due to being pegged" (non-peg multi-base coefficients −0.0256 to 0.0290, peg interactions 0.278 to 0.375), though the peg interactions are not significant even at 10%; for long rates non-pegs still show coefficients of 0.430 to 0.583 (significant at 1%), and a peg raises the correlation only insignificantly.
- **EMEs vs. advanced (text's Tables 3–4)**: EME non-pegs' short rates are less tied to base rates than advanced non-pegs'; for advanced economies dollar pegs matter more than pegs in general; much of advanced short-rate synchronization reflects common responses to global shocks; long-rate coherence is much greater for advanced economies; the VIX matters for long rates in both groups, "especially for non-advanced economies". Higher short-rate independence in EMEs could reflect capital controls, but per Klein and Shambaugh only "thoroughgoing and long-standing controls" seem effective.
- **Dynamics (text's Tables 5–6; printed as Tables 7–8)**: group averages of ξ are near 1 for short and long rates (short: 0.97 advanced, 1.43 emerging; long: 1.00 and 0.89), so rates "trend strongly with US rates in the long run". Long-rate half-lives average 17.54 months (advanced) and 14.64 (emerging); short-rate half-lives 25.44 and 13.12 — "about a year as opposed to over two years". Examples: Hong Kong unitary coherence and a short-rate half-life below four months; Singapore ξ only 0.39 with faster adjustment; Malaysia ξ 0.58 and a half-life of about a year and a half. Brazil's short rate (Mar 1995–Nov 2012) has ξ 1.39 and a half-life of 4.19 months, with a levels relationship significant under both stationarity assumptions; its long-rate equation (Jan 2000–Dec 2006, 84 observations) gives ξ −3.42 with a standard error of 8.78 and no half-life.
- **Policy (5–6)**: Basel III's jurisdictional reciprocity for countercyclical capital buffers raises the effectiveness of domestic macroprudential tools and "reduces the burden on monetary policy". Open questions: what drives long-rate correlation (expected short rates, term premia or currency risk premia); when capital controls help and which work; whether the central bank swap-line network, made permanent among six advanced-country central banks in October 2013, could be extended. Upside of globalization: the shift from foreign debt to equity is "a stabilising effect" that can make monetary policy more effective, so discouraging debt finance can raise welfare.
- **Issing's comment**: the financial trilemma is "de facto, a dilemma" between integration and stability, though integration may sometimes foster stability; monetary policy coordination is "unrealistic" and possibly undesirable; central banks should "consider – internalise is going too far" spillovers; inflation targeting "as it was practised is not enough" — monetary and credit developments must not be ignored; coordination of macroprudential rules is "indispensable to avoid regulatory arbitrage".
- **Ito's comment**: illustrates the old trilemma with China, Thailand and Hong Kong, and Thailand's 1994–96 attempt at all three as a crisis; finds the financial trilemma's cases harder to exemplify (Asian EMEs with capital controls keep national financial policy and stability). Most EME crises followed US rate rises (Latin America in the 1980s, Mexico 1994); QE spillovers "may have been overemphasised". Recommended response to inflow surges: some appreciation, some intervention, a Chilean-type withholding tax on short-term inflows and loan-to-value limits, since monetary policy alone cannot manage both overheating and inflows. Under the "separation hypothesis" (effective macroprudential policy lets monetary policy pursue flexible inflation targeting) there is no trade-off. Asian managed floats with reserves accumulated in 2000–2007 weathered 2008–09 outflows (except Korea briefly in 2009).

## Limitations and caveats

- **Equations (1)–(6) are garbled**: variables print as private-use glyphs (e.g. "݅௧"), so only the text's description of each equation is usable; the text also refers to "specification (3)" for the Table 1 regression and the PSS null, where the regression is (4) and the dynamic equation (6). Not restored here.
- **Table numbering is offset**: the data-appendix coverage tables print as "Table 1" and "Table 2", so the regression tables the text calls Tables 1–6 print as Tables 3–8. Results above are cited with the text's numbering and the printed one where useful.
- **Lost labels**: in the peg-interaction tables the rows for non-peg coefficients have no labels; in the dynamic tables the ξ and θ column headers and the legend symbols are lost. The values cited were assigned using the text's descriptions (Hong Kong, Singapore, Malaysia, group averages) and the remaining labelled rows; per-country values for other countries are not cited beyond Brazil.
- **Figures 1–7** survive only as titles, sources and axis labels; their values are known only from the text.
- The evidence is reduced-form: the author stresses that unobserved global shocks "bedevil" the interpretation of interest rate co-movement, and that long-rate results for advanced countries rest on longer, thicker series and should be read cautiously.
