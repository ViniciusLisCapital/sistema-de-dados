## Context and motivation

Chapter 4 showed that when staggered pricing is the only distortion, fully stabilizing the price level is feasible and optimal — "strict inflation targeting" — because the central bank faces no meaningful tradeoff. The chapter calls that environment "useful from a pedagogical point of view, but ... not realistic": central banks see themselves facing short-run tradeoffs, and even self-declared inflation targeters pursue "flexible inflation targeting" (footnote 1: a term coined by Lars Svensson), partially accommodating inflationary pressures while committed to a medium-term target. The chapter studies optimal policy in three environments with a nontrivial tradeoff — a time-varying gap between natural and efficient output with an efficient steady state, an inefficient (distorted) steady state, and a zero lower bound on the nominal rate — each solved under discretion and under commitment. It "follows closely Clarida, Galí, and Gertler (1999)".

## Core argument / thesis

Once the natural and efficient levels of output can diverge, zero inflation and an efficient level of activity cannot be attained simultaneously, and how much the central bank can achieve depends on whether it can commit. Under discretion the bank reoptimizes every period; under commitment it chooses a state-contingent plan that "generally involves actions that are suboptimal ex-post, but whose anticipation leads to an improvement of the policy tradeoffs (and higher welfare)". Because inflation is forward-looking, credible promises about future output gaps and the future price level move expectations today. Commitment therefore (i) dampens the impact of cost-push shocks at the cost of persistent later deviations, removing the *stabilization bias* of discretion; (ii) avoids, at least asymptotically, the *inflation bias* that arises under discretion when the steady state is inefficient; and (iii) at the zero lower bound, substantially reduces losses by promising to keep rates low after the adverse shock is gone — a theoretical underpinning for forward guidance.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Loss function and tradeoff (5.2)**: a second-order approximation to household welfare (appendix 5.1) makes losses proportional to the discounted sum of squared inflation plus ϑ times the squared welfare-relevant output gap xt (output minus efficient output), (1), with ϑ = κ/ϵ. Substituting into the NKPC gives `πt = βEt{πt+1} + κxt + ut` (2), where the *cost-push shock* ut is proportional to the gap between efficient and natural output, exogenous to policy, AR(1) with persistence ρu (3). Appendix 5.2 derives it from exogenous variations in desired price markups or in wage markups.
- **Discretion (5.2.1)**: taking expectations as given, the bank minimizes period losses subject to (2); the optimality condition (4), `xt = −(κ/ϑ)πt`, is a "leaning against the wind" rule — a negative output gap in the face of inflation — and can be read as a "targeting rule". Inflation (5) and the output gap (6) are proportional to the current cost-push shock. The implied nominal rate (8) — the *efficient* interest rate plus a term in ut — does not guarantee a unique equilibrium (one eigenvalue of the system matrix outside the unit circle, as in chapter 4); adding a response to inflation's deviation from its desired path with φπ > 1 (10) does, "in order to satisfy the Taylor principle". Rule (10) needs the model's parameters and real-time observation of the cost-push shock and the efficient rate; a targeting rule like (4) needs real-time efficient output — both limit practical appeal.
- **Commitment (5.2.2)**: the Lagrangian solution gives `x0 = −(κ/ϑ)π0` (11) and a condition in first differences thereafter (12), jointly the "levels" targeting rule (13): the output gap is set in proportion to the deviation of the price level from an implicit target, the price level one period before the plan is chosen. The price level follows a stationary process (14)–(15) with root δ ∈ (0, 1) and the output gap follows (16); commitment produces "endogenous or intrinsic persistence". By iterating (2) forward, inflation depends on current and expected future output gaps, so promising lower future gaps lets the bank achieve a given current inflation with a smaller current output decline. For ρu = 0 an interest rate rule responding to the price level's deviation from its prescribed path (with φp > 0) implements the plan as the unique equilibrium.
- **Distorted steady state (5.3)**: a steady-state wedge between the marginal rate of substitution and the marginal product of labor — for example uncorrected market power, where it equals one minus the inverse gross markup — adds a linear term in the output gap to the loss (17) under a "small distortion" assumption that keeps the problem linear-quadratic. Under discretion the targeting rule (19) is more expansionary for any inflation, and steady-state inflation is positive and increasing in the distortion — "the classical inflation bias phenomenon"; responses to cost-push shocks are unchanged. Under commitment (21)–(27) there is a deterministic transition with output and inflation above steady state that dies out asymptotically: the price level converges to a constant, so long-run inflation and the average output gap (relative to natural output) are zero, and the inflation bias is avoided "at least asymptotically". Shock responses are again unaffected by the distortion.
- **Zero lower bound (5.4)**: with no cost-push shocks and an efficient steady state, the natural rate falls unexpectedly from its steady-state value ρ to a negative value in period 0 and stays there through period tZ, after which it returns to ρ, with the path known from then on. Because supporting the efficient outcome needs `it = rn t`, the bound (28) rules out the first best while it binds. Under discretion (5.4.1) the rate is at zero through tZ, the output gap and inflation are negative, and both return to zero once the bound stops binding. Under commitment (5.4.2) the rate stays at zero through a period tC ≥ tZ, found by an iterative procedure on a system of 4(tc +2) equations, and inflation and the output gap are positive after liftoff, converging to zero (42)–(43).

## Main results / findings

- **Cost-push shock, discretion vs. commitment (Figures 5.1, 5.2)**, one percent shock, baseline calibration of chapter 3, ρu = 0 and ρu = 0.8: under discretion the bank partly accommodates the shock — inflation rises, but by less than with an unchanged output gap — and the price level shifts permanently, more so the more persistent the shock. With a transitory shock, under discretion the gap and inflation return to zero one period after the shock; under commitment they stay negative well beyond the shock, but the impact effect on inflation is smaller and the initial output gap loss smaller. With ρu = 0.8 commitment again attains lower inflation and a smaller output gap at impact, and the price level returns to its original level, more slowly. The discretionary tendency to stabilize the output gap in the medium term more than optimal is "the stabilization bias"; footnote 5 distinguishes it from the inflation bias and notes it arises "independent of the degree of inefficiency of the steady state".
- **Inflation bias (5.3.1)**: steady-state inflation under discretion is positive when natural output is inefficiently low, with a permanent increase of output above its natural level; under commitment the economy is "(asymptotically) observationally equivalent" to one with an efficient steady state.
- **Zero lower bound (Figure 5.3)**: the natural rate drops "from 1 percent to −1 percent (4 to −4 percent in annualized rates)" for 6 quarters (t = 0 to tz = 5). Under discretion the output gap and inflation "experience a large decline on impact and remain below their optimal values until the negative shock vanishes". Under commitment the deviations "are now much smaller, and so are the resulting welfare losses": the nominal rate stays at zero for two additional periods after the natural rate is back to normal and below the natural rate for a third, and the anticipation of that looser policy offsets the excessive tightness of the binding bound. The text reads this as "the theoretical underpinning to the so-called 'forward guidance' strategy adopted by the Fed and the ECB" after the 2008–2009 crisis.
- **Literature (5.5)**: the original inflation-bias and gains-from-commitment analysis is in Kydland and Prescott (1980) and Barro and Gordon (1983). Woodford (2003b) adds interest rate volatility as a source of losses when real balances yield utility, so the optimal policy smooths the nominal rate. Large steady-state distortions are beyond the book; three approaches are listed (Schmitt-Grohé and Uribe 2004; Benigno and Woodford 2005; Khan, King, and Wolman 2003). Benhabib, Schmitt-Grohé, and Uribe show that a Taylor rule with a ZLB implies two steady states, one a "liquidity trap"; Nakata and Schmidt (2014) study delegation to a "conservative" central banker with an occasionally binding ZLB.

## Limitations and caveats

- **Equations are damaged throughout**: summation signs, fraction bars, hats and tildes are lost and several symbols are missing altogether. Most visibly, the symbol for the steady-state distortion (5.3) is blank everywhere it appears (the definition reads "measured by a parameter  representing the wedge"), as is the weight on the linear loss term in (17); the price-level deviation and the gap in deviation from steady state print without their accents, so (13)–(16), (19)–(27) and the ZLB system (34), (40)–(50) and matrix M read as fragments. The interest rate expressions (8), (10) and the commitment rule for ρu = 0 have their coefficient symbols missing (the latter prints "αx" where the weight is elsewhere ϑ). They are described in words here, not restored.
- **Figures 5.1–5.3** survive only as their captions and panel labels; no magnitude can be read off them (the panel label "Cost-push stock" is the extraction's). The impulse-response statements above are the text's descriptions.
- **Section numbering is inconsistent**: the overview paragraph refers to sections 5.1 (efficient steady state), 5.2 (distorted) and 5.3 (ZLB), while the headings in the file are 5.2, 5.3 and 5.4 (with 5.1 repeating the chapter title). This note uses the headings.
- The analysis is linear-quadratic and limited to "small" steady-state distortions; the ZLB exercise is a single, unanticipated, deterministic shock "not meant to provide a comprehensive treatment" of the bound; ϑ can be read as the central bank's own weight rather than the welfare-based κ/ϵ.
- Exercises 5.1–5.5 (optimized Taylor rule, optimal Markovian policy, transaction frictions, inflation persistence with indexation, optimal steady-state inflation with the ZLB) are posed but not solved.
