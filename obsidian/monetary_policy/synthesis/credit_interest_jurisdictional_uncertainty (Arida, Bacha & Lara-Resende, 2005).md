## Context and motivation

Brazilian real interest rates stayed "extraordinarily high" after the 1994 Real Plan, and remained exceptionally high relative to other emerging markets even after 1999, when the floating rate, systematic primary surpluses and trade surpluses put macroeconomic policy in order. The usual explanations are a "bad equilibrium" (fiscal dominance in an open economy), "insufficient fiscal adjustment", and a "sequence of negative shocks" plus a central bank still building its reputation. The authors accept that these are not mutually exclusive but argue they miss a resilient, policy-related distortion, and link high short-term rates to a fact the debate had treated separately: Brazil has no local long-term credit or bond market. The chapter offers "conjectures": a concept, an account of the policies it provoked, and a small model of how undoing them would lower the interest rate.

## Core argument / thesis

The missing long-term domestic credit market and the high short-term interest rate have a common cause, **jurisdictional uncertainty**: a diffuse, unquantifiable uncertainty about the settlement of contracts under Brazilian jurisdiction, which shows up as an anti-saver and anti-creditor bias across the executive, the legislature and the judiciary. Because the uncertainty cannot be priced beyond a short horizon, long-term financial saving under Brazilian jurisdiction disappears. Policymakers misread the resulting lack of long-term saving as a market failure and responded with interventions — convertibility restrictions, artificial lengthening of public debt, compulsory saving funds, and forced saving through inflation, replaced after 1994 by "incomeless" turnover taxes — that aggravate the uncertainty and raise the interest rate needed to hold inflation on target. Unwinding them, gradually and credibly, would lower the short-term rate.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **The diagnosis by elimination (Table 8.1).** Long-term credit to Brazilian borrowers exists only offshore and in dollars; there is none onshore in reais or dollars, and offshore real-denominated credit is short-term and synthetic (settled in dollars, mirroring domestic instruments). The same creditors lend long offshore and not onshore, and even the Treasury, exempt from the restrictions on dollar indexation, does not issue long dollar-linked debt at home. So the divide is the jurisdiction, not the currency or the creditor's location.
- **What it is not.** Not just fear for the purchasing power of the currency (otherwise price-indexed long instruments would abound); not frontier/transfer risk or credit risk, which markets price across maturities (Brazil's long offshore bonds; country risk is the market's estimate of long-term credit risk offshore); not the general cost of doing business, since foreign firms make long-term direct investment but will not lend long locally — the bias is anti-creditor, not anti-business. The authors read it as what gives substance to the "original sin".
- **Where it comes from.** A history of losses on long-term financial contracts — manipulated indexation, changes of monetary standard, the Collor asset freeze, judicial annulment of foreign-currency adjustment clauses, delays in paying judgments against the government — worsened after the 1988 Constitution subordinated property to its "social function". It decomposes into the risk of acts of the Prince changing contract values and the risk of unfavorable court interpretation.
- **How it shapes the term structure.** Above some maximum term T the domestic market vanishes and the uncertainty cannot be expressed as an add-on to external rates; below T there is near-perfect arbitrage with dollar rates, so the uncertainty is embedded in the short-term spread over US Treasuries that onshore investors require. Long-term instruments are illiquid (only the original creditor can price them), the term structure is truncated, and markets cannot reveal long-term inflation expectations.
- **Effects on saving and investment.** Higher required short rates (absent convertibility), lower overall saving, saving kept short and liquid or moved offshore, preference for direct real investment; small firms limited to retained profits, large firms exposed to currency mismatch, cash-rich firms overinvesting in their own businesses; collateral is hard to repossess because law and jurisprudence favor the debtor.
- **Why Brazil stands out (Table 8.3).** Rates and long credit depend on jurisdiction quality and currency convertibility: strong jurisdiction gives low short rates (with long domestic-currency credit at home, and abroad too if convertible and large); weak jurisdiction with convertibility gives dollarization with moderate rates; weak jurisdiction without convertibility gives high short rates and no long credit — Brazil, unique in having built a large de-dollarized, captive short-term market for its public debt.
- **How the interventions raise rates.** Convertibility restrictions keep a risk of blocked remittances in the price and, under a float, signal that the authorities think no market premium could compensate for the jurisdiction; artificial lengthening relies on banks carrying long bonds with daily funding, so the central bank implicitly insures them and repurchases debt at unfavorable prices when rate bets fail; compulsory saving funds turned into a payroll tax wedge pushing workers into the lower-productivity informal sector; turnover taxes split industry into tax-evading small firms and large oligopolies, steepening supply. "Fiscal populism" — no tolerance for deficits or inflation deviations, but rising spending financed by distorting taxes — leaves the macro picture sound while natural output shrinks.
- **The model (section 8.8).** Strict inflation targeting with full credibility (both assumptions acknowledged as unrealistic). Equation (1), domestic balance: the central bank sets the nominal rate given the equilibrium real rate, the current and expected output gaps and depreciation — anything that lowers natural output raises the equilibrium rate; the DD curve slopes up in interest rate–exchange rate space. Equation (2), external balance with constant reserves: capital flows depend on the dollar return on real assets net of a risk factor p (credit, contractual and frontier risks), against net exports; EE slopes down. Unlike Blanchard, p is exogenous; if p rose with the interest rate, EE could slope up. Expectations are taken as exogenous because the truncated market makes rational expectations "artificial".

## Main results / findings

The chapter has no estimation; its evidence is institutional and descriptive, and its results are the model's comparative statics.
- **Evidence for the anti-creditor bias (Table 8.2)**: in an elite survey (Lamounier and Souza 2002), only 48 percent of respondents held that contracts must be enforced regardless of social effects; among the judiciary only 7 percent, and 61 percent said social justice justifies decisions in breach of contracts.
- **Evidence of truncated maturity**: the domestic market's longest maturity was seldom over one year and shrank to near overnight in stress; the domestic public debt's average tenor of around two and half years is obtained only through overnight indexation and captive demand, against an average of 12 years for external debt. As of January 2004 commercial banks held 46 percent of domestic federal debt and their clients' funds with daily liquidity another 44 percent, so 90 percent of it had its maturity "artificially lengthened" by the banking system.
- **Comparative statics of a credible reform program**: convertibility lowers p (EE shifts left); abandoning artificial lengthening lowers bailout-related costs (lower p); replacing turnover taxes flattens DD; balanced cuts in spending and taxes, and ending forced saving, raise natural output (DD shifts right); the signaling effect lowers the equilibrium rate. Net result: unwinding the distortions lowers the short-term rate needed to keep inflation on target, while the exchange rate effect is ambiguous — removing "financial" distortions appreciates it, removing "real" ones depreciates it.
- **What could contradict it**: the authors list how unwinding could backfire — convertibility read as a brief window to escape the jurisdiction, shorter debt read as vulnerability and monetization risk, the end of forced saving cutting long-term funding if savers stay reluctant, a switch from turnover to income taxes raising revenue doubts. All raise the expected exchange rate or the expected output gap and push the rate up instead.

## Limitations and caveats

- **Authors' caveats**: the argument is a conjecture; strict targeting and full credibility are simplifications (the BCB smoothed rates and accommodated the 2001 shocks; the lack of formal independence proved costly in late 2002); expectations are exogenous; p is exogenous; risk aversion is left out; the results depend on the reform being perceived as coherent and sustainable, and a "big bang" could be dangerous. A step-by-step announced program is suggested, but policy design is outside the chapter's scope.
- **What the text shows**: jurisdictional uncertainty is defined as unquantifiable, so it is identified by elimination and by institutional evidence (one elite survey, debt-structure facts), not measured. The double-equilibrium (fiscal dominance) model is called internally consistent with empirical relevance "not yet" established. A later IMF study by Segura-Ubiergo rejects the hypothesis on cross-country grounds (see Connections).

Equations (1) and (2) are reduced to stray symbols and are described above only from the prose; Figure 8.1 survives as axis and curve labels (DD, EE).
