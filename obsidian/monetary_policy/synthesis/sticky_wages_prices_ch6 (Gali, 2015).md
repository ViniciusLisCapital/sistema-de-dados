## Context and motivation

The earlier chapters treat the labor market as perfectly competitive, with households and firms taking the wage as given. This chapter gives workers (or unions representing them) monopoly power over the wage of their differentiated labor type and subjects them to Calvo-type constraints on how often they can reset nominal wages, following Erceg, Henderson, and Levin (2000), "the framework of reference in the literature". The aim is to see what nominal wage rigidity implies for monetary policy: how the economy responds to shocks, what the welfare criterion becomes, what the optimal policy looks like, and which simple rules approximate it.

## Core argument / thesis

"A key result emerges from the analysis of the model with sticky wages and prices: fully stabilizing price inflation is no longer optimal." With both rigidities, fluctuations in price inflation, wage inflation and the output gap all cause inefficiencies, and the flexible-price/flexible-wage allocation cannot in general be attained, because zero price and wage inflation imply a constant real wage while the natural real wage moves with technology. Optimal policy balances three objectives. In a special case it reduces to fully stabilizing a weighted average of price and wage inflation ("composite inflation"), with more weight on the stickier of the two — a form of divine coincidence — and numerically a rule that stabilizes composite inflation does about as well as the optimal policy, while strict price inflation targeting does worst. The text notes this "appears at odds with the practice of most central banks, which seem to attach little weight to wage inflation as a target variable".

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Firms (6.1.1)**: a continuum of firms with technology (1) using a CES bundle of a continuum of labor types (2), with ϵw the elasticity of substitution among labor varieties. Price setting is Calvo as in chapter 3 (reset probability 1 − θp), giving the price inflation equation (6) in terms of the deviation of the average price markup from its desired level.
- **Households and wage setting (6.1.2)**: income is pooled within the household; each labor type's wage is reset with probability 1 − θw per period, independently of the time since the last change. The reset wage (12) is a markup over a weighted average of expected future marginal rates of substitution plus prices — rising in expected prices "because households care about the purchasing power of their nominal wage". With full wage flexibility, Mw (= ϵw/(ϵw−1)) is the desired gross wage markup between the real wage and the marginal rate of substitution.
- **Wage inflation equation (15)**: wage inflation depends on its expected value and negatively on the average wage markup gap, with slope λw decreasing in θw, ϵw and ϕ — "analogous to (6)". It replaces the condition that the real wage equals the marginal rate of substitution: with sticky wages the real wage does not move one-for-one with the marginal rate of substitution.
- **Equilibrium (6.2)**: price inflation (18) and wage inflation (20) are written in terms of the output gap and the *real wage gap* (the real wage minus the natural real wage (16)); an identity (21) links the change in the wage gap to wage inflation, price inflation and the change in the natural wage; the dynamic IS equation (22) uses a natural rate now defined with flexible wages and prices. An interest rate rule (23) responds to price inflation, wage inflation and the output gap.
- **No first-best**: the system (24) "in general ... does not have a solution" with zero output gap, price and wage inflation, even if the rule tracks the natural rate one-for-one; that outcome is an equilibrium only if the natural wage is constant, which fails with technology shocks.
- **Determinacy**: with non-negative coefficients the equilibrium is unique if a condition on φp, φw and φy holds; with φy = 0 it reduces to `φp + φw > 1`, i.e. the nominal rate must respond more than one-for-one to "any arbitrary weighted average of price and wage inflation" — an extension of the Taylor principle. A larger φy lowers the required φp + φw (Figure 6.1).
- **Welfare (6.3, appendix 6.1)**: with an employment subsidy making the flexible-price/flexible-wage allocation efficient, losses (28)–(29) are a weighted sum of the variances of the output gap, price inflation and wage inflation. The output gap and price inflation weights are those of chapter 4; the wage inflation weight rises with ϵw, with the output elasticity of labor 1−α, and with wage stickiness θw, because wage dispersion lowers aggregate productivity.
- **Limiting cases**: with flexible wages the model collapses to the chapter 3 NKPC and optimal policy sets price inflation and the output gap to zero; with flexible prices and sticky wages optimal policy sets wage inflation and the output gap to zero. Hence strict price inflation targeting is suboptimal except under full wage flexibility, and strict wage inflation targeting except under full price flexibility.
- **Optimal policy under commitment (6.4)**: first-order conditions (30)–(33). With demand shocks only, the optimum fully stabilizes the output and wage gaps and both inflations, and can be implemented by `it = rn t + φpπp t` with φp > 1. With technology shocks the optimum trades off the real wage adjustment against nominal stability.
- **Special case (6.4.3)**: if κp = κw and ϵp = ϵw(1 −α), optimality reduces to (34)–(35) in composite inflation, the weighted average of price and wage inflation with weights λw/(λp + λw) on price and λp/(λp + λw) on wage inflation. Composite inflation obeys an NKPC in the output gap (36) for any parameters, so it and the output gap can both be zero; in the special case that is also optimal. Implementation does not need the output gap to be observed — "an instance in which the divine coincidence result introduced in chapter 4 obtains".

## Main results / findings

- **Monetary policy shock (Figure 6.2)**: chapter 3 calibration with rule (23) at φp = 1.5 and φy = 0.125, a 0.25 percentage point increase in the rule's shifter (one percentage point annualized absent the endogenous response), ρv = 0.5, ϵw = 4.5 ("consistent with an average unemployment rate of 5 percent", justified "in the next chapter"). Three cases: θp = θw = 3/4 (wage spells of four quarters, said to accord with the evidence), sticky prices and flexible wages (θw = 0), flexible prices and sticky wages (θp = 0). With both rigidities wage inflation and the real wage respond sluggishly, the price inflation response is limited, the policy rate stays persistently higher and output falls more. With flexible wages the real wage falls by an "(implausibly) large" amount; with flexible prices price inflation drops sharply. The estimates in the literature (Christiano, Eichenbaum, and Evans 2005 cited) are "more in line with the predictions of the model with both sticky prices and wages".
- **Technology shock under the optimal policy (Figure 6.3)**: with flexible wages the optimum keeps prices stable and the real wage adjusts through wage inflation; with flexible prices it keeps wages stable and adjusts through price deflation; with both sticky, the real wage rises less than the natural wage, smoothly, "through a mix of negative price inflation and positive wage inflation", and the output gap deviations are "quantitatively very small".
- **Table 6.1, technology shocks** (AR(1), ρa = 0.9, innovation standard deviation 0.01). Columns: optimal; strict price, wage and composite targeting; flexible price, wage and composite targeting (`it = 0.01 + 1.5πt`). Welfare losses L: 0.0330 (optimal), 0.78, 0.039, 0.0337 (strict), 0.47, 0.305, 0.307 (flexible). The optimal policy keeps the output gap nearly constant (σ(y) = 0.04) with wage inflation volatility about a third of price inflation's (0.03 vs. 0.11); its losses are "about 1/30 of a percent of steady state consumption". Strict composite targeting does as well as the optimum "for practical purposes"; strict wage targeting is second, since wage inflation has a weight of 0.85 in composite inflation under the baseline calibration; strict price targeting is worst (σ(y) = 3.38). The flexible price rule improves on strict price targeting; flexible wage and composite rules do worse than their strict counterparts.
- **Table 6.1, demand shocks** (AR(1) coefficient 0.5, innovation standard deviation 0.01): the optimal policy and all three strict targeting rules give zero losses — "a form of divine coincidence" — while the flexible rules leave output gap volatility (losses 0.061, 0.067, 0.066).
- **Conclusion (6.5)**: with sticky wages, policies focused only on price inflation "are clearly suboptimal, especially in the presence of technology shocks"; a policy responding aggressively to a suitable weighted average of price and wage inflation, with "a slightly dominant weight to wage inflation", performs as well as the optimal policy, though "choosing the appropriate weights remains a challenge".

## Limitations and caveats

- **Equations are damaged throughout**: integral and summation signs, hats, tildes and Δ are lost, so the output gap prints as "yt ≡yt −yn t", the change in the natural wage in (21) and in the shock vector after (24) prints without its Δ, and the wage and price dispersion terms after 6.2 lose their symbols. The aggregation identities, the matrices of (24) and of appendix 6.2, the welfare derivation of appendix 6.1 (where the steady-state distortion symbol is blank, as in chapter 5) and the loss (29) (whose leading fraction prints as "L =1") are fragments; the uniqueness condition after (24) prints partly intact. They are described in words here, not restored.
- **Figures 6.1–6.3** survive only as captions, panel labels and axis ticks (Figure 6.3 carries a stray "–0.05"); the impulse responses above are the text's descriptions.
- **Table 6.1** is extracted one value per line; the values quoted follow the extraction's order (seven columns per row), which matches the text's statements (each strict rule zeroes its own target; composite targeting zeroes the output gap). The output and output-gap symbol has lost its tilde ("σ(y)").
- κp and κw are defined twice with different expressions: at (18) and (20), and again in the limiting cases of 6.3 (with α added to ϕ inside the bracket). Flagged as printed, not reconciled.
- The welfare analysis assumes an efficient steady state (optimal subsidy) and full commitment; the medium-scale models cited in 6.6 add wage indexation, which this model does not have. Exercises 6.1–6.4 are posed but not solved.
