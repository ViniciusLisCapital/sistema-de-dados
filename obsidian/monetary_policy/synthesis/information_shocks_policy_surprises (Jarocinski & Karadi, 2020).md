## Context and motivation

High-frequency surprises around central bank announcements are now a standard way to isolate unexpected policy (Gertler & Karadi 2015; Nakamura & Steinsson). But an announcement conveys both news about policy and the bank's assessment of the outlook. Example: on January 22, 2008 the FOMC cut by a larger-than-expected 75 basis points, and the S&P 500 *fell* within 30 minutes — the statement cited "a weakening of the economic outlook and increasing downside risks to growth". Such a positive co-movement of rates and stocks accompanies "around one third of FOMC announcements since 1990". If surprises in the bank's assessment ("central bank information shocks") have macroeconomic effects, communication matters and ignoring them biases measured monetary non-neutrality.

## Core argument / thesis

Standard theory says a pure policy tightening lowers stock prices — the discount rate rises and expected dividends fall. So a **negative** high-frequency co-movement of interest rates and stocks identifies a **monetary policy shock**, and the orthogonal **positive** co-movement identifies a **central bank information shock**. The two shocks have "intuitive and very different effects": a policy tightening contracts output, lowers prices and tightens financial conditions; an information shock raises rates persistently *and* raises prices and activity and eases financial conditions — consistent with the bank revealing good news about demand and tightening to partly offset it. This rules out the ineffectiveness of central bank communication. Purging information shocks gives a faster price-level decline and a stronger financial-conditions response, which in a New Keynesian model with financial frictions means moderate nominal rigidity and a "prominent role" for financial frictions. For reading an announcement, "it is important to look at the response of the stock prices in addition to the response of interest rates."

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Surprises (section 2)**: 239 FOMC announcements, 1990 to 2016 (an update of the Gürkaynak, Sack & Swanson 2005 dataset), measured from 10 minutes before to 20 minutes after the announcement. Interest-rate surprise: the 3-month fed funds future (it combines rate-setting and near-term forward guidance and ignores "timing surprises"); stock surprise: the S&P 500. The narrow window avoids the pre-FOMC drift (Lucca & Moench: 49 basis points on average in the prior 24 hours, 1994–2011); the average 30-minute return in the sample is less than 2 basis points with standard deviation of 60.
- **VAR with surprises (section 3.1, equation 1)**: monthly Bayesian VAR in which the surprises mt are i.i.d. (they do not depend on lags) and the macro block yt does. Minnesota-type independent normal–inverted Wishart prior (λ1 = 0.2, λ3 = 1); Gibbs sampler that also draws missing surprises (Appendix A).
- **Identification (section 3.2, Table 1)**: (1) high-frequency — the surprises are moved only by the two announcement shocks; (2) sign restrictions — monetary policy shock: rate up, stocks down; central bank information shock: both up. No restrictions on the monthly variables. A block-Choleski structure with a uniformly drawn rotation of the first two shocks (Rubio-Ramírez, Waggoner & Zha); set identification, robust bounds following Giacomini & Kitagawa.
- **Baseline US VAR (section 4.1)**: seven variables — the two surprises; the 1-year Treasury yield (valid at the zero lower bound because it embeds forward guidance), the S&P 500, real GDP and the GDP deflator interpolated to monthly (Stock & Watson), and the excess bond premium (Gilchrist & Zakrajšek). 12 lags, July 1979 to December 2016; surprises available from February 1990.
- **Benchmarks and refinements**: "standard HFI" orders the fed funds surprise first in a Choleski VAR (equation 4, as in Gertler & Karadi 2015); "poor man's sign restrictions" assign each month's fed funds surprise wholly to policy if stocks moved the other way, otherwise to information; a third shock, "central bank information about supply" (rate up, stocks down, 2-year breakeven inflation up), separates bad news with a hike from a policy shock (Table 3).
- **Euro area (section 5)**: a new dataset of 284 ECB events, 1999 to 2016, with 3-month Eonia OIS and Euro Stoxx 50 surprises around the 13:45 press release and the 14:30 press conference (summed), built from cleaned bid/ask quotes (Appendix D). VAR with the German 1-year yield and the BBB spread, January 1998 to December 2016.
- **Structural model (section 6)**: a version of Gertler & Karadi (2011) — Taylor rule (5) with an AR(1) policy shock; a Phillips curve with Calvo parameter γ and indexation γP (8); habits; investment adjustment costs; banks with an endogenous leverage constraint (17) and net worth accumulation (18); households lending directly subject to a portfolio adjustment cost κ (19), so κ → 0 removes financial frictions. **Communication**: the bank learns of a shock two quarters ahead and reveals it exactly and credibly (6). γ, γP, κ and the policy shock's size and persistence are estimated by impulse-response matching on 5 variables over 12 quarters (Christiano, Eichenbaum & Evans 2005 weighting).

## Main results / findings

**The stylized fact (Figure 1).** Of the FOMC surprises with both components non-zero, 34% have a "wrong-signed" positive co-movement (quadrants I and III hold 17 and 41 points, II and IV 65 and 50; 66 lie on a border), spread over the whole sample; alternative surprise measures (a policy-indicator factor, a stock-index factor) give 33% (Appendix B).

**Impact on surprises (Table 2, basis points).** Monetary policy shock: fed funds futures +5 (3 to 6), S&P 500 -44 (-54 to -23). Information shock: +3 (0 to 5) and +28 (4 to 47). Standard HFI: +6 and -21.

**Responses (Figure 2).** *Monetary policy shock*: the 1-year yield rises around 10 basis points and reverts within about a year; stocks drop about 1 percent; the excess bond premium rises about 5 basis points; real GDP and the price level decline persistently by about 10 and 8 basis points. The novelty is the "fairly low persistence of the interest rate response and the vigorous price-level decline". *Information shock*: the 1-year yield rises about 20 basis points and reverts only in about two years; the excess bond premium falls about 3 basis points; the price level rises about 5 basis points and returns to baseline only after around 3 years; output rises slightly, reversing after about a year. If stock moves were just noise, the two columns would look the same; they do not.

**Bias in standard HFI.** Mixing the two shocks mutes the price response, underestimates the rise in the excess bond premium, and makes the interest-rate response larger and more persistent — with a similar output peak, so non-neutrality is underestimated: "a picture with very rigid prices and a smaller role for financial frictions". The authors hypothesize that this bias may explain the price puzzle in some subsamples; with industrial production and the CPI, standard HFI gives a small price puzzle that the sign restrictions remove (Appendix B.3).

**Robustness (section 4.3, Appendix B).** Poor man's proxies give "strikingly similar" responses (shock correlations 88% for policy, 54% for information). Similar results ending in December 2008 (before the zero lower bound), dropping pre-1994 surprises, using industrial production and the CPI (except that industrial production does not rise after an information shock), using factor surprises, unrestricted surprise dynamics, and Giacomini–Kitagawa bounds (wider, but the shocks remain clearly different).

**Over time (section 4.4, Figure 4).** The largest information shock is January 22, 2008. A run of negative information shocks from end-2000 to end-2002, as the FOMC cut from over 6% to close to 1% while statements stressed weak demand and downside risks. Before 1994, when no statement was issued, the two shocks are roughly proportional — as information models predict when markets observe only the rate.

**Other variables (section 4.5, Figure 5).** The information shock raises Consensus expectations of both growth and inflation (gradually) and the five-year breakeven rate — information about demand. Controlling for it removes the positive impact of a policy tightening on expected growth noted by Nakamura & Steinsson. After a policy shock, breakevens peak only after a couple of months, so contemporaneous breakeven responses understate the inflation-expectations response; the authors read the dynamics as moderate, not high, nominal stickiness. The policy shock temporarily raises the term premium (the 5-year forward 5 years ahead rises); the information shock does not.

**Information about supply (section 4.6, Table 4).** Impact: policy shock S&P 500 -21 and breakeven -4; information about demand +27 and +2; information about supply -34 and +2. The supply-information shock produces near-zero responses, and the other two are robust — breakevens add "only minimal independent information".

**Euro area (section 5, Figures 7–9).** 46% of ECB surprises are "wrong-signed" (I 57, II 64, III 69, IV 83), against 32% in the US sample starting in 1999 — consistent with the ECB's more transparent communication (press conferences since 1999, prompt staff forecasts). Standard HFI gives a puzzle: after a "tightening" stocks rise and corporate spreads fall. With sign restrictions (plus the 1-year yield rising at least 1 basis point on impact), a tightening lowers output and prices with an insignificant spread response; an information shock raises stocks strongly, output more and prices less than in the US, and is met by tightening. The non-technical summary puts information shocks at around 50 percent of high-frequency variation in the euro area against 33 percent in the US. Notable information shocks: August 4, 2011 (no change after two hikes, stocks fell on "particularly high" uncertainty), July 2012 (a 25 basis point cut, stocks down more than 2 percent), September 2001 and October 1999.

**Model (section 6, Table 5, Figure 10).** Matching the *standard HFI* responses needs γ = 0.94 and indexation γP = 0.91 (prices reset about every 4 years) and a tiny financial friction (κ = 0.0025); output is overpredicted and the spread response is near zero. Matching the *purged* responses gives γ = 0.88, no indexation (γP = 0.00) and κ = 0.0245, "an order of magnitude larger"; switching financial frictions off (κ = 0) makes output much more muted. Of the candidate news shocks (technology, discount factor, government spending, capital quality), only news of a capital-quality (asset-valuation) shock two quarters ahead matches the information shock — higher asset prices, more investment, lower spreads, higher output and prices, partly offset by policy; the model underpredicts the yield response.

**Fed private information (Appendix C, Table C.1).** Mixed: the Greenbook-minus-Blue Chip gap in one-quarter-ahead growth forecasts significantly predicts the information shock (0.0143), but the current-quarter growth gap predicts the *policy* shock (0.0183), and price or unemployment gaps predict neither (180 observations).

## Limitations and caveats

- **Version**: the file is the ECB working paper (No 2133, revised June 2018), not the 2020 *AEJ: Macroeconomics* article named in the file title and the tracker; numbers and sections may differ from the published version.
- **The reference list is not in the file**: the text goes from the conclusion (page 42) straight to the appendices.
- All figures (1–10, B.1–B.6, D.1) survive only as axis ticks and panel labels; responses quoted above come from the text. Tables 2 and 4 are split one entry per line but readable in order.
- Equations (1), (12) and the appendix derivations (A.1)–(A.20), (B.1) lose matrix layout and some fraction bars; (8) and (19) are legible apart from fractions split across lines. Their content is described in words above.
- Authors' own caveats: the shocks are the policy and information content of FOMC announcements only, not of speeches; the sign restrictions give set, not point, identification (robust bounds are wider and include zero more often); the baseline can misclassify hikes accompanied by bad news, which the supply-information extension addresses; the paper does not compute variance shares (that needs external-instrument rescaling, footnote 4); the inflation-compensation restrictions assume the inflation risk premium moves like the excess bond premium; the model's exact, credible communication is a simplification (footnote 17); the Calvo parameter remains above micro evidence, partly because wages are flexible in the model.
