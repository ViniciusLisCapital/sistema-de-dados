## Context and motivation

IS/LM treats money and credit asymmetrically: money is a special asset, while loans, bonds and other debt instruments are lumped into a "bond market" suppressed by Walras' Law, so "bank liabilities [are] central to the monetary transmission mechanism, while giving no role to bank assets." Three reasons to question this: a theoretical literature on intermediaries that gather information and monitor borrowers in ways the auction market cannot (so bank loans acquire "a special status"); the instability of money-demand equations, "itself probably a product of deregulation and innovation by financial intermediaries", which weakens money as a guide to policy; and Benjamin Friedman's finding that a credit measure correlates with nominal GNP as well as money does. Credit "will not mount a serious challenge to money as a transmission mechanism until we have a simple macro model that does for the 'credit view' what IS/LM does for the prevailing 'money-only' view". The paper presents the demand side of the simplest such model.

## Core argument / thesis

Make loans and bonds **imperfect substitutes** (the Tobin 1969 and Brunner–Meltzer 1972 tradition) — because of informational problems, differences in liquidity, or the transactions costs of raising funds in the open market — and the textbook model becomes a three-asset model (money, bonds, loans) in which credit-supply and credit-demand shocks have independent effects on aggregate demand and monetary policy works through bank assets as well as bank liabilities. Credit rationing is "not necessary to rationalize a credit channel for monetary transmission" and is left aside. The policy implication: the relative value of money and credit as indicators and targets depends on the relative variances of money-demand and credit-demand shocks, and the evidence suggests money-demand shocks became more important than credit-demand shocks in the 1980s. The authors do not conclude that money should be ignored; the message is "that a more symmetric treatment of money and credit is feasible and appears warranted."

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Loan market (section I, eq. 1)**: loan demand depends on the loan rate, the bond rate and GNP (a transactions demand for credit, e.g. working capital). Banks hold reserves (required plus excess), bonds and loans against deposits; loan supply is a portfolio share, depending on the two rates, of deposits net of required reserves. Equation (1) equates the two.
- **Money market (eq. 2)**: a conventional LM curve — deposit demand (depending on the bond rate and income) equals the money multiplier times bank reserves. Cash is ignored; excess reserves depend only on the bond rate.
- **Goods market (eq. 3)**: a generic IS curve in which spending depends on both the bond rate and the loan rate.
- **CC curve (section II, eqs. 4–5)**: substituting the money market into the loan market gives the loan rate as a function of the bond rate, income and reserves; substituting that into the IS curve gives the **CC curve** ("commodities and credit", in deference to Patinkin 1956). It slopes down like an IS curve but is shifted by monetary policy (reserves) and by credit-market shocks to loan demand or loan supply, which the IS curve is not. Figure 1 plots CC against LM.
- **Special cases**: CC reduces to IS if loans and bonds are perfect substitutes for borrowers or for lenders, or if commodity demand does not respond to the loan rate — the special assumptions implicit in the money-only view. The "credit-only" extreme is money and bonds as perfect substitutes (a horizontal LM, the liquidity trap); even then "monetary policy still matters because it influences the CC curve."
- **Resolving ambiguities (footnote 8)**: a few comparative-statics signs are ambiguous; each is resolved by one of three assumptions — income elasticities of money and loan demand not too different; loan supply and demand driven mostly by the spread between the loan and bond rates; interest elasticity of the money multiplier not too large.

## Main results / findings

**Comparative statics (section III).** Expenditure and money-demand shocks work as in IS/LM. A rise in reserves shifts both CC and LM outward, so it "might conceivably raise the rate of interest": the credit channel makes monetary policy more expansionary than in IS/LM and raises transactions money demand by more (footnote 9: ruled out under assumptions 1 and 3). An upward shift in credit supply (e.g. lower perceived loan riskiness) shifts CC out along a fixed LM, raising the bond rate and income while lowering the loan rate; a rise in credit demand (e.g. a greater need for working capital) has exactly the opposite effects. Credit-demand shocks are hard to identify in history; credit-supply shocks are easy to find — Bernanke's (1983) account of the Great Depression as a fall in credit supply from riskier loans and banks' concern for liquidity, and the credit controls of March–July 1980, when "'tight money' should, and apparently did, reduce interest rates on government bonds."

**Indicators and targets (section IV, Table 1).** Table 1 gives the qualitative responses of income, money, credit and the bond rate to five shocks with bank reserves as the instrument. Money is a good indicator of future GNP except when money-demand shocks are important; credit is a good indicator except when credit-demand shocks are. On targeting (a qualitative check rather than "a complete Poole (1970) style analysis"): both money and credit targets respond correctly to IS shocks and to credit-supply or money-multiplier shocks. A rise in money demand raises money but lowers credit, so a monetarist central bank contracts reserves and destabilizes GNP ("the familiar Achilles heel of monetarism") while a credit-targeting one expands them correctly; a credit-demand shock lowers money and raises credit, reversing the ranking. "If money demand shocks are more important that credit demand shocks, then a policy of targeting credit is probably better than a policy of targeting money."

**Evidence (section V).** "Money" is M1 and "credit" is the intermediated borrowing of households and businesses from the Flow of Funds (Blinder 1985), footnote 11.
- Table 2 (correlations of GNP growth with money and credit growth, three periods): money was far more correlated with income than credit in 1953–1973, the two were on a more equal footing in 1974:1–1979:3, and in 1979:4–1985:4 money–GNP correlations "dropped sharply" while credit's fell only slightly, "giving a clear edge to credit". Similar findings with VAR residuals (footnote 12).
- Structural demand equations (logarithmic partial adjustment, instrumental variables, 1974:1–1985:4; stability over the whole 1953–1985 period was rejected at a 1973:4/1974:1 break). Money equation: SEE .00811, DW 2.04. Credit equation: lagged credit .865, prime rate -.424, log P .075, log y .292, SEE .00797, DW 2.44. The two interest-rate coefficients in the credit equation are individually insignificant but jointly significant, correctly signed and almost equal in absolute value — suggesting the loan–bill spread drives credit demand. Residual variances of the two equations are about equal. The procedure was designed to fit money demand, so it is "biased toward finding relatively larger credit shocks than money shocks."
- Residual variances by subperiod: in 1974:1–1979:3, .265 (money) against .687 (credit); in 1979:4–1985:4, .435 for credit and a money residual variance the text describes as "much larger". "By this crude measure, the variance of money demand shocks was much smaller than that of credit demand shocks during the first subperiod but much larger during the second." The authors warn the relative sizes may "revert once again to what they were earlier."

## Limitations and caveats

- **Poor OCR throughout.** Greek letters and symbols in equations (1)–(5) are reduced to wrong characters (the loan rate, the reserve ratio and the loan-supply function appear as "o", "p", "t", "€", "A", "4", "1"), and the sign markers under the arguments are displaced; the structure above is taken from the surrounding prose, not reconstructed from the equations.
- **Estimated equations (section V) are partly garbled**: in the money-demand equation the lagged-money and interest-rate coefficients are fused with neighbouring characters (".93910gM.)", ".2221") and the income coefficient reads ".01Z"; in the credit equation the bill-rate coefficient is fused with the variable name (".5141"). Those coefficients are not reported here.
- **Table 2's correlations are scrambled** (decimal points and minus signs dropped or misplaced, e.g. "51, .37", "-50,.54"; period labels such as "197934"), so the values are not cited; the comparison above follows the text. The table's title says 1973-1985 while its rows start in 1953.
- **Residual-variance table**: the scale factor prints as "x 1074" (an OCR rendering of the power of ten), and the money residual variance for 1979:4–1985:4 reads ".g88", unreadable in its first digit.
- **Table 1 has a stray line** ("a el") after row 5; the signs of the five rows are legible. The heading "V. Empirical Evidence" prints as "ve Empirical Evidence".
- Footnote numbering is damaged (footnote 9 prints as "Sthis", footnote 6 as "65", footnote 8 as "SMost").
- Authors' caveats: only the demand side of the simplest of their models is presented (aggregate supply effects of the loan market are left to Blinder 1987); the partial-adjustment specification is one they are "not eager to defend"; the sample was too short to test parameter stability reliably; the evidence is "crude".
