# Capital Controls in Brazil: Effective? — Chamon & Garcia 2016

## Context and motivation

After the late-2008 sudden stop, capital flows to emerging markets recovered to pre-crisis levels, pushed by favorable EM fundamentals and low advanced-economy rates. The concern was that a strong but temporary appreciation does lasting damage to the tradable sector, that inflows overheat the economy (and are attracted further by higher policy rates), and that they fund risky liability structures and asset bubbles. The IMF had come to accept controls as a valid tool "under appropriate circumstances", and a theoretical literature was "begging for empirical studies". Brazil, from October 2009, experimented with market-based inflow controls more actively than any country of similar financial integration — the "currency wars" period — and the authorities were explicit that the aim was to stop the appreciation of the real. The paper asks whether those measures (a) segmented Brazilian from global financial markets and (b) moved the exchange rate.

## Core argument / thesis

The controls worked as a wedge but, for most of the episode, not as an exchange-rate tool. They created onshore–offshore price gaps that arbitrage could no longer close (in the onshore dollar rate and in ADR premia), which is evidence of segmentation even when the volume of flows barely changes. But the first nine measures (late 2009 to mid-2011) did not move the BRL. The exchange rate responded only to the last restrictions — the tax on the notional amount of currency derivatives (announced July 2011, effective September 2011) and the extension of the 6% tax on foreign borrowing to longer maturities — with a depreciation the authors put at 4 to 10 percent. Their reading: those last measures worked because they closed the remaining channels for bypassing the original inflow tax (complementarity with what came before), helped by an unexpected start of monetary easing. Controls "can be effective, but only if they are very comprehensive", and Brazil's non-convertible currency, whose price is formed in the onshore futures market, made them more so.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **The instrument.** Brazil's controls were not unremunerated reserve requirements (Chile, Colombia) but the IOF, an existing tax on financial transactions whose coverage and rate the Executive can change by decree, effective immediately and without Congress. Measures were announced after the close and took effect the next business day — the feature that makes daily-frequency identification possible (Table 1 lists every tightening and loosening, 2009–2014).
- **Gates, and how flows go around them.** The controls taxed only a subset of inflows ("gates" in Klein's terms). Each untaxed channel became a bypass, and the sequence of measures is the sequence of loopholes being closed:
  - equity inflows → buying Depositary Receipts abroad and converting them (taxed on issuance, then on cancellation);
  - fixed-income carry → offshore NDFs, with banks taking the offsetting position onshore and selling dollars to the central bank (same pressure on the currency) → curbed by the unremunerated reserve requirement on banks' gross short spot FX positions;
  - onshore derivatives carry → foreigners paid the inflow tax only on the margin they brought in → taxed directly through the tax on the notional amount of derivatives, levied when a contract shorting foreign currency is bought, sold or expires (so holders also bear the risk of the rate being raised);
  - short-term foreign borrowing → a 6% tax by maturity, which borrowers escaped by lengthening maturity, cheap because the dollar yield curve was flat.
- **Segmentation measured by prices, not volumes.** Since the counterfactual volume of flows is unknown, the test is whether wedges open between onshore and offshore prices of similar assets. Fixed income: the *cupom cambial*, the onshore dollar rate implied by currency futures through covered interest parity, against the offshore dollar rate. Equities: the premium of Petrobras and Vale ADRs over the underlying local shares, which a tax opens into a no-arbitrage band whose upper edge is reached when foreign excess demand is strong.
- **Exchange-rate equation.** Daily change in the log BRL/USD (PTAX) on singleton dummies for each measure, controlling for the CDI–LIBOR spread, the onshore dollar rate, Ibovespa, VIX, CRB commodities, the Fed's dollar index, Asian and Latin American (ex-BRL) currency indices, and BCB FX purchases and sales (instrumented with their own lags and lagged implied volatility). A second version adds lagged levels (error correction). Longer windows (t−1 to t+1, t−2 to t+2) and an event study with abnormal cumulative changes test whether markets took time to digest the measures.
- **Identification assumption.** Controls were decided on a months-long appreciation trend, not on the previous few days' moves, and a forward-looking exchange rate should price their long-run effect at once. A probit for the timing of measures gives low fitted probabilities even on the dates themselves. Any remaining endogeneity biases the effect toward zero. The assumption that carries the result is that the reaction function did not change in late 2011; the authors argue that would be "farfetched".
- **Why sterilized purchases had no traction (footnote 19).** With the onshore dollar rate above the offshore rate, BCB purchases raise the onshore rate further and banks bring in short-term funds to arbitrage it, supplying the very dollars the central bank bought. Sales do not trigger the reverse, because the onshore rate stays above the offshore one.

## Main results / findings

- **Segmentation: yes, more for equity than for fixed income.** The onshore–offshore dollar spread hovered around 1 percent before the controls, barely reacted to the first measures, and spiked after the March–April 2011 taxes on short-term foreign loans (90-day cupom cambial peaking at over 8 percent), then drifted back as banks switched to longer, tax-exempt foreign borrowing — controls becoming more porous over time. The Petrobras ADR premium, near zero before, rose with the first controls and stayed high until the equity tax was removed in December 2011; the DR-issuance tax is the most robust effect on ADR premia. Equity segmentation was closer to the tax rate because the equity tax stayed at 2 percent and equity investors (pension and mutual funds) are longer-term and more constrained in using derivatives.
- **Flows adapted rather than stopped.** After the 6% tax on borrowing under one year, short-term external borrowing fell from US$ 6.5 billion in March 2011 to US$ 26 million in April 2011, and almost all new debt moved above the taxed maturity (Fig. 3). Total volume was more volatile and on average smaller. FDI rose strongly in the same period, which may partly be relabeling, though the BCB told the authors intra-company loans were taxed like any loan.
- **Exchange rate: nothing from the first nine, a lot from the last.** Brazil, June 2009–March 2012: the average effect of the 13 control dummies is positive and significant (0.34 percent per measure in Table 2, about 4.5 percent if all are treated as permanent), but the average of the first nine is close to zero and insignificant in every specification. The significant dummies are the initial 2% inflow tax, the derivatives tax and the extension of the borrowing tax to five years; the implementation day of the derivatives tax gives the largest effect in the two-day window (2.8 percent, Table 3). Over the preferred t−2 to t+2 window the 13-measure average implies 7.5 to 10 percent (Table 4). The event study agrees: the surprise rate cut plus implementation of the derivatives tax shows a cumulative depreciation peaking above 10 percent before settling around 7.5 percent.
- **What did not fit the textbook.** The interest rate differential is insignificant or very small (in the error-correction version a 1 percent rise appreciates the real by only 0.15 percent on impact), the onshore dollar rate is insignificant, and sterilized FX intervention shows no effect in either direction. The BRL is driven instead by commodity prices, the dollar index and Latin American currencies. Taxes on foreign borrowing up to one and two years come out with a *negative* sign (appreciation) in the two-day window.
- **Persistence.** A levels regression without the control dummies, fitted on samples ending at each stage, tracks the BRL in sample and only falls systematically below the actual rate from the surprise rate cut onward, with the actual rate 5 to 10 percent more depreciated than fitted after March 2012. Against other EM and commodity currencies, the BRL breaks from the Chilean and Colombian pesos from July 2011.
- **Costs.** Higher funding costs for Brazilian firms (equity raised from foreigners bore the 2% tax; small firms could not borrow long abroad), lower liquidity in derivatives markets, and, given a domestic saving rate of 16%, possibly lower investment and growth; a market view (Volpon) attributes weak growth to a self-inflicted "sudden stop". On the prudential side, the maturity lengthening may have raised resilience.

## Limitations and caveats

- **Snapshot identification.** The estimates are taken around event dates, so the authors cannot say which channels or policy interactions drove the depreciation, nor separate the derivatives tax from the cumulative effect of earlier measures or from the August 2011 surprise rate cut and the easing cycle that followed (the policy rate fell from 12.50% to 7.25%). They suggest the same 6% tax may have deterred more once the policy rate was lower.
- **Persistence is not established.** The models explain short-run changes; whether the effect lasted is inferred loosely, and the authors are cautious with longer windows (t−2 to t+5 yields about 15 percent but is not their preferred estimate).
- **External validity.** The effect is much larger than the literature's typical findings (Magud et al. survey: mixed evidence on volumes, stronger on composition), which the authors attribute to how comprehensive the measures were and to the real's non-convertibility with price discovery in the first-to-mature onshore futures contract. Neither condition need hold elsewhere, and more comprehensive controls may also cost more — left for further research.
- **Few intervention sales.** Only 6 BCB sale episodes in the sample, so the no-effect result for sales is weakly identified.
- **Damaged passages.** The covered-interest-parity formula for the cupom cambial (Section 3.1) and both regression specifications in Section 3.3 are extracted as scattered symbols and variable names, unreadable as equations; the variable list survives in the surrounding prose. Figures 1–7 survive only as axis ticks and annotation labels. Appendix S1 (econometrics for the cupom cambial and ADR premia, foreigners' derivatives positions, IOF revenue plot, comparison with other EM currencies) is referenced but not in the file. Footnote 25 is cut off mid-sentence at the end of the file. The text refers to "Table 4" and "Table 1" in two places where Table 2 is meant.
