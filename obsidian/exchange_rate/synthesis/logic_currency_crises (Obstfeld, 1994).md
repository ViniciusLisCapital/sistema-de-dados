## Context and motivation

The 1992–93 crisis of the European Monetary System, which ended in the August 1993 widening of the ERM bands, shook even pegs that looked sustainable, and the variety of currencies attacked led observers to suspect self-fulfilling forces. Economists had good reason to distrust that story — ministers prefer to blame speculators rather than policy errors — and Krugman (1979) had given them a rigorous alternative: a peg collapses because unsustainable policy runs reserves down, and foresighted speculators attack just before the end. Obstfeld argues that this model cannot explain the European episode. Industrial countries with access to world capital markets can borrow reserves, so reserve adequacy is no longer the binding constraint; what governments weighed in 1992 were the costs of high interest rates and rising unemployment. Once the government is treated as an agent that chooses whether to hold the peg, the question becomes what makes it give up — and that opens the door to crises that happen only because they are expected.

## Core argument / thesis

A government defends a peg as long as the cost of defending it is smaller than the cost of abandoning it. But part of the cost of defending depends on what markets expect: devaluation expectations raise domestic interest rates (worsening the public finances, banks and firms) and enter wage settlements (eroding competitiveness and raising unemployment). Because expectations shape the government's incentives and the incentives shape the expectations, there can be more than one equilibrium: one in which the peg is credible and survives, and one in which markets expect a devaluation, the expectation makes holding on too painful, and the government devalues. A peg that is viable under one set of expectations can collapse under another, and the shift between them can be set off by trivial news. Reserve losses accompany such a crisis but do not cause it. The "fundamentals versus self-fulfilling" dichotomy is false: the fundamental is the government's inability to commit, given its preferences and constraints, and the constraints themselves depend on expectations.

## Key mechanisms / model

**Section 2 — the first-generation benchmark (Krugman 1979, via the exhaustible-resource model).**
- A government that pegs the price of an exhaustible resource ("gold") below its free-market path sells from its stockpile until it is gone. The *shadow price* — the market price that would prevail if the stockpile passed to private hands at that moment — rises over time as the private stock is drawn down. The attack happens exactly when the shadow price reaches the pegged price: earlier, buyers would make a capital loss; later, they would forgo an infinite instantaneous gain. At that moment speculators buy the entire remaining stock and the price does not jump.
- The currency analogue: with money demand falling in the nominal interest rate, interest parity, PPP and a central bank whose domestic credit grows at an exogenous constant rate, reserves must fall over time under the peg. With a reserve floor (arbitrarily zero) and a float afterwards, the *shadow exchange rate* (Flood and Garber) is the float that would clear the market once reserves are gone. The attack comes when it reaches the peg; reserves jump to zero rather than running down smoothly, because the switch to a float raises expected depreciation and the interest rate, so real money demand falls and the money supply must shrink at once to avoid a jump in the exchange rate. The attack comes earlier the more interest-sensitive money demand is; with zero sensitivity there is no attack, only exhaustion. In stochastic versions (Flood and Garber) interest rates rise as reserves fall and the collapse brings an unanticipated discrete depreciation.
- What carries the result: the exogenous credit process and the reserve floor. The model never asks why the government lets reserves go, or why it could not borrow more — which is the gap the paper fills.

**The Swedish case (1992) as a diagnosis.** Riksbank reserves were flat or rising until early summer; the krona survived an August–September attack with an overnight rate pushed to 500 percent and borrowed reserves, after which reserves rose and rates fell — then the peg was dropped on November 19 without a comparable interest-rate defense. Recession, a budget swing from surplus to deep deficit, a fragile banking system and a real appreciation made defense painful; the benefits were credibility of the nominal anchor and a demonstration of readiness for EC membership. The Danish Maastricht vote, the Finnish float, Black Wednesday and the French "petit oui" lowered those benefits and made devaluation more "excusable". Reserve losses in November were a symptom, not the cause; the apparent exhaustion of net reserves is an artifact of intra-government accounting (the National Debt Office had borrowed the intervention reserves).

**Section 3.1 — interest rates and public debt (two-period model).**
- A government with domestic-currency ("lira") debt and possibly foreign-currency ("mark") positions can, in period 2, finance itself with distorting taxes or with depreciation (an inflation levy on money and nominal debt), minimizing a loss in both. It cannot precommit in period 1.
- The market sets the period-1 nominal interest rate from expected depreciation (interest parity). A higher rate raises the government's period-2 nominal obligations — the base on which depreciation raises revenue — and so raises the depreciation it finds optimal. With a positively sloped government reaction function and the interest-parity schedule, there can be two intersections: a low-depreciation and a high-depreciation equilibrium (Figure 5). The government prefers the low one but cannot promise to avoid validating the high one.
- Under a peg with a fixed cost of realignment, there can be an equilibrium with no devaluation expected and none occurring, and another where the market expects devaluation, sets a high rate and the government devalues despite the cost — the first example of a self-fulfilling attack. A fall in the realignment cost (as after the Danish vote) can create a devaluation equilibrium where none existed.
- What carries it: the government must roll over or issue domestic-currency debt in period 1. With no lira debt to issue, the reaction function is flat and the bad equilibrium disappears. Long maturities help (Giavazzi and Pagano), but the condition is broader — short-term commitments matched by short-term receipts. Borrowing enough in foreign currency to cover the period-1 cash need removes the multiplicity; borrowing a little can make things worse by shrinking the depreciation tax base without removing the incentive (Figure 7). The model is linked to Italy in September 1992 and to Britain in the 1950s and 1960s.

**Section 3.2 — wages, competitiveness and the escape clause (Barro–Gordon in an open economy).**
- Output rises with the real exchange rate (exchange rate relative to the predetermined nominal wage) and falls with a demand shock; wages are set a period ahead to target a constant real wage. A government that dislikes inflation and wants output above its natural level uses depreciation to offset shocks and to attempt surprises, which wage setters anticipate: an inflation bias with no output gain. A fixed rate removes the bias but also the ability to respond to shocks.
- With a fixed cost of realignment, the government holds the peg for moderate shocks and devalues only when the shock is large enough that the gain from adjusting exceeds the cost — an "escape clause", which in principle can raise welfare.
- But the shock threshold at which the government devalues depends on the depreciation already built into wages, and that expectation depends on where markets think the threshold is. This circularity gives multiple equilibria: a credible one with a high threshold and low expected depreciation, and a fragile one in which high expected depreciation raises wages, worsens competitiveness and unemployment, and makes devaluation likely unless shocks are favorable — low credibility validates itself.

## Main results / findings

The paper has no econometrics; its evidence is the Swedish narrative, one table and numerical examples of the models.

- **Markets did not see 1992 coming.** Table 1: a German investor rolling one-month deposits from the adoption or tightening of an ECU peg to the month of collapse earned less than on DM deposits — for the krona (June 1991–Dec. 1992) an annualized return of -0.12 against 9.73 on the DM, with lower returns than the DM also on the lira and sterling. Little devaluation premium had been priced, consistent with a sudden shift of expectations rather than a slow, foreseen exhaustion.
- **Sweden's reserves contradict the Krugman pattern.** Reserves did not trend down before the attacks, the September defense was won with interest rates and borrowed reserves, and the November collapse came after reserves had been rebuilt, once the political and economic benefits of holding on had fallen — the Riksbank sold over 160 billion kronor in six days, a symptom of the decision to let go.
- **Numerical examples confirm multiplicity.** In the wage model (uniform shocks, a fairly accommodative government in the paper's parameterization), one equilibrium threshold carries expected depreciation of 1.23 percent per period and the other 5.71 percent (Figure 8); the shift between them can take the market from viewing devaluation as nearly impossible to nearly certain, with a jump in interest rates and reserve losses along the way. In the debt model, the illustrated equilibrium rates are implausibly high, which the author attributes to the two-period setup in which all debt is repaid at once.
- **What would contradict the models:** crises preceded by steady, foreseeable reserve depletion under exogenous credit growth with no change in the government's costs of defense — the first-generation pattern. Persistent negative output shocks in the wage model, however, also produce rising rates and reserve losses before a realignment, so a gradual run-up alone does not discriminate.

## Limitations and caveats

- **No theory of which equilibrium occurs or when.** The author says economists have "no more than an inkling" of why attacks happen on one day and not another ("why was Black Wednesday not Black Tuesday or Black Thursday?"); a model of signals accumulating into a crash is left to further work.
- **Stylized models.** Money demand in the debt model is interest-inelastic (adding elasticity would only make self-fulfilling attacks more likely, the author notes); the second model assumes serially independent shocks and history-independent expectations (no reputation), and its multiplicity in the example relies on a fairly accommodative government — more complex shock distributions are said to give multiplicity under less accommodative ones (citing Obstfeld 1991). The realignment cost is assumed, not derived; the author does not explore why governments chose to bind themselves to it.
- **Reserves are freely borrowable** in both models, subject only to the intertemporal budget constraint — the opposite extreme from Krugman's; for countries without market access the first-generation logic may still apply.
- **Damaged passages.** OCR has garbled nearly every equation: the resource model's price and stock equations (1)–(3) and the slope derivation after (3); the interest-parity equation (5) is missing between (4) and its description; (7)–(9) (credit growth, the reserve-share expression and the shadow exchange rate); the government budget constraints (10), (11), (13), (17), (18) and (22); the loss functions (15), (21), (25), (26), (30); the reaction function and equilibrium conditions (19), (20), (27)–(29), (31)–(35). Greek letters are misread throughout (the parameters of the Figure 8 example print as "a = 1, 6 = 0.15" and "« = 1 and 9 = O.15"), and the parameter list for Figure 5 (footnote 23) is scrambled. Figures 1–8 survive only as axis labels and scattered tick values. Several footnote markers are corrupted into stray characters, and the reference list is not in the file. None of these equations or parameter settings is reproduced here.
