## Context and motivation

Written by two BCB deputy governors and a BCB researcher, the paper tells how monetary policy brought inflation and survey expectations back to target after the 2002 confidence crisis: the disinflation and recovery of 2003, the 2004 inflation rebound and tightening, and the consolidation of 2005–06. The organizing idea is in the title: under limited credibility, expectations over-react to current shocks, especially upward surprises, so disinflation is "a process of taming inflation expectations". The question is whether the regime ended up anchoring them, measured with the BCB's market survey of some 100 professional forecasters (daily median, mean and standard deviation since January 2000). Inflation-linked bonds are set aside: liquidity at policy-relevant horizons was too low to trust the implied expectations (footnote 3).

## Core argument / thesis

Expectations in Brazil are driven by past inflation, the target, the exchange rate, commodity prices, activity and the policy stance. As credibility builds, the backward-looking component cedes ground to the target. Credibility is built by acting on expectations: a policy rate held against public pressure until expectations turn, explicit and transparent convergence paths when a shock pushes inflation off target, and an asymmetric reaction (counter adverse surprises, bank favourable ones). By 2005–06 expectations had converged, the dispersion of forecasts had fallen and the target had become a statistically significant attractor, but the record was still short: the upper tail of expectations remained high, so the authors argue the bank still had to lean against a bias toward the upper half of the band.

## Key mechanisms / model

**Why expectations over-react under weak credibility.** When the public doubts the target, current inflation surprises are read as information about future inflation, so short-term shocks contaminate the medium-term outlook. The policy reply is to prevent that contamination rather than to offset the shock itself. Two reasons for fast rather than gradual disinflation in 2003 follow: Brazil's history of indexation, and credibility (lingering fears of fiscal dominance and of policy ineffectiveness).

**Transparent, partial accommodation of an inflation overshoot (2004).** After the 2004 overshoot, Copom estimated the inertia it would carry into 2005 and chose to accommodate 2/3 of it, announcing an explicit 5.1% objective for 2005 instead of the 4.5% midpoint, which lengthened convergence from 12 to 24 months. The point was to stop expectations settling near the top of the band, the 7% neighbourhood they seemed to be heading for, by naming a path the bank would defend.

**Asymmetric response.** While credibility is being built, the bank lets favourable surprises pass fully into price setting and counteracts adverse ones. In mid-2005 a run of favourable surprises then cut both the mean and the dispersion of expectations, which the authors attribute to cumulative policy plus the signal from the last small hikes rather than to those hikes breaking inertia.

**The yield curve as the policy stance.** Because markets price expected cuts, the current Selic is a poor measure of the stance during disinflation; too inverted a curve can undo the intended tightening. In 2004 the one-year real rate barely rose after three hikes (a Brazilian "conundrum"), and with buoyant asset prices and new payroll-backed household credit this weakened transmission. The authors read longer rates moving ahead of the Selic in 2003 and 2005 as evidence of more credibility, not less effectiveness.

**Managed prices as an expectations channel.** About a third of the IPCA is set by contracts, often indexed backward to the IGP-DI, which is dominated by wholesale prices and sensitive to the exchange rate and commodities (footnote 7). A depreciation therefore raises projected managed prices with a lag, which is how the May 2004 depreciation moved expectations.

**Why the lower half of the band matters.** If agents regard the lower half of the tolerance band as unattainable, their subjective distribution will not be centred on the midpoint, and the bank must keep leaning against that skew just to hit the centre. Undershooting the midpoint in 2006, a first for the regime, is presented as important for this reason.

**Regressions (Table 1; 36-month rolling windows, Figures 8–14).** The dependent variable is 12-month-ahead survey expectations of 12-month IPCA inflation. Specification I regresses it on the 12-month-ahead target, in the spirit of rational-expectations tests: a zero constant means no systematic deviation, a slope of one means full anchoring. Specification II adds the output gap (HP-filtered industrial production, lagged twice), past 12-month inflation and the six-month nominal exchange-rate change; specification III replaces the exchange-rate change by EMBI+ Brazil and the real effective exchange-rate gap. The authors warn of omitted-variable bias: the target was set in light of inflation history (footnote 13).

## Main results / findings

- **The target is an attractor, unevenly over time.** Over 2000:1–2006:8 the target coefficient is significant and not different from one in specifications I and II (0.80 and 0.77, Table 1). In rolling windows it starts near one, falls below half and loses significance in the windows dominated by the 2002 crisis, and returns to significance and near one in the recent windows; the constant moves the other way, large and significant in the crisis windows and near zero recently.
- **Backward-looking weight fades.** In specification II past inflation enters with 0.20 (a 1 p.p. rise in 12-month inflation raises expectations by 0.20 p.p.) and the exchange-rate change with 0.06, "similar to the pass-through coefficient usually found in Phillips curve estimations" for the period; the output gap is not significant. In recent rolling windows past inflation is around zero and insignificant and the exchange-rate coefficient falls, possibly lower pass-through. In specification III past inflation is not significant over the full sample. The authors stress that significant non-target regressors do not by themselves mean low credibility: transmission lags, and a dependent variable that includes the very short run, leave room for cyclical effects even under a credible regime.
- **Lower inflation uncertainty.** Twelve-month forecast errors (actual minus expected) reached 12.8 p.p. during the crisis, turned negative from November 2003 to June 2004 (expectations formed in the crisis overestimated inflation), and stayed below 1 p.p. in absolute value from July 2005 to August 2006. Disagreement across respondents, measured by the coefficient of variation, rose above 0.3 between mid-2002 and mid-2003 and has generally stayed below 0.1 since late 2003. The survey has point forecasts only, so disagreement is the only uncertainty measure available; the authors justify it by its co-movement with individual uncertainty in the US Survey of Professional Forecasters (correlation of 0.6, Giordani and Söderlind).
- **The policy episodes support the mechanism.** Expectations for 2003 kept rising until the April 2003 decision to hold the Selic at its peak, and only then started falling; in 2004 tightening at first did not lower expectations, which the authors explain by the weak link from the Selic to market rates and by credit growth.
- **The tail is not yet tamed.** An imputed ceiling of 12-month-ahead expectations (mean plus two standard deviations) averaged 7.2% since December 2001, and only 13% of the time was it below 5%.

## Limitations and caveats

- By the authors' account: the target regressions suffer from omitted variables (targets were set in light of past inflation); the output gap uses revised data and an HP filter applied to the full sample; the six-month exchange-rate change is chosen because monthly changes are too noisy; the dependent variable includes the very short run; the lags of transmission are less robustly estimated for Brazil than for countries with longer histories; disagreement is only a proxy for uncertainty.
- What the text shows: the authors were BCB officials narrating their own policy, so the episodes are the bank's reading of events. The regressions use one survey measure, one horizon and a short sample (2000:1–2006:8); the rolling-window results are shown only as figures, and the specification III rolling results are "not shown". Links between policy moves and expectations are argued from timing, not identified.
- Damaged passages: Figures 1–14 survive only as axis values, scattered data labels and legends (Figure 3's title appears below its numbers and a stray block of numbers precedes Figure 4), so they cannot be read from the file.
