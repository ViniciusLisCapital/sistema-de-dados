## Context and motivation

Inflation targeting made market forecasts central for the BCB: it reads them to assess its own policy, and uses them as an input to decisions, since expected inflation enters firms' price setting. Earlier Brazilian work had studied inflation forecasts alone and mostly the aggregate. The paper takes the whole panel of the BCB's survey of about 100 professional forecasters (the Gerin survey, now Focus) during the first decade of targeting and asks five questions about it: are inflation forecasts rational; do they beat standard time-series models; how good are inflation, Selic and exchange-rate forecasts jointly; do the published Top 5 forecasters steer the rest ("epidemiology"); and what drives inflation forecasts, Selic forecasts and the dispersion of inflation forecasts. Bonds are set aside as a measure of expectations because their liquidity at policy-relevant horizons was too low (footnote 1). Forecasts are read on the eve of the IPCA-15 release (the "critical date"), so that they contain no information on the current month's prices.

## Core argument / thesis

The survey behaves like the expectations of agents who believe in the regime. Inflation forecasts are anchored by the target, more so once the 2002–2003 confidence crisis faded, and Selic forecasts move with the gap between expected inflation and the target by more than one for one: the market expects the BCB to follow a Taylor-type rule consistent with targeting. That chain (Selic expectations depend on inflation expectations, which depend on the target) is what the authors call the credibility built by the BCB. The forecasts are not textbook-rational, though: unbiased but inefficient in the use of interest-rate and exchange-rate information; their errors are mostly common to all respondents, i.e. driven by aggregate shocks (or by forecasters aligning with peers); and part of the panel adjusts to the published Top 5 forecasts. Since survey forecasts are at least as accurate as standard models, the authors argue that publicizing them, and the Top 5 in particular, is welfare-improving.

## Key mechanisms / model

**Rationality tests (Section 3).** Unbiasedness: realized 12-month inflation regressed on the forecast should give a zero constant and a unit slope (3.1), or the error should have zero mean (3.2); an asymmetric variant (3.3, after Ang, Bekaert and Wei) adds a dummy for inflation above its 12-month moving average, to ask whether forecasts are biased when inflation is rising. Efficiency: the error should not be predictable from variables known at the forecast date (3.4–3.5). Overlapping 12-month horizons make errors serially correlated by construction (a surprise in one month enters twelve consecutive forecasts), so the regressions use Newey–West errors with an MA(k) structure.

**Survey against models (Section 4).** The mean survey forecast is compared, by root mean squared error, with recursively estimated ARMA, VAR and Bayesian VAR forecasts on the standard monthly set (inflation, Selic, exchange rate, industrial production), in log-levels and log-differences. The purpose is not to find the best forecaster but to see whether a large, judgment-based information set adds to standard autoregressions.

**Joint accuracy (Section 5, Bauer, Eisenbeis, Waggoner and Zha).** Each forecaster's vector of errors for inflation, Selic and exchange rate is normalized by a covariance matrix, so that errors on easier-to-forecast variables weigh more and correlations among variables are taken into account; under normality the normalized error is chi-squared and its p-value, the "accuracy score", is the probability of a worse forecast. The covariance splits into a **common** component (errors from unpredictable aggregate shocks, proxied by the mean survey forecast's error or by a BVAR's) and an **idiosyncratic** one (the cross-section dispersion of errors, a forecaster using a worse model).

**Epidemiology (Section 6, inspired by Carroll 2003 but run respondent by respondent).** Each forecaster's 12-month forecast is regressed on the lagged published long-term Top 5 forecast, controlling for her own lagged forecast and lagged error (6.1); then adding either the mean BVAR forecast as a proxy for the statistical model forecasters use (6.2) or the current median survey forecast (6.3), and, for inflation, the target. A significant Top 5 coefficient means the forecaster adjusts to the published leaders. The controls are imperfect by the authors' account: the BVAR may not be anyone's model, and the median already embeds the Top 5 influence (footnote 18).

**Why publicizing the leaders can be good (Section 6).** Following Cornand and Heinemann, low-precision public information should be partly withheld and high-precision information released fully; the more precise the public signal, the less likely an exaggerated weight on it reduces welfare. Since survey forecasts are as good as standard models and the Top 5 are better still, publishing them lowers the cost to firms and markets of forming higher-order expectations.

**Determinants of inflation forecasts (Section 7.1, after Bevilaqua, Mesquita and Minella).** Regressors come from a Phillips curve: the 12-month-ahead target, the output gap (recursive HP filter on industrial production, lagged two months), past 12-month inflation, and six-month changes in the exchange rate and commodity prices; a second specification adds a dummy for the peak of the crisis (2002M11–2003M1). Why other variables than the target can legitimately matter under a credible regime: the dependent variable is inflation over the next 12 months, not inflation 12 months ahead, so transmission lags leave room for current shocks; and the tolerance band lets the bank accommodate part of a shock, since neutralizing every temporary shock would create stop-and-go policy.

**Perceived policy rule (Section 7.2).** The 12-month-ahead forecast of the average Selic target is regressed, by 2SLS, on the gap between 12-month-ahead expected inflation and the target, with and without the lagged Selic (smoothing). The test is the Taylor principle as perceived by the market: a response above one means agents expect real rates to rise when expected inflation rises. The steady-state constant reads as the equilibrium real rate the market has in mind.

**Dispersion (Section 7.3).** The survey collects point forecasts only, so the coefficient of variation across respondents is the available proxy for inflation uncertainty, justified by its co-movement with individual uncertainty in the US SPF (footnote 26).

## Main results / findings

- **Unbiased, but weakly so; not efficient.** Unbiasedness is not rejected for the median 12-month forecast, Jan 2002 to Aug 2007 (Table 3.1), but the estimated constant is high and the non-rejection owes much to large standard errors; no asymmetry is found when inflation rises (Table 3.2), again with large standard errors. Forecasts use past inflation and the output gap efficiently but not exchange-rate and interest-rate information over 2002:1–2007:7 (Table 3.3). From 2004 on the exchange-rate inefficiency disappears and the interest-rate one remains; the (unshown) coefficients say higher interest rates would have reduced forecast errors, read as a misperception of how fast policy transmits to inflation.
- **Survey at least as good as models, and better once the crisis is out.** Over 2002:1–2007:6 the mean survey forecast's error is very close to the ARMA and the log-differenced VARs and BVARs; over 2004:1–2007:6 it is well below every autoregressive model: 1.22 against 2.47 for the best (Table 4.1). The authors read it as forecasters using information and judgment beyond the autoregressions, and the crisis-period gap as an overshooting of forecasts driven by a regime change that did not happen.
- **The exchange rate drove the big inflation errors.** Inflation and exchange-rate forecast errors have a correlation of 0.90 (Figure 4.1), very strong in the first half of the sample and unimportant in the last three years. The extreme case: in 2002:5 agents expected a 9.0% average depreciation over the next twelve months against 40.7% realized.
- **Errors are mostly common.** Joint accuracy is very low in 2002, worsens again over 2004–2005 (survey-based scores), and improves afterwards, not monotonically (Figure 5.1); the 2004 trough is shallower with model-based scores (Figure 5.3). For each variable the common error component prevails over the idiosyncratic one, except when dispersion is large (end-2002) or the average error small (second half of 2004) (Figure 5.2) — aggregate shocks, or forecasters aligning with peers to avoid "large desynchronized forecast errors".
- **Top 5 forecasts spread to the panel, robustly for inflation and the Selic, fragilely for the exchange rate.** For inflation, the Top 5 coefficient is significant in a little more than half of the respondents' regressions once controls are added (6.2b and 6.3b, Table 6.1), and about 25% adjust to the Top 5 rather than to their own past forecast. For the Selic, about two-fifths of respondents. For the exchange rate, about half with BVAR controls but about one-fifth with the median as control. Own lagged forecasts and errors are also significant for many, which the authors read as learning.
- **The target anchors inflation forecasts, more after the crisis.** Over 2000:1–2008:6 the target coefficient is significant, 0.51 and 0.72 without and with the crisis dummy, and a unit coefficient is rejected at 10% (Table 7.1). Past inflation, the exchange-rate change and, new in the Brazilian literature, commodity prices (small) are significant; the output gap is not. In 48-month rolling windows (Figure 7.1) the target coefficient falls to around zero in windows containing the crisis and is around one or above for samples starting in 2004; the past-inflation weight drops in 2003 and stays around 0.15-0.30; the exchange-rate coefficient falls and narrows (lower pass-through to expectations); commodity prices matter only in the last windows.
- **The market expects the Taylor principle.** The response of Selic forecasts to expected inflation minus target is 1.42 without smoothing and 1.23 with it, both significantly above one (Table 7.2, 2002:1–2008:7, unadjusted targets); smoothing raises the fit substantially, and the constant implies an equilibrium real rate of 7.7% per year. Inflation and Selic forecast errors correlate at 0.95 (Figure 7.2). With the adjusted target series the point estimates are 1.22 and 1.32 and a unit response is not rejected in specification II (p-value 0.12, footnote 25): the "greater than one" result depends on which target the market is assumed to use.
- **Dispersion rises with country risk and rising inflation.** The coefficient of variation of inflation forecasts depends positively on lagged EMBI and on the six-month change in 12-month inflation, with a dummy for 2002:10 (Table 7.3); dispersion of inflation, Selic and exchange-rate forecasts move together (correlation around 0.6-0.8).

## Limitations and caveats

- By the authors' account: a short sample hit by large shocks, so the rationality tests have little power; the model comparison and the output gap use revised, not real-time, data (footnotes 13 and 22); the 12-month forecasts for 2000:1–2001:10 are interpolated from calendar-year forecasts and kept out of the bias tests (footnote 2); some epidemiology regressions have serially correlated residuals and are dropped, and Table 6.1 reports only the rest; the BVAR and median controls are imperfect; the Selic equation uses 12-month inflation forecasts for any horizon h "for simplicity"; dispersion is only a proxy for uncertainty. Which learning rule fits the data, and how learning and higher-order expectations constrain policy, are left open.
- What the text shows: the "credibility" reading rests on the regime surviving the 2002 crisis and on a sample ending in 2008. The Top 5 influence is identified from lagged correlation with published forecasts, which also captures any common information the Top 5 react to first; the welfare argument for publicizing them is drawn from theory, not tested. The perceived-rule result flips to "not different from one" with the adjusted targets (footnote 25), the target the paper itself calls "the main reference for agents" for 2003–2005. The working-paper date in the file (Apr. 2009, n. 185) differs from the year in its file name and in the tracker (2012), which Gaglianone also uses when citing it; the published version is not in the file.
- Damaged passages: all equations (3.1)–(3.5), (5.1)–(5.2), (6.1)–(6.3b) and (7.2.1)–(7.2.2) are spread over several lines with subscripts and superscripts detached; the note describes them in words. The header of Table 6.1 is scrambled into fragments, so its columns are identified only from the table note (the last two columns are the shares significant on the Top 5 but not on the own lagged forecast, or the own error). In Table 3.2 the model is labelled "3.2" though the text describes equation (3.3). In Table 7.1 the standard error of the target in specification I lacks its parentheses. All figures (2.1–2.4, 4.1, 5.1–5.3, 7.1–7.3) survive only as axis values, legends and reversed month labels ("20-naJ"). Footnote markers are fused to the words they follow ("inflation1"). The reference list is not in the file.
