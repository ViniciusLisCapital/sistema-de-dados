## Context and motivation

How expectations are formed, and how to model it, is an open question; survey expectations are consistently biased and their errors predictable, which contradicts full-information rational expectations (unbiased expectations, unpredictable errors). Models with information frictions explain both features. The paper collects stylized facts about inflation expectations in Brazil from 23 papers "selected ad hoc", to help model builders and policymakers bridge theory and practice. It is a catalogue, not a new estimate: each finding is attributed to its paper, and Table A2 gives the sample of each.

## Core argument / thesis

Brazilian evidence fits the information-friction view better than full-information rational expectations. Professional forecasters' expectations are persistent, anchored by the target and shaped by fiscal policy, the exchange rate, commodity prices, activity and monetary policy. They update infrequently and disagree even when they update; the Top5 forecasters update more often and lead the others; and on average they underpredict inflation, though unbiasedness is not rejected at short horizons. Consumers, by contrast, adjust essentially to current inflation and overpredict it. The target worked as coordinator of expectations, with imperfect credibility: expectations over-react to upward surprises, and lower credibility goes with more disagreement.

## Key mechanisms / model

**Two families of information-friction models (Section 1).** In *sticky information* (Mankiw and Reis; Reis), each period an agent receives new information with a constant probability (a Poisson process) and, when she does, forms expectations rationally with full information; the rest keep old expectations. That probability is the degree of attention and its complement the degree of information rigidity; updaters all share the same expectation, non-updaters do not revise (footnotes 1–2). In *noisy information* (Woodford; Sims; Maćkowiak and Wiederholt) agents update every period but see only a noisy signal of the state, so expectations are a weighted average of new and previous information, the weight on past beliefs measuring the rigidity. Morris and Shin add strategic interaction: forecasters weigh public against private signals and may overweight private ones to differentiate themselves.

**Why forecasts can be biased without irrationality (footnote 7).** Asymmetric loss functions over forecast errors, model misspecification, strategic incentives and information rigidities all produce bias; with different loss asymmetries, disagreement can arise without information rigidities.

**Two measures and their trade-off (Section 2).** Break-even inflation (from NTN-B, Svensson curve) is daily and based on decisions with money at stake, but carries liquidity and risk premia; survey expectations can outperform statistical models and embed forecasters' judgment. Figure 1 shows the Focus median following the low-frequency movement of 12-month break-even inflation, with the survey smoother, which the author attributes to the risk premium in the market series. The Focus survey (BCB, since 1999, more than 100 institutions, daily, with a Top5 ranking contest) and the FGV consumer survey (monthly since September/2005, more than 2,000 consumers, by income and education) are the two survey sources.

**What the break-even decomposes into (Section 3.2, Vicente and Graminho).** Break-even inflation = expected inflation + inflation risk premium − liquidity premium + convexity; the last two are below 1 basis point at 12 months and can be ignored.

## Main results / findings

The findings are grouped in nine topics (Section 3). The ones that bear on the argument:

- **Drivers.** The target anchors expectations and past inflation matters little (Cerisola and Gelos); the backward-looking component cedes ground to the target (Bevilaqua, Mesquita and Minella); 12-month-ahead expectations rise with the target, current inflation and the exchange rate and fall with the Selic, while 3-month-ahead ones depend on current inflation and past exchange-rate volatility (Araujo and Gaglianone); sticky information fits median expectations better than rational or adaptive expectations (Guillén). Consumers adjust only to current inflation (Campelo Jr et al.).
- **Disagreement.** It is persistent, explained by the country risk premium and the change in inflation (Carvalho and Minella), falls with output growth, and rises with inflation for consumers and with the change in inflation for professionals (Gaglianone); lower credibility goes with more disagreement (Sicsú).
- **Information and updating.** Forecasters weight public information more because it is more precise, yet overweight private information to stand apart (Areosa). New information is absorbed immediately but with a modest coefficient, and more weight goes to more precise news (Correa and Picchetti). Not everyone updates: the probability of revising the inflation forecast is 12% a day and 48% a month (Gaglianone et al., Focus, Jan/2004–Jan/2015); Top5 institutions update every 7 days on average against 12 for the others in the 6 months before the ranking (Marques). A sticky-plus-noisy information model fits the data only with more rigidity than observed (Cordeiro et al.).
- **Accuracy.** Survey forecasts beat VAR forecasts and common errors dominate idiosyncratic ones (Carvalho and Minella); bias-adjusted forecasts beat the median and an AR(1) (Kohlscheen); combining consumers and professionals with bias correction beats the consensus (Gaglianone, Issler and Matos). One study finds break-even-based forecasts beat Focus at 12 months (Val, Barbedo and Maia), another finds them competitive in the short run (Araujo and Vicente).
- **Bias and rationality: mixed, by horizon and sample.** Unbiasedness and weak rationality are not rejected at one month (Kohlscheen) or at 1 and 3 months, but rejected at 6 and 12 (Araujo and Gaglianone, May/2002–Dec/2008), and not rejected at any horizon in 2004–2008. Many studies find a negative bias: professionals underpredict. Consumers overpredict, more so with lower income or education: by 2.01 p.p. against an underprediction of 0.68 p.p. by market agents (Gaglianone, Issler and Matos, Jan/2006–May/2015), with 22% to 40% of consumers passing rationality tests individually. Forecast errors are serially correlated, and rising inflation is followed by underprediction, favouring models that give past inflation more weight than rational expectations do (Kohlscheen).
- **Credibility.** The target coordinated expectations and the BCB reacted strongly to them (Minella et al.); agents perceive a Taylor rule consistent with targeting (Carvalho and Minella). A break-even-based credibility measure falls in mid-2008 and at the end of 2015 and recovers from mid-2016, while the survey-based one is more regular (Val et al.).

## Limitations and caveats

- By the author's account: the 23 papers are chosen ad hoc and the list is not exhaustive; results on rationality depend on horizon and sample; consumer expectations refer to each consumer's own basket, not to one CPI (footnote 4).
- What the text shows: the survey reports each study's conclusion without assessing it, so findings with different samples, methods and horizons sit side by side (e.g. break-even forecasts better than Focus in one study, only competitive in another); the samples mostly end by the mid-2010s. Several numbers appear twice with small inconsistencies: the market underprediction is "-0.35 p.p." in Section 3.8 and "0.35 p.p." in Table A2; the standard deviation of the updating probability is "7%" in Section 3.6 and "0.07" in Table A2. Bevilaqua, Mesquita and Minella is cited as 2008 (the published version), the working paper as 2007 in this vault.
- Damaged passages: Figures 1–3 survive only as axis values, reversed month labels ("60/naJ") and legends. Table A1 (papers by topic) has its column headers broken across lines, so which "x" belongs to which topic cannot be read. In Table A2 the paper names are interleaved with the lines of their findings. The page numbering jumps from 18 to 22 and the reference list is not in the file; stray "4" and "64" lines follow the title.
