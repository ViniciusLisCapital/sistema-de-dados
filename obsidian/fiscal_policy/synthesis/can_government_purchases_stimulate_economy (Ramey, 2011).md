## Context and motivation

Before 2008 the short-run stimulus effect of fiscal policy was "a backwater" next to monetary policy research, partly because fiscal implementation lags were thought too long to fight recessions. With the Fed at the zero lower bound and a stimulus package on the table, the question "what is the government spending multiplier?" became urgent. Ramey reviews what theory predicts, the U.S. aggregate time-series evidence and the new cross-state evidence, to narrow the range for the experiment relevant to the stimulus debate: a temporary, deficit-financed increase in government purchases.

## Core argument / thesis

"The multiplier" is "a nebulous concept": it depends on the type of spending, its persistence, how it is financed and how monetary policy reacts. For the specific experiment of a temporary, deficit-financed rise in purchases that enter utility separately and do not raise private productivity, the U.S. aggregate multiplier "is probably between 0.8 and 1.5", near the upper end if undertaken in a severe recession — but "reasonable people could argue that the multiplier is 0.5 or 2.0 without being contradicted by the data". There is still no consensus on the mechanism by which spending raises GDP.

## Key mechanisms / model

Every theory hinges on how spending moves **equilibrium hours**: with capital fixed in the short run, output rises only if hours rise, so the multiplier is tied to the labor response and to diminishing returns to labor.

- **Neoclassical.** Higher spending is a negative wealth effect: households work more and consume less (leisure a normal good). A persistent increase raises desired capital more than a transitory one, so it raises hours more now. With lump-sum taxes Ricardian equivalence holds and financing does not matter; with distortionary taxes it does. Baxter and King (1993): the lowest multipliers come from temporary spending financed by concurrent distortionary taxes (as low as -2.5); temporary deficit-financed spending gives multipliers still well below unity; permanent spending gives larger ones. When spending is followed by a hump-shaped rise in distortionary taxes (Burnside, Eichenbaum and Fisher 2004), intertemporal substitution moves labor to the present and raises the short-run multiplier. The carrying assumptions: persistence of the spending and the timing of distortionary taxes.
- **Keynesian.** With interest rates fixed, the multiplier is 1/(1 – mpc); imports or rising rates lower it, accelerator effects raise it. The New Keynesian model, a sticky-price edifice on a neoclassical base, gives multipliers equal to or below unity (Cogan et al. 2010 with Smets–Wouters). Galí, López-Salido and Vallés (2007) reach 2.0 only by assuming at least half of consumers are rule-of-thumb and employment is demand-determined — which "essentially convert the New Keynesian model back into a traditional Keynesian model".
- **Zero lower bound.** With the nominal rate stuck, a deficit-financed rise in spending raises expected inflation and lowers the real rate; Christiano, Eichenbaum and Rebelo (2011) get a peak multiplier of 2.3 with rates held for twelve quarters. This is the one route to large multipliers in the NK model without widespread non-optimizing behavior.
- **Other considerations.** Productive public capital raises long-run but not short-run multipliers; transfers, treated as negative lump-sum taxes, should have little effect under permanent income and Ricardian equivalence (most of the 2009–2010 U.S. package was transfers); models assume full employment at the start, so slack is untested theoretically.

**Identification** is the crux of the evidence. Military buildups are used because they are least likely to respond to the economy, but wars bring patriotism (raising labor supply) or rationing and capacity limits (dampening it). Blanchard–Perotti SVARs are easy to implement, but most spending and tax changes are **anticipated**, which invalidates VAR inferences that ignore anticipation (Ramey and Shapiro 1998, Ramey 2011, Leeper, Walker and Yang 2011), and small changes in assumed elasticities move estimates a lot (Caldara 2011). Controlling for taxes is hard because tax multiplier estimates range from -0.5 to -5.0.

**Cross-state multipliers are a different object.** They hold national effects constant (time fixed effects). In a simple Keynesian model, a $1 transfer to Mississippi financed by lump-sum taxes on all states has an aggregate multiplier of 0, yet a panel regression would estimate mpc/(1 – mpc) — 1.5 with an mpc of 0.6. Translating local to aggregate multipliers depends on the type of spending and the model (states as small open economies in a currency union).

## Main results / findings

- **Aggregate U.S. studies (Table 1)** span samples, experiments and identification methods yet mostly give multipliers of about 0.6 to 1.8; the range within studies is almost as wide as across them, and standard errors are always large. Low estimates come from tax-financed episodes (Korean War–dominated samples; Barro and Redlick 2011 controlling for marginal tax rates get 0.6), but Ramey finds that holding marginal tax rates constant in her own framework leaves a multiplier of approximately unity. Fisher and Peters (2010) get 1.5, but their shocks are very persistent, which by the neoclassical logic overstates the multiplier for a temporary stimulus.
- **State dependence is contested.** Time-varying-parameter studies find little difference between expansions and contractions. Auerbach and Gorodnichenko's regime-switching model without regime change gives implausible dynamics; with endogenous switching it gives multipliers between 0 and 0.5 in expansions and between 1.0 and 1.5 in recessions, which Ramey finds more plausible. Gordon and Krenn's large 1940–41 multiplier may partly be firms gearing up for anticipated future spending; including Ramey's news variable removes the interaction with unemployment in Barro and Redlick.
- **No ZLB amplification in history.** From 1939 to the second quarter of 1947 the T-bill rate never exceeded 0.38 percent; restricting the sample to 1939–1949 gives a multiplier of 0.7 (with very large standard errors) — no evidence of larger multipliers at the bound.
- **Cross-state studies (Table 2)** mostly find income multipliers of 0.5 to 2.0, many around 1.5 to 1.8 with an implied cost of around $35,000 per job, and several find higher multipliers with more slack. Exceptions: earmarks reduce corporate employment, investment and R&D (Cohen, Coval and Malloy 2010); New Deal farm payments to idle land had a negative multiplier.
- **Mechanism unresolved:** some studies find consumption falls (neoclassical wealth effect), others that it rises (rule-of-thumb consumers); industry markups do not move as the NK model requires (Nekarda and Ramey 2011).

## Limitations and caveats

- The 0.8–1.5 range is a judgment: Ramey truncates the low estimates (usually accompanied by distortionary tax increases) and the very high ones (identification concerns); it applies to purchases, not to transfers, which dominated the 2009 package.
- The evidence is U.S.; multi-country studies are said to find similar ranges but are not reviewed.
- The estimates say nothing about welfare; that would need the mechanism to be understood.
- Many studies were very recent at writing; the author asks for time to check robustness.
- **Damaged passages:** the Bellman equation and the decomposition of dn/dg in section 2.1 are split across lines and partly reduced to symbols; footnote 4 is broken by a page break; the text ends mid-sentence in the Conclusions ("whether government") and the reference list is not in the file.
