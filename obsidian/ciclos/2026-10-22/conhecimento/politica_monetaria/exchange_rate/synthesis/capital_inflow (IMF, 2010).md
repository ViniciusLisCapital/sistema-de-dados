## Context and motivation

As the global economy emerged from the 2008–09 crisis, capital flowed back to emerging market economies (EMEs), much of it perceived as temporary, driven by interest differentials that would partly reverse once advanced-economy rates normalized. Brazil and Taiwan Province of China had just reimposed controls. The crisis had also weakened the view that unfettered flows are benign and always the product of rational decisions: herd behavior and excessive optimism by foreign investors, and collateral damage (bubbles, booms and busts) even from sound flows, were taken more seriously. The note asks under what conditions controls on inflows are a legitimate part of the toolkit, alongside fiscal, monetary and exchange rate policy, intervention and prudential regulation — and whether they have worked.

## Core argument / thesis

Controls on inflows are justified, as a complement and never a substitute for macroeconomic and prudential policy, when four conditions hold together: the economy is near potential, reserves are adequate, the exchange rate is not undervalued, and the flows are likely transitory. There are two reasons to use them: to limit appreciation (macroeconomic) and to limit crisis vulnerability from excessive or risky foreign borrowing (prudential). Controls can keep their bite even when investors find ways around them, as long as circumvention costs more than the expected return — "sand in the wheels". Against this stands a multilateral cost: controls by countries with undervalued currencies resist needed appreciation, deflect flows to others and can spread as financial protectionism.

## Key mechanisms / model

The note is a decision sequence (Figure 1 in the original) rather than a formal model:

- **Should the currency appreciate?** If it is undervalued from a multilateral perspective, the answer is to let it appreciate passively. Only if it is already overvalued or near equilibrium is a more active response needed.
- **Reserve accumulation, then sterilization.** If more reserves are desirable, inflows are an opportunity to buy them; inflation concerns are met by sterilizing. Sterilization has limits: shallow markets may not absorb the bonds, there is a fiscal cost from the gap between domestic interest paid and reserve yields, and — the self-defeating part — it keeps domestic rates high and so perpetuates the inflows, especially if markets expect the reserve policy to be abandoned and the currency to appreciate. Raising reserve requirements avoids the fiscal cost but risks disintermediation.
- **Monetary and fiscal policy.** Lower rates reduce the incentive for inflows and fiscal tightening reduces appreciation pressure, but cutting rates is unattractive when the economy is overheating, and fiscal consolidation is slow and political.
- **Controls, last.** When these are exhausted or inappropriate, controls on inflows enter — most clearly for transitory surges, because the appreciation is temporary while the damage to the tradable sector (hysteresis) may be permanent. If the surge is persistent the economy should instead adjust to the higher real exchange rate, since controls lose effectiveness over time and must be continually tightened, raising distortions. Footnote rule of thumb: flows that push the real exchange rate toward equilibrium are more likely to be persistent than flows that contribute to overshooting.
- **Prudential side: a pecking order of liabilities.** From riskiest to safest: foreign-currency debt, CPI-indexed local-currency debt, local-currency debt, portfolio equity, FDI — and within each, short-term riskier than long-term. Debt concentrates risk on the borrower; equity and FDI share it. Inflows fund credit booms, including FX lending to unhedged borrowers. In normal times prudential regulation of banks may suffice; with large inflows, controls targeted at the riskiest flows can buttress it. Instruments: unremunerated reserve requirements on FX debt, taxes on short-term debt (which lengthen maturities; the optimal tax depends on the risk of liquidity panics, the size and social cost of the associated fiscal adjustment, and the substitutability across maturities), financial transaction taxes (which bite hardest on short-term carry trades) and minimum-stay requirements.
- **Conditions that decide effectiveness.** An existing administrative apparatus (easier in already-closed accounts, which need controls less); for open capital accounts, the need to build new controls and tighten them against circumvention. Relaxing outflow controls has an ambiguous effect on net inflows: some inflows are offset, but easier repatriation can attract more.
- **Multilateral channel.** Widespread controls reduce gains from intertemporal trade "much as tariffs limit the gains from goods (within-period) trade", let undervalued currencies avoid appreciation, redirect flows to countries less able to absorb them and invite contagion (beggar-thy-neighbor). The offsetting effect: by curbing hot money, controls may lower precautionary demand for reserves and so reduce global imbalances.

## Main results / findings

- **Survey of past episodes (Table 1, Box 1): composition yes, volume and exchange rate mostly no.** Individual country studies usually find little effect on the aggregate volume of inflows and therefore on appreciation; the evidence is stronger for a shift in composition toward longer maturities and less vulnerable liabilities, and for some monetary autonomy. Over their 1990s controls the real effective exchange rate still appreciated by about 5 and 4 percent annually in Brazil and Chile (Box 1). In Chile the FDI share of liabilities rose from about 34 percent in 1991 to 53 percent in 1998; in Colombia the medium- and long-term share of private external debt went from 40 percent in 1993 to 70 percent in 1996. For Brazil 1993–97 the studies in Table 1 disagree: some find short-term effects on volume and composition, Ariyoshi and others find none. Effects are often short lived as markets adjust; strong enforcement capacity, as in Chile, is what keeps controls working.
- **Why the volume evidence is weak:** recent controls were marginal; they came in packages with other policies; their intensity is hard to measure; and reverse causality — countries facing the largest inflows impose controls — can produce no or even a positive association.
- **Natural experiment of the global financial crisis (Appendix 1).** In a sample of up to 37 EMEs, the growth decline in 2008–09 relative to 2003–07 was larger where end-2007 stocks of debt liabilities and of financial-sector FDI were larger, and smaller where nonfinancial FDI was larger (Table A1). Debt and financial FDI are strongly associated with credit booms and FX-denominated bank lending (Table A2), which is one channel; but debt still predicts worse outcomes after controlling for those, because firms and households can borrow abroad directly or through nonbanks — so bank regulation alone does not cover the risk. The surprise is financial FDI: part of it is parent-bank lending to subsidiaries, debt in all but name.
- **Controls and crisis outcomes.** Countries with inflow controls before the crisis were less likely to suffer a "crisis" (a growth decline in the sample's lowest decile) in a probit (Table A3); the association is driven by controls on debt (bond) inflows, while controls on equity inflows enter with the opposite sign and FDI controls are insignificant. Controls are not associated with lower average pre-crisis growth.

## Limitations and caveats

- **Suggestive correlations, not causation**, in the authors' own words; the crisis sample is a cross-section of at most 37 countries (out of 50), with the capital-controls index (Schindler) ending in 2005. The "jury is still out" on whether controls work.
- **Context-specific.** The evidence that controls work comes mostly from countries with extensive restrictions; the policy question is reimposition by countries with largely open capital accounts, where the evidence is thinner. Telling a transitory surge from a persistent one in real time is acknowledged to be hard, yet the recommendation turns on it.
- **Composition versus volume.** The authors note that finding an effect on composition but not on volume is inconsistent unless substitution across flow types is perfect.
- **Damaged passages.** Figure 1 (the decision flowchart "Coping with Surges in Capital Inflows") is absent: the page is blank in the file. Box 1's prose is interleaved with the axis labels and legends of its two charts, so sentences are broken mid-line. Figures A1 and A2 survive only as titles and sources. In Table A1 empty cells are not marked, so coefficients cannot be assigned to columns with certainty, and its note lists the significance levels in reverse order of Table A3's. The reference list is not in the file.
