# Inflation Targeting

**Type:** Monetary policy regime / strategy
**Tags:** #inflation-targeting #nominal-anchor #monetary-policy-regime

---

## Definition

A monetary policy strategy in which the central bank aims to bring inflation to a stated target over time, using the short-term interest rate. Clarida, Galí & Gertler (1999) give it a model-based meaning: optimal policy under discretion "embeds inflation targeting" in that it aims for convergence of inflation to its target over time — gradually, not immediately. Their definition differs from Svensson's, who classifies inflation targeting by the weights in the loss function (no weight on output = strict, positive weight = flexible).

## Mechanism

In the New Keynesian model with a cost-push shock, inflation above target calls for contracting the output gap by raising the real rate, so the nominal rate must move more than one-for-one with expected inflation (Clarida, Galí & Gertler, Results 2–3). Immediate ("extreme") targeting is optimal only if there is no cost-push inflation or no weight on output; otherwise convergence is gradual. With transmission lags, policy is set on the inflation forecast (Svensson's forecast targeting, as Clarida, Galí & Gertler present it). The case for formal targets: a clear nominal anchor directly in terms of inflation (unlike money growth, which money demand shocks can unhinge) and a higher effective weight on inflation, which the commitment analysis says is optimal.

## When it applies / when it fails

- Gradualism vs. extreme targeting depends on the presence of cost-push shocks; inflation inertia makes the tradeoff less favorable and requires responding to inflation forecasts far ahead (Clarida, Galí & Gertler, section 6).
- Under fiscal dominance it can be perverse: if a higher real rate raises the probability of default on the debt, it depreciates the currency and raises inflation, and fiscal policy is the right instrument (Blanchard 2004, Brazil 2002).
- Price-level targeting, its stricter variant, is rejected by Clarida, Galí & Gertler because "bygones are bygones" under inflation targeting but not under a price-level target.

## BRL / application to the current analysis

Blanchard (2004) argues that in Brazil in 2002 a rate rise in response to inflation would probably have depreciated the Real and raised inflation, and that the BCB's choice not to raise the real rate until early 2003 was right. Clarida, Galí & Gertler say nothing about Brazil.
