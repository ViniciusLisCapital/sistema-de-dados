# Currency Crisis Models (Speculative Attacks)

**Type:** Theoretical framework — balance-of-payments crises
**Tags:** #currency-crisis #speculative-attack #self-fulfilling #multiple-equilibria #fixed-exchange-rate

---

## Definition

Models of how a fixed exchange rate collapses under a speculative attack. Two families:

- **First generation (Krugman 1979; Flood and Garber):** policy inconsistent with the peg — domestic credit growing at an exogenous rate — runs reserves down; foresighted speculators attack and take the remaining reserves at a precise moment before they would have run out. The crisis is the inevitable end of unsustainable policy.
- **Second generation (self-fulfilling crises):** the government chooses whether to keep the peg by weighing the cost of defending it against the cost of abandoning it, and the cost of defense depends on market expectations. More than one equilibrium can exist, so a peg that is viable under some expectations collapses because markets expect it to.

## Mechanism

**First generation.** The key object is the *shadow exchange rate*: the floating rate that would clear the market if all remaining reserves passed to private hands at that moment. While it is below the peg, attacking would bring speculators a capital loss; once it would rise above, they would forgo an instantaneous gain. So the attack comes exactly when the shadow rate reaches the peg. Reserves then drop to zero in one jump, because the move to a float raises expected depreciation and the interest rate, cutting money demand, and the money supply must fall at once for the exchange rate not to jump. The more interest-sensitive money demand is, the earlier the attack. In stochastic versions, interest rates rise as reserves fall and the collapse brings a discrete depreciation (Obstfeld's account).

**Second generation.** Expectations of devaluation raise the cost of holding the peg through endogenous variables:
- *interest rates* — higher rates on short-term or floating-rate public debt worsen the public finances (and banks', firms' and mortgage holders' positions), raising the government's incentive to inflate the debt away;
- *wages* — expected depreciation is built into wage settlements, eroding competitiveness and raising unemployment, raising the incentive to devalue.
With a fixed cost of realignment, the government devalues only when the gain exceeds that cost; but where that threshold lies depends on expectations, and expectations depend on where markets think the threshold is. The circularity produces multiple equilibria, and the timing of a switch between them is essentially arbitrary — it can be triggered by seemingly minor events.

## When it applies / when it fails

- **First generation fits** when reserves are the binding constraint and the policy driving them is exogenous; Obstfeld argues it fits poorly for industrial countries with access to world capital markets, which can borrow reserves, and that it ignores the government's policy options and their costs.
- **Second generation needs** a government that cannot precommit, a cost of defense that rises with expected devaluation, and a realignment cost of intermediate size. Multiplicity disappears when the government has no domestic-currency debt to roll over in the crisis period, or borrows enough foreign currency to cover it; small foreign borrowing can make the bad equilibrium worse. Institutions that tie the authorities' hands remove it altogether.
- **Reserves are a symptom, not the trigger,** in second-generation crises: they fall when the decision to abandon the peg is near, not steadily before it.
- **Neither family says when an attack happens**: which equilibrium the market coordinates on, and why on one day rather than another, is unexplained.
