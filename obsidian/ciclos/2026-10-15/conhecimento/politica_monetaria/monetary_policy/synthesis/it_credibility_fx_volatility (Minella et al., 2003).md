## Context and motivation

A shorter, updated version of the authors' 2002 working paper, finished in May 2003, which re-runs its tests through the end of 2002. That extension is the point: in 2002 a confidence crisis about the incoming government's policies and higher international risk aversion lowered the rollover of domestic public debt and produced a "sudden stop" of capital inflows; the country risk premium rose from 750 basis points in April to a peak of 2,400 at the end of September, the Real depreciated sharply and inflation reached 12.5%, more than 5 points above the band's upper limit. The paper asks whether Brazil's inflation targeting regime survived this "stress test", concentrating on two challenges common to emerging markets: constructing credibility, and high exchange rate volatility.

## Core argument / thesis

Inflation targeting in an emerging market must build credibility while lowering inflation and absorbing larger shocks. Under large supply shocks even a strong policy response cannot prevent inflation from breaching the target, so what matters is that the central bank acts consistently with the framework, communicates why the target was missed, and that expectations converge back to the target over some horizon. The authors argue the regime passed: the BCB responded more than one-for-one to expected inflation, the targets kept coordinating expectations until the last quarter of 2002, and inflation persistence had fallen. The late-2002 break in expectations reflected uncertainty about whether the policy would be maintained by the next government, not a lack of credibility of the policy being run. Offsetting the shocks fully and quickly would have cost far more output.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Forward-looking reaction with smoothing (equation (1))**: the Selic responds to the expected deviation of inflation from target (current and next year, weighted by the months left), to the output gap and, new in this version, to the 12-month exchange rate change, with a lagged Selic. Expectations come from the BCB's constant-rate Inflation Report forecasts or the market survey.
- **Why the output gap can come out negative**: supply shocks such as electricity rationing raise inflation and lower output at once, so a rate increase coincides with falling output; if expected inflation does not fully capture the shock, the gap coefficient turns negative. External shocks act the same way, which is why the gap loses significance once the exchange rate is included.
- **Accommodation and the "adjusted target"**: policy accommodates the first-round effects of supply and cost-push shocks and neutralizes second-round effects. The BCB computes the primary impact of current shocks and the secondary impact of past ones (inertia); since the primary effect is accommodated, and part of the inertia because output volatility also counts, the optimal path can leave 12-month inflation above the old target. The BCB then announces an adjusted target (published in the January 2003 open letter for 2003 and 2004). The target change costs some credibility, but transparency gains more — keeping an unattainable target would cost credibility too.
- **Two conditions for anchored expectations**: policy consistent with the Copom's stated guidelines, and clear communication (minutes, Inflation Report, open letters explaining the 2001 and 2002 breaches).
- **Why persistence matters**: if inflation targeting lowers the backward-looking component of price setting, persistence falls and so does the output cost of curbing inflation; lower persistence also mechanically lowers the measured 12-month pass-through and the cumulative effect of unemployment on inflation.
- **Exchange rate volatility in an emerging market**: countries that cannot borrow in their own currency face balance-sheet mismatches and self-fulfilling crises; reserves insure against the bad equilibrium. The BCB runs a dirty float with interventions made as transparent as possible, so intervention is not read as putting the exchange rate ahead of price stability. Inflationary pressure depends more on the size of the depreciation than on the pass-through coefficient.
- **Administered prices** (oil by-products, fixed telephone, residential electricity, public transportation) depend on international prices, have higher exchange-rate pass-through and stronger backward-looking indexation; their ratio to market prices rose substantially under inflation targeting.

## Main results / findings

The evidence supports the credibility argument up to September 2002 and shows its strain afterwards.
- **Reaction function (Tables 3–4; BCB forecasts 1999:07-2002:12, market forecasts 2000:01-2002:12)**: smoothing is high (lagged Selic between 0.7 and 0.9). The response to expected inflation exceeds one and is significant in every specification; with market expectations it is statistically greater than one (around 2.0-2.3), with BCB forecasts less stable (from 2.7 to 5.7) and significantly above one or close to it. The output gap is wrong-signed (significant once); the exchange rate change is marginally significant with BCB forecasts and not with market ones.
- **Expectations and the target (Table 5, 2000:1-2003:2)**: 12-month-ahead market expectations stayed below the band's upper limit until the last quarter of 2002, even after actual inflation passed it in mid-2001, and stayed below actual inflation, i.e. agents expected the rise to reverse. Expectations respond to the target with a coefficient around 1 even controlling for past inflation. What does not fit the clean story: past inflation is significant in the full sample but not with a sample ending in 2002:09, and its recursive coefficient starts rising at the end of 2001 and accelerates in late 2002; the EMBI becomes significant only with the crisis months; models estimated to September 2002 forecast expectations for the next five months well below the actual path. The median 2002 expectation was around 4.5% through September and reached 11% by end-December. Expectations reverted from early 2003.
- **Persistence (Table 6, monthly 1995:08-2002:12)**: the autoregressive coefficient falls from 0.56 to 0.10 under inflation targeting (specification I), but the result is fragile: a recursive estimate shows no fall, and a time-varying-coefficient estimate falls over time except for a rapid rise in the last months of 2002, which required a dummy for 2002Q4.
- **Pass-through**: the BCB structural model gives one-year pass-through of 12% for market and 25% for administered prices, about 16% for the IPCA; the dollar rose 86.7% from January 2001 to December 2002 while the IPCA rose 21.2%, placing Brazil near the low end of cross-country estimates. In the VAR (Table 7) administered-price pass-through is 1.9 times that of market prices with the sample to December 2002 (2.5 to June 2002), and pass-through rises when the crisis months are added, though within the June-sample bands. Unlike the 2002 version, the recursive supply-curve estimate and the targeting-period VAR now point to lower pass-through after the January 1999 float, consistent with Muinhos (2001).
- **Decomposition (Table 1)**: exchange rate pass-through accounts for 38% of 2001 inflation and 46% of 2002 inflation.

## Limitations and caveats

- **Authors' caveats**: credibility is "still under construction"; the targeting-period VAR is too short to be significant at two standard errors; the late-2002 period is hard to fit with a simple Phillips curve; lower output and interest-rate volatility under targeting need not mean a better trade-off; with the sample ending 2002:4 inflation volatility is higher, not lower (Table 2). The supply curve used here differs from the BCB's quarterly structural model.
- **What the text shows**: the interpretation of the late-2002 expectation jump as uncertainty about the future government rather than loss of credibility is an argument, not a test; the rising weight on past inflation in expectations and the rebound of persistence in late 2002 are the evidence most at odds with the paper's conclusion. Written by BCB staff evaluating BCB policy.
- **Inconsistency in the source**: the text gives first-month administered-price pass-through of 32.7%, while Table 7 prints 32.9%. Flagged as written.
- **Damaged passages**: equation (1) and the deviation measure in footnote 12 are reduced to vertical lists of symbols; the text describes their content. Tables 1–7 are flattened into single columns (headers after the numbers), so only figures the text itself states are cited here; the Table 6 note is cut off ("to avoid the inclusion of data b"). Figures 1–12 survive only as axis values and series labels.
