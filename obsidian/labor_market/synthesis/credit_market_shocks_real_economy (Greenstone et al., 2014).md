## Context and motivation

The 2008 crisis brought a sharp fall in US employment and a slow recovery, with competing explanations: weak aggregate demand (Mian and Sufi), uncertainty, structural factors. A fourth was the collapse of bank lending to small businesses. Small firms lost a disproportionate share of jobs, and policymakers (Bernanke 2010, Krueger 2010) argued that fractured credit markets were a major cause of the employment decline, so restoring credit was central to the policy response. Behind it sits the old view that banks are "special" because small and medium firms have no ready substitute for bank credit (Brunner and Meltzer, Bernanke 1983, Bernanke and Gertler 1995). The paper asks how much of the 2007–2009 employment loss the cut in small business credit supply explains, and whether local lending supply shocks matter for real activity in the calmer 1997–2007 period too.

## Core argument / thesis

Credit supply shocks to small businesses had real effects in the Great Recession, but small ones, and essentially none in normal times. The chain is: some national banks cut small business lending far more than others for reasons on their own balance sheets; counties where those banks held larger pre-crisis market shares saw local small business lending fall, because borrowers cannot switch lenders quickly; that depressed small establishment employment. The first link is strong; the second is statistically significant but economically modest. Even under the extreme assumption that the whole national fall in small business lending was supply-driven, it explains only a small fraction of the fall in small firm and total employment. The credit channel, in this evidence, contributed to the recession but was not a primary driver, and it does not show up in employment outside the crisis.

## Key mechanisms / model

- **Imperfect substitutability of bank credit.** The identifying premise, which the authors treat as testable: a firm whose bank cuts lending can only partially replace it elsewhere, because lending is local and relationship-based (soft information, short distances between firm and lender). If firms could switch freely, a bank-specific cut would not move county lending at all.
- **Purged shift-share (modified Bartik) instrument.** A standard shift-share would interact each bank's pre-period county market share with its national change in lending. That fails here because banks lend less where local demand collapsed, so a bank's national change mixes its own supply decision with the demand shocks of the places it operates. The fix is a regression of each bank's lending change in each county on county fixed effects (local credit demand) and bank fixed effects (the bank's supply shift, purged of its exposure to local demand) — Equation (3). The county shock is the market-share-weighted average of those bank effects (Equation (4)), standardized, and computed for every pair of consecutive years from 1997.
- **What the identification needs.** Only that banks with above- or below-average supply shifts are not systematically located in counties with worse-than-average outcome shocks — it would fail if, say, managerial skill in choosing branch locations correlated with skill in choosing the bank's portfolio. The appendix states it as a covariance condition on the supply component of the instrument, and shows why the demand component of a plain Bartik instrument is a threat (banks concentrated in hard-hit markets such as Florida, Nevada and Arizona lend less simply because demand fell there).
- **Within-state comparisons.** Market shares vary enough across counties of the same state that all comparisons are made with state-by-year fixed effects, plus 2006 county characteristics (and debt-to-income ratios) interacted with year. The essence: within a state, did counties with more Citigroup than US Bancorp branches before the crisis do worse?
- **Outcomes and timing.** Small business loan originations (CRA data, loans to firms with under $1 million in revenue) as the first stage; LBD employment and establishment growth of small (under 20 employee) establishments, with a symmetric growth measure based on base-period size class; county-wide employment from CBP and QCEW. Equation (6) is a difference-in-differences: the 2008 and 2009 shocks interacted with year dummies, summarized as the cumulative effect of a one standard deviation shock. Shocks are signed so that a positive value means more predicted lending.
- **Normal times and the bounding exercise.** Equation (7) pools all years, letting the effect shift in 2008 and 2009; Equation (8) estimates a year-by-year effect with a lead as a placebo. The aggregate contribution comes from 2SLS elasticities of employment to instrumented originations, applied to the national fall in lending under the assumption that all of it was supply.
- **Specification checks.** Small establishments of multi-unit (and, in NETS, multi-state) firms should be insensitive to local bank credit; finding effects there would signal that the shock picks up local business-cycle differences. Small banks below the CRA reporting threshold could offset the cuts; the authors test for it.

## Main results / findings

- **First stage: switching lenders is costly.** Predicted shocks strongly predict realized small business originations, with pre-2007 trends largely removed by the regression adjustment. A one standard deviation fall in the 2009 predicted shock goes with a 17% fall in county small business originations over 2009–2010; the 2008 shock gives a large and persistent decline in each of 2008, 2009 and 2010 (Table 4). Banks below the CRA threshold do not expand to offset the larger banks' cuts (Appendix Table 2).
- **Small establishment employment: a small effect, only from the 2009 shock.** A one standard deviation lower 2009 shock lowers 2009–2010 small establishment employment growth by 0.6 percentage points with county covariates; the 2008 shock's effect is close to zero (Table 5). Adding the debt-to-income control does not change the estimate, so the shock is not picking up household deleveraging. Effects on small establishment births and deaths are imprecise in the LBD; NETS gives smaller, more precise, significant effects (Appendix Table 3), so the evidence on business formation is suggestive only.
- **Placebo groups behave.** Small establishments of multi-unit firms show insignificant employment effects (Table 6); the NETS multi-state estimates range from insignificant to borderline negative, which the authors attribute to chains possibly gaining share from credit-constrained standalones.
- **County employment: smaller still.** The cumulative effect of a one standard deviation 2009 shock on county employment growth is a significant 0.34 percentage points, smaller than for small firms as expected, with the interactions only marginally significant jointly (Table 7).
- **Normal times: lending moves, employment does not.** Over 1997–2007 predicted shocks still predict lending in every year, but their association with small establishment and county employment is small and insignificant; the excess effect of the 2009 shock is significant (Table 8, Figure 4). The first-stage relation is countercyclical, its point estimate almost 5 times larger in 2009 than in 2004 (Figure 3), which the authors read as mechanical: it is easier to predict where lending falls than where it grows. The lead term is insignificant, supporting the design.
- **Aggregate contribution: an upper bound of about a twentieth.** With 2SLS elasticities of small establishment employment of 0.016 to current and -0.007 to lagged originations (Table 9), and treating the whole 22% (2008) and 33% (2009) fall in CRA small business lending as supply, the cut lowered small business employment by 0.5 percentage points by end-2010 — 5 percent of the 10 percent decline for firms under 20 employees; at the upper end of the 95% confidence interval, 13 percent. For total employment the bound is 0.3 percentage points, up to 5 percent of the 6 percent decline, and no more than 15% at the upper confidence bound.

## Limitations and caveats

- **The bound combines two extreme assumptions** (all lending decline is supply; upper confidence bound), so the authors say the true contribution is very likely much lower; translating cross-county estimates into an aggregate effect also ignores general equilibrium effects they call non-trivial.
- **Counterfactual policy.** They cannot rule out that employment effects would have been larger without TARP, cheap Federal Reserve lending to banks and the stress tests; the paper is silent on the moral hazard costs of those interventions, so it is no welfare verdict.
- **Bank credit is not all credit.** The null in normal times may mean firms substitute to non-bank credit (home equity loans, also restricted in 2007–2009, may explain the larger crisis effect); and since credit was booming before the crisis, the results may reflect an asymmetry between negative and positive credit supply shocks. The policy reading that programmes to raise bank credit supply are unnecessary in normal times rests on that interpretation.
- **Coverage and measurement.** Only CRA-reporting banks (above an asset threshold; 654 holding companies) enter the shock; loan originations proxy the unobserved change in outstanding small business loans; the LBD cannot tell whether a multi-unit firm is concentrated in one county; population changes cannot be measured, so out-migration versus employment rates is unresolved; the LBD confidence interval for the bound uses the covariance from the CBP/QCEW estimates, because the restricted data did not allow computing it directly.
- **External validity.** US counties, small firms, 1997–2010; the mechanism rests on local, relationship-based small business lending, and the result on the size of the credit channel is specific to that structure.
- **Damaged passages.** Equation (3), the bank-by-county regression of lending changes on bank and county fixed effects, is missing from the text (only the description around it survives), as are the two appendix equations after (a3) (the instrument decomposed into demand and supply components, and the appendix version of the purged regression). Equations (1), (2) and (4)–(8) and the appendix equations are OCR-damaged (subscripts and Greek letters reduced to stray characters). Figures 1–4 and the appendix figures are OCR noise (axis labels and map text only). Table 2's rows and columns are scrambled; Table 6's columns are misaligned with broken parentheses; Table 7's columns (5)–(6) are printed apart from the rest of the table; Appendix Table 3 has broken parentheses.
